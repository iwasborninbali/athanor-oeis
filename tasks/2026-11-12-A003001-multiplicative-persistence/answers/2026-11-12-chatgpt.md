# OEIS A003001 — Status and Proof Attempt

**Report date:** November 12, 2026 **Subject:** Finiteness of digit-product equivalence-class representatives at fixed multiplicative persistence.

## 1. Status and literature

**Verdict: OPEN in its full form, but a substantial part follows from an existing theorem on $S$-unit equations.**

The conjecture in OEIS A003001, attributed to A. H. M. Smeets (November 16, 2018), states:

> For any multiplicative persistence, except multiplicative persistence 2, the set of class representative numbers with that multiplicative persistence is conjectured to be finite.

The live OEIS entry still identifies this as a conjecture. I found no verified proof or counterexample resolving the full assertion.

### Precise formulation

Let $f(n)$ denote the product of the decimal digits of $n$, with $f(0)=0$. Define

$$
\pi(n)=\min\{j\geq0:f^j(n)<10\},
$$

and write $\delta(n)=f^{\pi(n)}(n)$ for its terminal digit.

Define the equivalence relation

$$
n\sim m\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(n)=f(m).
$$

For a digit product $q$, let

$$
r(q)=\min\{n\geq10:f(n)=q\},
$$

whenever the set is nonempty. For $k\geq1$, define

$$
R_k=\{r(q):\pi(r(q))=k\}.
$$

The substantive conjecture is

$$
\boxed{\forall k\geq3,\quad |R_k|<\infty.}
$$

The cases $k=0,1$ are elementary, and $R_2$ is infinite.

**A definitional correction is needed.** The original description takes the minimum over all nonnegative integers, whereas Smeets's table counts the one-digit numbers $0,\ldots,9$ separately and then uses $10,\ldots,19$ as the persistence-one representatives.

Under the unrestricted definition, $9\sim19$ although $\pi(9)=0$ and $\pi(19)=1$. Restricting representatives to integers at least 10 resolves this discrepancy without changing any class of persistence at least 2.

Also, the phrase *ending in digit zero* must mean **having terminal digit zero**, not having a decimal representation ending in zero. Every multi-digit integer literally ending in zero has persistence 1.

### Literature examined

**Sloane (1973).** N. J. A. Sloane, “The persistence of a number,” *Journal of Recreational Mathematics* **6**, 97–98. This introduces the classical persistence problem. The familiar conjecture that persistence is universally at most 11 remains unresolved.

**de Faria and Tresser (2014).** Edson de Faria and Charles Tresser, “On Sloane's persistence problem,” *Experimental Mathematics* **23**(4), 363–382; arXiv:1307.1188 (2013). Their abstract says:

> We also formulate a conjecture concerning the asymptotic distribution of digits in long products of finitely many primes

They state that this distribution conjecture would solve the persistence problem. It is relevant to the present question but is not a proof of the required finiteness.

**Brier, Clavier, Gutsche and Naccache (2021).** “The Multiplicative Persistence Conjecture Is True for Odd Targets,” arXiv:2110.04263.

Their proved conclusions are:

$$
\delta(n)\in\{1,3,7,9\}\implies\pi(n)\leq1,
$$

$$
\delta(n)=5\implies\pi(n)\leq5.
$$

The authors write:

> The particular case $d=0$ can not be treated by our method.

Their work establishes finite backward genealogies for odd terminal digits, but leaves the zero-terminal problem unresolved.

**Fonga (2026).** Patrick Nyadjo Fonga, “The Multiplicative Persistence Conjecture: Resolving the $2$-Adic Obstruction for Nonzero Even Targets,” arXiv:2608.27802, submitted August 28, 2026.

This preprint proves a uniform $2$-adic valuation bound for numbers with prescribed non-one digits. It provides a finite procedure for further analysis of nonzero even terminal digits, but does not settle either the general persistence conjecture or the zero-terminal case. Its Section 5 explicitly leaves an exhaustive verification outstanding.

**Evertse, Schlickewei and Schmidt (2002).** Jan-Hendrik Evertse, Hans Peter Schlickewei and Wolfgang M. Schmidt, “Linear equations in variables which lie in a multiplicative group,” *Annals of Mathematics* **155**(3), 807–836, Theorem 1.1; Math Reviews MR1923966.

This theorem bounds the number of nondegenerate solutions of an $S$-unit equation. It is the key established result used in Section 2 below. The journal abstract states:

> We derive an explicit upper bound ... which depends only on the dimension $n$ and on the rank $r$.

The paper has Math Reviews and zbMATH indexing.

**Latest OEIS computation.** Arin Jander's contribution of October 30, 2026, states:

> a(12), if it exists, exceeds 2.67*10^375000.

This is a computational lower bound for the smallest number of persistence 12, not a proof of finiteness of class representatives.

## 2. Proof attempt and partial theorem

### 2.1 Reduction to smooth numbers

Let

$$
S=\{2^a3^b5^c7^d:a,b,c,d\geq0\}.
$$

These are precisely the positive integers that can occur as products of decimal digits. They are sometimes called $7$-smooth numbers.

**Proposition 1 (proved).** For every $k\geq2$ and terminal digit $d$,

$$
|R_k^{(d)}|
=
\left|
\{q\in S:q\geq10,\ \pi(q)=k-1,\ \delta(q)=d\}
\right|,
$$

where $R_k^{(d)}=\{r\in R_k:\delta(r)=d\}$.

**Proof.** Every positive digit product has prime factors only in $\{2,3,5,7\}$. Conversely, every $q\in S$, $q\geq10$, is the digit product of an integer formed from the appropriate numbers of digits $2,3,5,7$. Therefore its class has a unique least representative $r(q)$.

Since $r(q)\geq10$,

$$
\pi(r(q))=1+\pi(q),
\qquad
\delta(r(q))=\delta(q).
$$

Distinct products correspond to distinct classes. This establishes the bijection. $\square$

### 2.2 Finite-fiber theorem

**Lemma (proved here using an established theorem).** For every fixed positive integer $t$, the set

$$
F_t=\{x\in S:x\geq10,\ f(x)=t\}
$$

is finite.

This is the essential partial result.

**Proof.**

Take $x\in F_t$. Because $f(x)>0$, its decimal expansion contains no zero digit. Let $L\geq2$ be its number of digits.

Suppose exactly $j$ digits differ from 1. Write those digits as $d_1,\ldots,d_j\in\{2,\ldots,9\}$, occupying distinct positions $e_1,\ldots,e_j\in\{0,\ldots,L-1\}$.

Since their product is $t$,

$$
2^j\leq t,
$$

so

$$
j\leq\lfloor\log_2t\rfloor.
$$

Thus only finitely many choices of $j$ and of the exceptional-digit tuple $(d_1,\ldots,d_j)$ are possible.

For any fixed choice, expressing $x$ as a repunit with digit corrections gives

$$
x=\frac{10^L-1}{9}
+\sum_{i=1}^j(d_i-1)10^{e_i}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\boxed{
10^L+\sum_{i=1}^j9(d_i-1)10^{e_i}-9x=1.
}
\tag{1}
$$

Every variable $10^L,10^{e_1},\ldots,10^{e_j},x$ belongs to the multiplicative group of rational numbers generated by $2,3,5,7$, a group of finite rank.

Equation (1) is therefore an $S$-unit equation to which the theorem of Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt (2002, Theorem 1.1) applies, provided its solutions are nondegenerate.

To check nondegeneracy, observe that every positive term on the left of (1) is an integer at least 9. The sole negative term is $-9x$.

A vanishing proper subsum cannot consist entirely of positive terms. It must therefore include $-9x$. But then the omitted terms are positive integers, each at least 9, whose sum would have to equal 1. This is impossible.

Hence every solution of (1) is nondegenerate.

The $S$-unit theorem guarantees finitely many solutions for each fixed tuple of exceptional digits. There are finitely many such tuples, so $F_t$ is finite. $\square$

**Attribution:** The deep ingredient is the published 2002 $S$-unit theorem. The argument above is an application of that theorem, not a claim that the underlying Diophantine finiteness result is new.

### 2.3 Main partial theorem

**Theorem (proved).** For every fixed multiplicative persistence $k\geq1$, and every nonzero terminal digit $d\in\{1,\ldots,9\}$, there are only finitely many digit-product class representatives having persistence $k$ and terminal digit $d$.

In symbols,

$$
\boxed{
\forall k\geq1,\ \forall d\in\{1,\ldots,9\},
\quad |R_k^{(d)}|<\infty.
}
$$

**Proof.**

For a fixed nonzero terminal digit $d$, define

$$
S_0(d)=\{d\},
$$

and recursively

$$
S_{j+1}(d)
=
\{x\in S:x\geq10,\ f(x)\in S_j(d)\}.
$$

We prove by induction that every $S_j(d)$ is finite.

The base set $S_0(d)$ contains one element.

If $S_j(d)$ is finite, then

$$
S_{j+1}(d)
=
\bigcup_{t\in S_j(d)} F_t.
$$

Each $F_t$ is finite by the lemma, so this finite union is finite.

Consequently, $S_j(d)$ is finite for every $j\geq0$.

By Proposition 1, for $k\geq2$, representatives of persistence $k$ with terminal digit $d$ correspond bijectively to elements of $S_{k-1}(d)$. The assertion follows.

For $k=1$, the representatives are among $10,\ldots,19$, so finiteness is immediate. $\square$

**Consequence.** The original conjecture is proved for all nonzero terminal digits, including the previously difficult nonzero even digits $2,4,6,8$.

This does **not** prove a uniform upper bound on persistence, nor does it determine the exact finite sets.

### 2.4 Why terminal zero remains different

The induction above fails for terminal digit zero because

$$
\{x\in S:x\geq10,\ f(x)=0\}
$$

is infinite: it contains $10^m$ for every $m\geq1$.

Indeed, $10^m=2^m5^m$ is itself an attainable digit product. Its representative $r(10^m)$ satisfies

$$
r(10^m)\longrightarrow10^m\longrightarrow0.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{|R_2^{(0)}|=\infty.}
$$

This proves the known persistence-two exception.

For every $k\geq3$, the remaining unresolved assertion is exactly

$$
\boxed{
\left|
\{q\in S:q\geq10,\ \pi(q)=k-1,\ \delta(q)=0\}
\right|<\infty.
}
\tag{2}
$$

Unlike the nonzero-terminal case, the first backward layer from zero is infinite. Finite branching above individual nodes does not establish finiteness of a union over that infinite layer.

No argument in this report closes this gap.

### 2.5 Conditional connection to the classical persistence conjecture

There is also a useful conditional equivalence.

Let $U$ be the set of all $q\in S$, $q\geq10$, whose decimal representation contains no zero digit.

Suppose, additionally, that the classical Sloane conjecture is true:

$$
\forall n,\quad \pi(n)\leq11.
$$

Then the original class-finiteness conjecture is equivalent to the statement that **$U$ is finite**.

To prove one direction, suppose $U$ is finite. Any representative of persistence at least 3 has a digit product $q\in U$, so only finitely many such representatives can exist.

Conversely, suppose the class-finiteness conjecture holds. By the finite-fiber lemma, only finitely many members of $U$ have $f(q)\leq9$. Every remaining $q\in U$ has $\pi(q)\geq2$ and hence corresponds to a class representative of persistence at least 3. The assumed universal bound places these representatives in the finite union

$$
R_3\cup R_4\cup\cdots\cup R_{12}.
$$

This union is finite by the class-finiteness conjecture. Therefore $U$ is finite.

This equivalence is **conditional**, because Sloane's universal bound remains open. It connects the residual question to the occurrence of zero digits in sufficiently large smooth numbers, an issue closely related to the digit-distribution questions investigated by de Faria and Tresser.

## 3. Computational verification

I implemented two checks in Python using exact integer arithmetic.

The first enumerates minimal digit-product representatives using the eight regular-expression families recorded by Tim Peters in OEIS A003001 (September 19, 2023). These cover precisely the positive digit products not divisible by 10. Every representative of persistence at least 3 belongs to this collection.

The second independently enumerates all nondecreasing digit strings over $2,\ldots,9$ through length 12 and verifies their minimal representatives. It also enumerates smooth numbers below $10^9$ to test finite inverse fibers.

### Reproducible Python program

Python 3; standard library only

```python
from collections import Counter
from itertools import combinations_with_replacement

def f(n):
    p = 1
    for c in str(n):
        p *= int(c)
    return p

def persistence(n):
    k = 0
    while n >= 10:
        n = f(n)
        k += 1
    return k, n

def candidates(N):
    result = set()
    prefixes = ('', '2', '3', '4', '6', '26', '5', '35')

    for prefix in prefixes:
        a, b, c = (
            ('5', '7', '9') if prefix in ('5', '35')
            else ('7', '8', '9')
        )
        m = N - len(prefix)
        for i in range(m + 1):
            for j in range(m - i + 1):
                for h in range(m - i - j + 1):
                    s = prefix + a*i + b*j + c*h
                    if s:
                        result.add(s)
    return result

# Independent verification of canonical patterns.
minimum = {}
for L in range(1, 13):
    for ds in combinations_with_replacement(
        '23456789', L
    ):
        p = 1
        for d in ds:
            p *= int(d)
        if p % 10:
            minimum.setdefault(p, ''.join(ds))

assert set(minimum.values()) == candidates(12)
print("Independent length <=12:", len(minimum),
      "matches")

# Exhaustive enumeration in the eight families.
for L in (15, 80):
    cs = candidates(L)
    total = Counter()
    nonzero = Counter()
    least = {}

    for s in cs:
        k, d = persistence(int(s))
        total[k] += 1
        if d:
            nonzero[k] += 1
        if k not in least or (
            len(s), s
        ) < (len(least[k]), least[k]):
            least[k] = s

    print("L <=", L, "candidates:", len(cs))
    print("Persistence >=3:",
          {k: total[k] for k in sorted(total)
           if k >= 3})
    print("Nonzero terminal:",
          {k: nonzero[k] for k in sorted(total)
           if k >= 3 and nonzero[k]})

    if L == 15:
        print("Least at 3..11:",
              [least[k] for k in range(3, 12)])

# Independent finite-range test of the S-unit lemma.
smooth = {1}
for prime in (2, 3, 5, 7):
    for old in tuple(smooth):
        v = old * prime
        while v < 10**9:
            smooth.add(v)
            v *= prime

fibers = Counter(f(v) for v in smooth if v >= 10)
print("S-units in [10, 10^9):",
      sum(v >= 10 for v in smooth))
print("Fixed fibers:",
      {t: fibers[t] for t in
       (2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 18, 20)})
```

## 4. Verdict

**Status: OPEN, with a proved partial result.** The conjecture is true for every fixed persistence when the terminal digit is nonzero: this follows rigorously from the finite-fiber lemma derived from Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt (2002). The only substantive unresolved cases are persistence $k\geq3$ with terminal digit zero; persistence 2 with terminal zero is provably infinite. I do not claim originality for the underlying $S$-unit theorem or priority for its application here. Confidence in the mathematical deductions is high; confidence in the negative literature search is necessarily qualified by database-access limitations. A suitable proposed OEIS comment would be: “For each fixed $k$, there are finitely many reduced representatives of persistence $k$ with nonzero multiplicative digital root. Indeed, for each fixed positive $t$, only finitely many $\{2,3,5,7\}$-smooth integers have digit product $t$, by the nondegenerate $S$-unit equation theorem of Evertse, Schlickewei and Schmidt (2002). The finiteness conjecture therefore reduces to terminal digit zero for persistence at least 3.” This report establishes a partial result and a precise reduction, **not a complete proof or counterexample**. No OEIS submission or edit was made.
