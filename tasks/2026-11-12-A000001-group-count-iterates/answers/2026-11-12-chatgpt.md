# Report: OEIS A000001 — The Galloping Gnus Conjecture

**Research date:** 12 November 2026 **Sequence:** [OEIS A000001](https://oeis.org/A000001) — Number of isomorphism classes of groups of order $n$.

## 1. Status and literature

**Status: Open in the literature identified so far.** The conjecture is mathematically well-posed. No general proof or counterexample has been established by the sources examined.

### Original formulation

John H. Conway, Heiko Dietrich, and E. A. O'Brien, *Counting groups: gnus, moas and other exotica*, **The Mathematical Intelligencer** 30 (2008), 6–15, §10, Conjecture 10.1, state:

> “For every positive integer n, the sequence [...] consists ultimately of 1s.”

The statement appears on pages 11–12.

Writing $g(n)$ for the number of groups of order $n$, this means

$$
\boxed{\forall n\ge1,\ \exists k\ge0:\ g^{\circ k}(n)=1.}
$$

Since $g(1)=1$, reaching 1 implies remaining there forever.

The original paper establishes the conjecture for $1\le n<2048$, with at most five iterations, and explicitly notes two weaker unresolved questions: whether any group-perfect number other than 1 exists, and whether any group-amicable pair exists.

### An important correction to the 2008 computations

The paper's example for $n=1024$ uses the historical value

$$
g(1024)=49\,487\,365\,422.
$$

The corrected value is

$$
\boxed{g(1024)=49\,487\,367\,289.}
$$

OEIS A000001 records this correction, attributed to Benjamin Przybocki (6 January 2022).

The corrected number factors as

$$
49\,487\,367\,289=29\cdot181\cdot9\,427\,961.
$$

All three factors are prime, and no smaller prime divides a larger prime minus 1. By the classical characterization of cyclic group orders (also Theorem 7.1 of Conway–Dietrich–O'Brien), its group number is 1.

Consequently, the **corrected trajectory** is

$$
\boxed{1024\longrightarrow49\,487\,367\,289\longrightarrow1.}
$$

This fixes an obsolete numerical example without changing the conjecture's truth status.

## 2. Proof attempt and partial results

### 2.1. A precise equivalent descent criterion

**Proposition (proved).** The galloping gnus conjecture is equivalent to

$$
\boxed{\forall n>1,\ \exists k\ge1:\ g^{\circ k}(n)<n.}
$$

**Proof.** If the conjecture holds, some iterate equals $1<n$.

Conversely, assume every $n>1$ has an iterate smaller than itself. Use strong induction on $n$. The case $n=1$ is immediate. For $n>1$, choose $k$ such that

$$
1\le m=g^{\circ k}(n)<n.
$$

By the induction hypothesis, $m$ eventually reaches 1. Hence so does $n$. $\square$

This is an elementary reformulation, not a new solution or a reduction to a previously solved problem.

It also shows why the inequality $g(n)<n$ is not required at every step. For example, $g(32)=51>32$, but that trajectory still terminates.

The domain restriction matters: OEIS assigns $g(0)=0$, so including zero would immediately make the conjecture false. Conway–Dietrich–O'Brien explicitly restrict Conjecture 10.1 to positive integers.

### 2.2. An infinite-family result

**Proved from known results.** If $n$ is a prime or the product of two primes, counted with multiplicity, its trajectory reaches 1 within two iterations.

Indeed, the standard classifications give

$$
g(p)=1,\qquad g(p^2)=2,
$$

and $g(pq)\in\{1,2\}$ for distinct primes $p,q$. Since $g(2)=1$, the claim follows.

For prime powers of exponents three and four, the known formulas give

$$
g(p^3)=5,\qquad
g(p^4)=
\begin{cases}
14,&p=2,\\
15,&p\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
$$

Their trajectories also terminate, since $g(5)=g(15)=1$ and $g(14)=2$. These are established classifications, not new discoveries.

### 2.3. A density lower bound for first-step descent

**Proved corollary of Dietrich–Jefferies (2026).**

Their arXiv:2608.18815 establishes that, for almost every cubefree integer $n$,

$$
g(n)\le(\log n)^{(\log\log n)^{O(1)}}.
$$

The right-hand side is $n^{o(1)}$, and hence is eventually smaller than $n$.

The natural density of cubefree positive integers is $1/\zeta(3)$. Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\liminf_{x\to\infty}
\frac{\#\{n\le x:g(n)<n\}}{x}
\ge\frac1{\zeta(3)}
\approx0.83190737.
}
$$

**Conclusion:** At least approximately 83.19% of positive integers, in lower asymptotic density, have a first iterate smaller than themselves. This follows from known results; no claim of priority is made for the corollary.

It does **not** prove that those trajectories terminate. Decreasing once and eventually reaching 1 are different properties. Dietrich–Jefferies also give examples of cubefree orders with $g(n)>n$.

### 2.4. Independently checking the exceptional intermediate values

For the finite computation below, the starting range is $1\le n\le2047$. Some iterates exceed 50,000, so the available data table alone is insufficient.

For squarefree $n$, I used Hölder's group-enumeration formula, stated as Theorem 5.1 by Conway–Dietrich–O'Brien.

Let $P$ be the set of prime factors of $n$, and define

$$
o(p,E)=\#\{q\in E:q\equiv1\pmod p\}.
$$

Then

$$
g(n)=
\sum_{S\subseteq P}
\prod_{p\in S}
\frac{p^{o(p,P\setminus S)}-1}{p-1}.
$$

The empty product is 1. This is an exact formula, not a heuristic.

It gives

$$
g(10494213)=5,\qquad
g(408641062)=4,
$$

and $g(49487367289)=1$, as established in Section 1.

Two additional nonsquarefree values require separate justification.

**Claim 1 (proved): $g(56092)=11$.**

Factor

$$
56092=4\cdot37\cdot379.
$$

Sylow's theorems show that both the 37-Sylow and 379-Sylow subgroups are normal: their possible numbers must divide $4\cdot379$ and $4\cdot37$, respectively, while being congruent to 1 modulo the relevant prime. In each case the only possibility is 1.

Their product is therefore a normal cyclic Hall subgroup

$$
K\cong C_{37}\times C_{379}.
$$

By Schur–Zassenhaus, every group of this order is a semidirect product $K\rtimes H$, where $H$ is either $C_4$ or $C_2^2$.

Now

$$
\operatorname{Aut}(K)\cong C_{36}\times C_{378},
$$

whose 2-primary component is $C_4\times C_2$.

For $H=C_4$, there are eight possible images of a generator; identifying inverse images leaves six isomorphism classes.

For $H=C_2^2$, homomorphisms have images in $C_2^2$. Up to automorphisms of the domain, they are classified by their images, which are its five subgroups.

Consequently,

$$
g(56092)=6+5=\boxed{11}.
$$

This independently reproduces the value in the 2008 paper.

**Claim 2 (proved): $g(241004)=4$.**

Here

$$
241004=4\cdot60251,
$$

where 60251 is prime and congruent to 3 modulo 4.

The prime-order Sylow subgroup is necessarily normal. Again, the quotient is $C_4$ or $C_2^2$. Since the 2-primary part of $C_{60250}\cong\operatorname{Aut}(C_{60251})$ is $C_2$, each quotient gives two possible actions up to automorphism.

Thus

$$
g(241004)=2+2=\boxed4.
$$

These calculations, Hölder's formula, and the corrected finite-group counts are sufficient to complete the tested orbits below.

### Reproducible Python program

Download `gnu50000.g` from the linked GitHub repository into the working directory, then run:

```python

import re
from collections import Counter
from pathlib import Path

raw = Path("gnu50000.g").read_text()
pairs = re.findall(r"\[\s*(\d+)\s*,\s*(\d+|fail)\s*\]", raw)

a = {int(n): int(v) for n, v in pairs if v != "fail"}
a[1024] = 49487367289  # corrected value

def factor(n):
    f = {}
    p = 2
    while p*p <= n:
        while n % p == 0:
            f[p] = f.get(p, 0) + 1
            n //= p
        p = 3 if p == 2 else p + 2
    if n > 1:
        f[n] = f.get(n, 0) + 1
    return f

def holder(n):
    f = factor(n)
    if any(e > 1 for e in f.values()):
        return None
    P = list(f)
    total = 0
    for S in range(1 << len(P)):
        term = 1
        for i, p in enumerate(P):
            if (S >> i) & 1:
                opp = sum(
                    (q-1) % p == 0
                    for j, q in enumerate(P)
                    if not ((S >> j) & 1)
                )
                term *= (p**opp - 1) // (p-1)
        total += term
    return total

def g(n):
    if n in a:
        return a[n]
    if n == 56092:
        return 11
    if n == 241004:
        return 4
    return holder(n)

def steps(n):
    seen = set()
    k = 0
    while n != 1:
        if n in seen:
            return None
        seen.add(n)
        nxt = g(n)
        if nxt is None:
            return None
        n = nxt
        k += 1
    return k

def test(N):
    results = {n: steps(n) for n in range(1, N+1)}
    hist = Counter(k for k in results.values() if k is not None)
    maximum = max(hist)
    print(f"Range: 1..{N}")
    print("Certified:", sum(k is not None for k in results.values()))
    print("Uncertified:", sum(k is None for k in results.values()))
    print("Step counts:", sorted(hist.items()))
    print("Maximum:", maximum)
    print("At maximum:", [n for n, k in results.items() if k == maximum])

squarefree = [
    n for n in range(1, 2048)
    if all(e == 1 for e in factor(n).values())
]
assert all(holder(n) == a[n] for n in squarefree)
assert all(
    a[n] <= n * __import__("math").prod(
        (p-1)/p for p in factor(n)
    )
    for n in squarefree
)
print("Squarefree cross-checks:", len(squarefree), "passed")

test(2047)
test(50000)
```
