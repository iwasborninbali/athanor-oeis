# OEIS A129924 — Status and Proof of the A064384 Identity

## 1. Status: PROVED (2007)

**Verdict: The conjectured identity is already a theorem, not an open problem.** It was explicitly confirmed by Jonathan Sondow on June 12, 2007, in the OEIS entry for A129924.

The conjecture is

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{A129924}(n)=\mathrm{A064384}(n+1),\qquad n\ge1.}
$$

The decisive statement appears in [OEIS A129924](https://oeis.org/A129924), in a comment by **Jonathan Sondow, June 12, 2007**:

> The conjecture is true.

Sondow identifies the result as a consequence of the identity

$$
\gcd\bigl(\mathrm{A061354}(m),
         \mathrm{A061354}(m+2)\bigr)
=\mathrm{A124779}(m),
$$

using $m=p-3$. His comment explicitly points to the related entries A064384 and A124779 and his work on the Taylor series for $e$.

The apparent open conjecture is therefore a **stale conjecture line** retained alongside its resolution in the same OEIS entry. Its inclusion in a conjecture-screening list is a false positive.

The remaining issue—whether infinitely many primes occur in A064384—is separate from this identity.

A stronger, independently checkable published source is:

**Jonathan Sondow and Kyle Schalm (2010)**, *Which Partial Sums of the Taylor Series for e Are Convergents to e? (and a Link to the Primes 2, 5, 13, 37, 463), II*, in *Gems in Experimental Mathematics*, Contemporary Mathematics, vol. 517, American Mathematical Society, Providence, Rhode Island. Earlier version: [arXiv:0709.0671](https://arxiv.org/abs/0709.0671) (2007).

Section 5, **Theorem 5.1**, states:

> More precisely, $R_1=2$, and $R_{p-3}=p$ if $p\in P^*$ is odd; otherwise, $R_n=1$.

Here $R_n=\gcd(N_n,N_{n+2})$, where $N_n$ is the reduced numerator of the $n$-th Taylor partial sum for $e$, and $P^*$ is precisely the prime set A064384. Lemma 5.2 supplies the alternating-factorial characterization.

This establishes the requested identity directly.

## 2. Proof: a direct reconstruction

**Status: PROVED.** The following is an elementary reconstruction of the known result, not a claim of a new theorem.

Define

$$
F_m=m!\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac1{k!},
\qquad
N_m=\frac{F_m}{\gcd(F_m,m!)}.
$$

Thus $N_m=\mathrm{A061354}(m)$, and

$$
F_0=1,\qquad F_m=mF_{m-1}+1.
$$

We prove that for every prime $p\ge3$,

$$
\boxed{
p\mid N_{p-3},\ p\mid N_{p-1}
\iff
p\mid\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(-1)^j j!
}
$$

### Step 1. Reduced numerators

If $p$ is prime and $0\le m<p$, then $p\nmid m!$. Consequently,

$$
p\mid N_m \iff p\mid F_m,
$$

because the cancellation factor $\gcd(F_m,m!)$ is not divisible by $p$.

This applies to both $m=p-3$ and $m=p-1$.

### Step 2. The two divisibility conditions coincide

Applying the recurrence twice gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_{p-1}
&=(p-1)F_{p-2}+1\\
&=(p-1)(p-2)F_{p-3}+p.
\end{aligned}
$$

Modulo $p$,

$$
F_{p-1}\equiv 2F_{p-3}\pmod p.
$$

Since $p\ge3$, the factor $2$ is invertible modulo $p$. Hence

$$
p\mid F_{p-3}\iff p\mid F_{p-1}.
$$

By Step 1, the condition defining A129924 is therefore equivalent to

$$
p\mid F_{p-1}.
$$

### Step 3. Connection with alternating factorials

By definition,

$$
F_{p-1}
=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\frac{(p-1)!}{k!}.
$$

Put $j=p-1-k$. Then

$$
F_{p-1}
=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}
(p-1)(p-2)\cdots(p-j),
$$

with the empty product equal to $1$.

For each $j$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
(p-1)(p-2)\cdots(p-j)
&\equiv(-1)(-2)\cdots(-j)\\
&=(-1)^j j!\pmod p.
\end{aligned}
$$

Summing,

$$
\boxed{
F_{p-1}\equiv
\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(-1)^j j!
\pmod p.
}
$$

But the right-hand divisibility condition is exactly the definition of [A064384](https://oeis.org/A064384).

Combining Steps 1–3 proves

$$
p\in\mathrm{A129924}
\iff
p\in\mathrm{A064384}
\quad(p\ge3).
$$

### Step 4. The exceptional prime 2

The prime $2$ belongs to A064384 because

$$
0!-1!=0.
$$

It cannot belong to A129924, since that definition would require evaluating $N_{-1}$, outside the sequence's domain.

The prime $3$ belongs to neither sequence.

Therefore A129924 consists of exactly the terms of A064384 after deleting its first term, $2$. Both sequences have offset 1, giving

$$
\boxed{
\mathrm{A129924}(n)=\mathrm{A064384}(n+1)
\quad\text{for every }n\ge1.
}
$$

**QED.**

This proof establishes an identity of the complete, mathematically defined sequences; it does not depend on how many terms have been calculated.

## 3. Computational verification

**Status: COMPUTED**, independently of the published theorem.

I ran the following Python 3 program using exact integer arithmetic. It checks the modular equivalences for every prime up to 10,000 and separately constructs reduced numerators through index 1002.

```python

from math import gcd, isqrt

B, M = 10_000, 1_000
prime = [True] * (B + 1)
prime[0] = prime[1] = False

for d in range(2, isqrt(B) + 1):
    if prime[d]:
        for x in range(d*d, B+1, d):
            prime[x] = False

primes = [p for p in range(2, B+1) if prime[p]]

# Modular recurrence and alternating factorials
P = []
for p in primes:
    f = fac = alt = 1
    fp3 = None

    for k in range(1, p):
        f = (k*f + 1) % p
        fac = k*fac % p
        alt = (alt + (-fac if k % 2 else fac)) % p
        if k == p-3:
            fp3 = f

    assert f == alt
    if p >= 3:
        assert (fp3 == 0) == (f == 0)
    if f == 0:
        P.append(p)

# Independently construct exact reduced numerators
numerators = []
f = fac = 1
for k in range(M+3):
    if k:
        f = k*f + 1
        fac *= k
    numerators.append(f // gcd(f, fac))

direct = [
    p for p in primes if 3 <= p <= M+2
    and numerators[p-3] % p == numerators[p-1] % p == 0
]

R = [
    (n, gcd(numerators[n], numerators[n+2]))
    for n in range(M+1)
    if gcd(numerators[n], numerators[n+2]) > 1
]

assert direct == [p for p in P if 3 <= p <= M+2]
assert R == [(1, 2)] + [(p-3, p) for p in direct]

print(f"Modular checks: {len(primes)} primes p <= {B}; "
      f"{len(primes)} congruences and {len(primes)-1} "
      "two-step equivalences passed")
print("A064384 hits:", P)
print(f"Exact reduced numerators: n = 0..{M+2}; "
      f"gcd tests n = 0..{M} passed")
print("A129924 hits (exact, p <= 1002):", direct)
print("Nontrivial gcds (n, gcd):", R)
```

## 4. Verdict

**PROVED — known since 2007; high confidence; no new mathematical result claimed.** The identity $\mathrm{A129924}(n)=\mathrm{A064384}(n+1)$ follows directly from the recurrence for the integer numerators of the Taylor partial sums for $e$ and a congruence with alternating factorials.
