# OEIS A011541 — Status and proof attempt for the taxicab prime-factor conjecture

**Research date:** November 12, 2026 **Verdict:** Apparently open; verified for all six published terms. A numerical error in the conjecture's OEIS commentary is identified and corrected. No general proof or counterexample was obtained.

## 1. Status

### 1.1 The conjecture

Let $a(n)=\operatorname{Ta}(n)$ denote OEIS A011541, the smallest positive integer expressible as a sum of two positive integral cubes in at least $n$ distinct unordered ways.

Let $\omega(m)$ denote the number of distinct prime divisors of $m$, without multiplicity, as in OEIS A001221.

The conjecture is

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\omega(a(n+1))&>\omega(a(n)),\\
\omega(a(n))&\leq 2n
\end{aligned}
\qquad(n\geq1).
}
$$

**Source:** Sergey Pavlov, March 1, 2017, comment on [OEIS A011541](https://oeis.org/A011541).

The decisive wording is:

> Conjecture: the number of distinct prime factors of a(n) is strictly increasing as n grows

The comment also conjectures the bound $2n$.

The statement is mathematically well-defined. Its two assertions are logically separate; neither is known to follow from the other.

### 1.2 Literature findings

I found no published proof or counterexample to either assertion.

Wilson establishes the fifth term through computation and describes the combination and magnification techniques for constructing numbers with more cube-sum representations.

### 1.3 A correction to the OEIS conjecture comment

Pavlov's comment includes the parenthetical assertion that strict increase would fail if $a(7)$ equaled the published upper bound.

This is **arithmetically incorrect for the displayed bound**.

The sixth listed term is

$$
a(6)=24153319581254312065344
$$

with factorization

$$
a(6)=2^6 3^3 7^4
\cdot13\cdot19\cdot43\cdot73
\cdot79^3\cdot97\cdot157.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\omega(a(6))=10.
$$

The seventh upper-bound candidate is

$$
B_7=24885189317885898975235988544.
$$

Exact division gives

$$
\boxed{B_7=101^3a(6).}
$$

Since 101 does not divide $a(6)$,

$$
\boxed{\omega(B_7)=11>10=\omega(a(6)).}
$$

Moreover, $11\leq14=2\cdot7$.

Thus, **if $a(7)=B_7$, both conjectured inequalities would hold at $n=7$**.

This corrects the parenthetical remark without proving the conjecture. The factor count of an upper bound says nothing conclusive about the factor count of the unknown minimum.

## 2. Proof attempt and partial results

### 2.1 Exact divisor criterion for representations — proved

Define

$$
r(N)=\#\{(x,y)\in\mathbb Z_{>0}^2:x\leq y,\ x^3+y^3=N\}.
$$

For any representation, set

$$
s=x+y,\qquad t=y-x.
$$

Then

$$
x=\frac{s-t}{2},\qquad y=\frac{s+t}{2},
$$

and direct expansion gives

$$
\boxed{4N=s^3+3st^2.}
$$

Also,

$$
N=(x+y)(x^2-xy+y^2),
$$

so $s\mid N$.

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{t^2=\frac{4N/s-s^2}{3}.}
$$

The representation conditions are precisely:

1. $s$ is a positive divisor of $N$;
2. $N<s^3\leq4N$;
3. $(4N/s-s^2)/3$ is a nonnegative perfect square $t^2$;
4. $0\leq t<s$, and $s\equiv t\pmod2$.

These conditions are necessary by the preceding identities.

They are also sufficient: the last condition produces positive integers

$$
x=(s-t)/2,\qquad y=(s+t)/2,
$$

and the displayed identity guarantees $x^3+y^3=N$.

Different unordered representations have different values of $s$, because a valid $s$ determines $t^2$, the nonnegative $t$, and hence $x,y$ uniquely.

Therefore, representations are in bijection with divisors satisfying this test.

**Corollary — proved:**

$$
\boxed{
r(N)\leq
\#\{d:d\mid N,\ N<d^3\leq4N\}
\leq\tau(N),
}
$$

where $\tau(N)$ counts the positive divisors of $N$.

In particular,

$$
\boxed{\tau(a(n))\geq n.}
$$

This is a general necessary condition, not a proof of either prime-factor conjecture. The divisor function depends on prime exponents as well as the number of distinct primes.

I do not claim this elementary divisor method as new mathematics.

### 2.2 A necessary growth condition — proved conditionally

Suppose strict increase holds for all $n$.

Because $\omega(a(1))=1$ and the values of $\omega$ are integers,

$$
\omega(a(n))\geq n.
$$

Let $p_k$ denote the $k$-th prime. Any positive integer with at least $n$ distinct prime divisors is at least the product of the first $n$ primes.

Hence strict monotonicity implies

$$
\boxed{
a(n)\geq\prod_{k=1}^{n}p_k.
}
$$

This gives a potential route to refutation: an independently verified upper bound for $a(n)$ smaller than the $n$-th primorial would contradict strict increase.

The currently examined bounds do not yield such a contradiction.

This implication is elementary and conditional; it is not evidence that the full conjecture is true.

### 2.3 The seventh upper bound — computed and certified

The representation algorithm from §2.1 finds exactly seven unordered positive-cube representations of $B_7$.

Six arise by multiplying the six published representations of $a(6)$ by $101$. The seventh is

$$
\boxed{
B_7=459531128^3+2915734948^3.
}
$$

Neither of these summands is divisible by 101, so this representation is distinct from the six scaled representations.

Direct integer arithmetic verifies the equality.

Therefore $a(7)\leq B_7$, independently of the OEIS assertion of that bound.

The next two published candidates satisfy

$$
B_8=127^3B_7,\qquad
B_9=139^3B_8.
$$

The same divisor criterion finds exactly eight and nine representations, respectively.

Their distinct-prime-factor counts are

$$
\omega(B_7)=11,\quad
\omega(B_8)=12,\quad
\omega(B_9)=13.
$$

These are **properties of the candidate upper bounds**, not established values of $\omega(a(7)),\omega(a(8)),\omega(a(9))$.

### 3.2 Reproducible Python program

The following program uses only Python's standard library. Trial division is sufficient because every number tested factors entirely into primes no larger than 223.

```python

from collections import Counter
from math import isqrt

A = [2, 1729, 87539319, 6963472309248,
     48988659276962496, 24153319581254312065344]
B = [24885189317885898975235988544,
     50974398750539071400590819921724352,
     136897813798023990395783317207361432493888]

def factor(n):
    f, p = {}, 2
    while p*p <= n:
        if n % p == 0:
            e = 0
            while n % p == 0:
                n //= p
                e += 1
            f[p] = e
        p += 1
    if n > 1:
        f[n] = 1
    return f

def divisors(f):
    ds = [1]
    for p, e in f.items():
        ds = [d*p**j for d in ds for j in range(e+1)]
    return ds

def representations(N, f):
    out = []
    for s in divisors(f):
        if not N < s**3 <= 4*N:
            continue
        q = 4*(N//s) - s*s
        if q < 0 or q % 3:
            continue
        t = isqrt(q//3)
        if t*t == q//3 and t < s and (s-t) % 2 == 0:
            x, y = (s-t)//2, (s+t)//2
            if x > 0 and x**3 + y**3 == N:
                out.append((x,y))
    return out

for k, N in enumerate(A+B, 1):
    f = factor(N)
    r = len(representations(N, f))
    assert r == k and len(f) <= 2*k
    print(f"{k}: kind={'a' if k<=6 else 'B'}, omega={len(f)}, r={r}, 2n={2*k}")

counts = Counter()
x = 1
while 2*x**3 <= A[2]:
    y = x
    while x**3 + y**3 <= A[2]:
        counts[x**3 + y**3] += 1
        y += 1
    x += 1

print('exhaustive pairs:', sum(counts.values()))
print('distinct sums:', len(counts))
print('minima n=1..3:', [min(v for v,c in counts.items() if c>=k) for k in (1,2,3)])
print('B7=a6*101^3:', B[0] == A[-1]*101**3)
print('B8=B7*127^3:', B[1] == B[0]*127**3)
print('B9=B8*139^3:', B[2] == B[1]*139**3)
```

## 4. Verdict

**Status: apparently open, with no proof or counterexample found as of November 12, 2026.** The conjectured strict increase and $2n$ upper bound hold for all six values presently published in A011541, with distinct-prime-factor counts $1,3,5,8,9,10$. An exact divisor-based test independently verifies their representation counts and the seven-, eight-, and nine-representation upper-bound candidates. The main concrete correction is that Pavlov's 2017 parenthetical remark is inconsistent with the published seventh upper bound: $B_7=101^3a(6)$, so $\omega(B_7)=11>\omega(a(6))=10$, and equality $a(7)=B_7$ would not violate the conjecture. The divisor criterion and necessary growth inequality are elementary partial results, not claimed as novel. No reduction to a named open problem was established. Confidence is high in the exact arithmetic and the correction, but limited regarding global open status because complete arXiv, Zenodo, MathSciNet, and zbMATH catalogue searches were not available. An appropriate OEIS comment would report the computed counts and correct the parenthetical without asserting a resolution of the conjecture.
