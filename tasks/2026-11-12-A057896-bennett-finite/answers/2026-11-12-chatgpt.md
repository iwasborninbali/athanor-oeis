# 1. Status — Open (as of 12 November 2026)

**Verdict:** The conjecture underlying OEIS A057896 remains apparently **open**. I found no verified proof or counterexample. The existing literature provides substantial partial results, but not a complete classification.

### Precise statement

Define

$$
R(n)=\{(m,k)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge2}^2:m^k-m=n\}.
$$

The conjecture is that

$$
\boxed{
\{n>0:|R(n)|\ge2\}
=
\{6,30,210,240,2184,8190,78120,24299970\}.
}
$$

OEIS also includes $0$, which has infinitely many trivial representations (for example, $m^1-m=0$ for every positive integer $m$). Thus the complete proposed sequence is

$$
0,6,30,210,240,2184,8190,78120,24299970.
$$

This formulation is well-posed and does not depend on the convention for $0^0$.

### Primary references

**Michael A. Bennett (2001).** *On Some Exponential Equations of S. S. Pillai*, Canadian Journal of Mathematics **53**(5), 897–922, DOI: [10.4153/CJM-2001-036-6](https://doi.org/10.4153/CJM-2001-036-6).

Bennett's Theorem 1.1 proves that the equation

$$
a^u-b^v=c
$$

has at most two positive-integer solutions $(u,v)$ when $a,b\ge2$ and $c\ne0$ are fixed. His Conjecture 1.2 asserts that two solutions occur only for a specified collection of exceptional equations.

This theorem does **not** prove A057896: a collision

$$
x^r-x=y^s-y
$$

already supplies two solutions, $(u,v)=(1,1)$ and $(s,r)$, to

$$
y^u-x^v=y-x.
$$

Bennett's stronger **Conjecture 1.2**, if proved, would imply completeness of A057896 after retaining the exceptional equations that admit $(1,1)$. The reverse implication is not established.

**Dana Mackenzie (2018).** *2184: An Absurd (and Adsurd) Tale*, **INTEGERS 18**, paper A33, published 30 March 2018. [Full paper](https://math.colgate.edu/~integers/s33/s33.pdf).

On page 2, Mackenzie states the decisive formulation:

> Conjecture 3. (Bennett) The eight numbers listed below are the only doubly absurd numbers.

Mackenzie proves special cases. In particular, Theorem 1 establishes that

$$
x^r-x=y^3-y,
$$

with $x>1$, $y$ prime, $r>3$ odd, has only the nontrivial solution

$$
(x,r,y)=(3,7,13).
$$

Theorem 2 gives further exclusions for exponents $4\le s\le14$, under additional congruence and primality hypotheses. These do not establish the full conjecture.

**OEIS (2026).** The live [A057896 entry](https://oeis.org/A057896) still says:

> Conjectured to be finite and complete by Bennett (2001).

Its strongest reported computational bound is Max Alekseyev's comment dated **4 March 2026**:

> a(10) > 10^50, if it exists.

This is a reported computational result, not a published proof of completeness. The entry also cites earlier bounds by David Wasserman (2002) and Giovanni Resta (2018).

# 2. Proof attempt and rigorous partial results

## 2.1. An elementary exclusion for non-coprime exponents

**Proved.** If

$$
x^r-x=y^s-y>0
$$

has two distinct representations with $x,y,r,s\ge2$, then

$$
\boxed{\gcd(r,s)=1.}
$$

This eliminates every pair of exponents having a common factor greater than 1, without any upper bound on the bases or exponents.

**Proof.**

Because the function $t\mapsto t^k-t$ is strictly increasing on integers $t\ge2$ for every $k\ge2$, and increases strictly with $k$ for fixed $t\ge2$, we may arrange the distinct representations so that

$$
2\le x<y,\qquad r>s\ge2.
$$

The given equality becomes

$$
y^s-x^r=y-x,
$$

hence

$$
0<y^s-x^r=y-x<y. \tag{1}
$$

Suppose $d=\gcd(r,s)\ge2$. Put

$$
A=x^{r/d},\qquad B=y^{s/d}.
$$

Then $A$ and $B$ are positive integers, $B>A$, and $B\ge y$.

Factoring the difference of powers gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
B^d-A^d
&=(B-A)\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}B^{d-1-j}A^j\\
&\ge B^{d-1}\\
&\ge B\\
&\ge y.
\end{aligned}
$$

But $B^d-A^d=y^s-x^r=y-x<y$ by (1). This is a contradiction.

Therefore $\gcd(r,s)=1$. $\square$

**Status:** Rigorous elementary deduction, independently checked here. I do not claim originality; the argument uses a standard gap between consecutive perfect powers.

In particular, there can be no collision involving exponents $(4,2)$, $(6,3)$, $(10,5)$, or indeed any pair with nontrivial greatest common divisor.

## 2.2. A complete classification for small base differences

**Known theorem, derived corollary.** Bennett's Theorem 1.5 (2001, pp. 899–900) completely classifies the relevant solutions when the difference between the bases is at most $100$.

Specifically, if

$$
2\le x<y,\qquad 1\le y-x\le100,
$$

then the only possible collisions $x^r-x=y^s-y>0$ are:

| $n$ | Smaller-base representation | Larger-base representation | $y-x$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 | $2^3-2$ | $3^2-3$ | 1 |
| 30 | $2^5-2$ | $6^2-6$ | 4 |
| 210 | $6^3-6$ | $15^2-15$ | 9 |
| 240 | $3^5-3$ | $16^2-16$ | 13 |
| 2184 | $3^7-3$ | $13^3-13$ | 10 |
| 8190 | $2^{13}-2$ | $91^2-91$ | 89 |

**Proof of the corollary.** Write $a=y$, $b=x$, and $c=y-x$. The equation

$$
a^u-b^v=c
$$

then has two distinct solutions $(1,1)$ and $(s,r)$. By Bennett's Theorem 1.5, since $1\le c\le100$, the triple $(a,b,c)$ must belong to his explicit list of ten exceptional triples. Imposing the additional relation $c=a-b$ leaves exactly

$$
(3,2,1),\ (6,2,4),\ (15,6,9),\
(16,3,13),\ (13,3,10),\ (91,2,89).
$$

These yield precisely the six values above. $\square$

This is a rigorous infinite-range result: **the exponents are unrestricted**. It is not new mathematics; it follows directly from Bennett's published theorem.

## 2.3. What remains unresolved

The conjecture is not settled by either result. The known solutions include the two cases with base differences greater than 100:

$$
78120=5^7-5=280^2-280,
$$

$$
24299970=30^5-30=4930^2-4930.
$$

The unbounded regime with relatively prime exponents and arbitrary base differences remains the principal obstacle.

The exact reduction

$$
x^r-x=y^s-y
\iff
y^s-x^r=y-x
$$

places the problem inside Bennett's generalized Pillai framework. A proof of his Conjecture 1.2 would prove the desired completeness, but his Theorem 1.1 alone cannot do so.

# 3. Independent computation

**Computed, not proved globally.** I implemented an exhaustive search for all positive values

$$
0<n\le10^{18}.
$$

The algorithm enumerates every representation $m^k-m\le10^{18}$ with $m\ge2$, $k\ge3$. It does not enumerate quadratic bases up to $10^9$. Instead, it uses the exact criterion

$$
n=y^2-y
\iff
4n+1=(2y-1)^2.
$$

Every positive collision must contain at least one exponent $k\ge3$, because the quadratic function $m^2-m$ is strictly increasing for $m\ge2$. Thus this search is exhaustive within its stated numerical bound.

### Python 3 checker

```python

from math import gcd, isqrt

LIMIT = 10**18
seen = {}
collisions = {}
checked = 0
maxk = 0

k = 3
while 2**k - 2 <= LIMIT:
    maxk = k
    m = 2
    while True:
        v = m**k - m
        if v > LIMIT:
            break

        checked += 1
        pair = (m, k)

        if v in seen:
            collisions.setdefault(v, {seen[v]}).add(pair)
        else:
            seen[v] = pair

        D = 4*v + 1
        s = isqrt(D)
        if s*s == D:
            y = (1+s)//2
            assert y*(y-1) == v
            collisions.setdefault(v, {pair}).add((y, 2))

        m += 1
    k += 1

results = {
    v: sorted(reps)
    for v, reps in sorted(collisions.items())
    if len(reps) > 1
}

for n, reps in results.items():
    for m, k in reps:
        assert m**k - m == n
    assert all(
        gcd(a[1], b[1]) == 1
        for i, a in enumerate(reps)
        for b in reps[i+1:]
    )

print(f'LIMIT={LIMIT}')
print(f'MAX_EXPONENT={maxk}')
print(f'NONQUADRATIC_REPRESENTATIONS={checked}')
print(f'DISTINCT_NONQUADRATIC_VALUES={len(seen)}')
print(f'POSITIVE_COLLISIONS={len(results)}')
for n, reps in results.items():
    print(f'{n}: {reps}')
print('INCLUDING_ZERO=' + repr([0]+list(results)))
print('ALL_COLLISION_EXPONENT_PAIRS_COPRIME=True')
```

# 4. Verdict

**Status: apparently open, with high confidence in the stated partial results but not in the exhaustiveness of the literature search.** The eight-positive-values conjecture of Bennett remains unproved and unrefuted in the sources I could verify as of 12 November 2026. Bennett (2001) proves a complete classification for base differences at most 100 via Theorem 1.5, and Mackenzie (2018) proves additional infinite families, notably the prime-base, cubic-exponent case. I supplied a short independent proof that colliding representations must have coprime exponents, without claiming it is original, and independently computed all collisions through $10^{18}$; no new terms were found. The strongest recorded computational exclusion is Alekseyev's March 2026 OEIS bound $a(10)>10^{50}$, which I have not reproduced. A suitable **proposed OEIS comment**, without submitting it, would be: “In any nontrivial equality $x^r-x=y^s-y$, with $x,y,r,s\ge2$ and distinct representations, the exponents $r,s$ must be coprime: otherwise the two powers are distinct $d$-th powers whose difference is smaller than the larger base, contradicting the minimum gap between $d$-th powers. An independent exhaustive search through $10^{18}$ recovers exactly the currently listed terms. Completeness remains conjectural.”

**Final answer:** No complete proof or counterexample found. The conjecture remains open on the available evidence; the proved coprime-exponent restriction and the bounded computational verification are checkable contributions to this report, not a resolution of A057896.
