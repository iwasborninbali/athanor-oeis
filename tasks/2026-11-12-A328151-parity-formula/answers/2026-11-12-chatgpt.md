# OEIS A328151 — Status and proof of the parity formula

**Date:** 12 November 2026 **Conjecture:** Jon E. Schoenfield, 23 January 2022 **Verdict:** **Both identities are true, with an elementary proof.** The OEIS entry still labels the statement a conjecture. I did not locate a previously published proof, but the argument below uses standard, established mathematics and should not be claimed as a novel mathematical discovery without further literature review.

## 1. Status and prior work

Let $a(n)$ denote OEIS A328151.

The conjecture is

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
a(2k)&=\mathrm{A093195}(k), && k\geq1,\\
a(2k+1)&=2\,\mathrm{A006339}(k)^2, && k\geq0.
\end{aligned}}
$$

The definitions of the related sequences are:

- **A093195**, introduced by Lekraj Beedassy (2004, OEIS): the smallest positive integer representable as a sum of two distinct positive squares in exactly $k$ unordered ways.
- **A006339**, by David W. Wilson (OEIS): the smallest hypotenuse of exactly $k$ distinct integer right triangles. Its computational formula was supplied by Albert H. Mao (2012, OEIS).
- **A328151**, by Felix Fröhlich (2019, OEIS): the smallest nonnegative integer admitting exactly $n$ ordered representations as a sum of two positive squares. Schoenfield's 2022 formula remains explicitly labeled “Conjecture” in the live entry.

A relevant prior observation is due to Chai Wah Wu (28 June 2024, OEIS A328151):

> If a(n) exists, then a(n) is of the form 2*m^2 if and only if n is odd.

This observation identifies the parity structure but does not itself state the minimization argument establishing both conjectured identities.

## 2. Proof

### Definitions

For every nonnegative integer $N$, define

$$
R(N)=\#\{(x,y)\in\mathbb Z_{>0}^2:x^2+y^2=N\}
$$

and

$$
D(N)=\#\{(x,y)\in\mathbb Z_{>0}^2:x<y,\ x^2+y^2=N\}.
$$

Thus $R(N)$ counts ordered representations and $D(N)$ counts unordered representations with distinct positive entries.

Every off-diagonal representation contributes two ordered representations. A diagonal representation exists precisely when $N=2m^2$ for some positive integer $m$, and is then unique.

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{R(N)=2D(N)+\mathbf1_{\{N=2m^2,\ m\geq1\}}.}\tag{1}
$$

This is a proved identity, independent of any OEIS conjecture.

### Lemma: the Pythagorean bijection

**Claim.** For every positive integer $m$,

$$
\boxed{D(2m^2)=D(m^2)=T(m),}\tag{2}
$$

where $T(m)$ is the number of distinct Pythagorean triangles with hypotenuse $m$, counting interchange of the legs as the same triangle.

**Proof.**

A Pythagorean triangle with hypotenuse $m$ has uniquely ordered positive legs $u>v>0$, satisfying

$$
u^2+v^2=m^2.
$$

Define

$$
x=u+v,\qquad y=u-v.
$$

Then $x>y>0$, and

$$
x^2+y^2
=(u+v)^2+(u-v)^2
=2(u^2+v^2)
=2m^2.
$$

Conversely, suppose $x>y>0$ and

$$
x^2+y^2=2m^2.
$$

Because the sum is even, $x$ and $y$ have the same parity. Therefore

$$
u=\frac{x+y}{2},\qquad
v=\frac{x-y}{2}
$$

are positive integers with $u>v$, and

$$
u^2+v^2=\frac{x^2+y^2}{2}=m^2.
$$

The two transformations are inverses, establishing a bijection.

By the definition of a Pythagorean triangle, $D(m^2)=T(m)$. The bijection proves $D(2m^2)=T(m)$, completing the proof. $\square$

Combining (1) and (2) gives the stronger identity

$$
\boxed{R(2m^2)=2T(m)+1.}\tag{3}
$$

### The odd-index identity

**Theorem 1.** For every $k\geq0$,

$$
\boxed{a(2k+1)=2\,\mathrm{A006339}(k)^2.}
$$

**Proof.**

By (1), $R(N)$ is odd if and only if $N=2m^2$ for some positive integer $m$.

By (3),

$$
R(2m^2)=2k+1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
T(m)=k.
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
a(2k+1)
&=\min_{\substack{m\geq1\\T(m)=k}}2m^2\\
&=2\left(\min_{\substack{m\geq1\\T(m)=k}}m\right)^2\\
&=2\,\mathrm{A006339}(k)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last equality is exactly the definition of A006339.

Thus the odd-index identity is proved. $\square$

### The even-index identity

**Theorem 2.** For every $k\geq1$,

$$
\boxed{a(2k)=\mathrm{A093195}(k).}
$$

**Proof.**

Write

$$
B=\mathrm{A093195}(k)
=\min\{N\geq0:D(N)=k\}.
$$

We first prove that $B$ cannot have the form $2m^2$.

Suppose, for contradiction, that $B=2m^2$. By the lemma,

$$
D(m^2)=D(2m^2)=D(B)=k.
$$

But

$$
m^2<2m^2=B,
$$

contradicting the minimality of $B$.

Therefore $B\neq2m^2$ for every positive integer $m$.

Equation (1) consequently gives

$$
R(B)=2D(B)=2k.
$$

Hence $B$ is a candidate for $a(2k)$, so

$$
a(2k)\leq B.
$$

Conversely, if $R(N)=2k$, then equation (1) forces the diagonal indicator to be zero and $D(N)=k$. By the definition of $B$,

$$
N\geq B.
$$

Taking the minimum over such $N$ yields

$$
a(2k)\geq B.
$$

Combining both inequalities,

$$
a(2k)=B=\mathrm{A093195}(k).
$$

Thus the even-index identity is proved. $\square$

### Existence and boundary cases

Both minimizations exist for every stated index.

For $k\geq0$, the classical two-squares representation formula gives

$$
r_2(5^{2k})=4(2k+1),
$$

where $r_2$ counts all signed, ordered integer solutions, including zeros.

Exactly four solutions lie on the coordinate axes, and no nonzero solution has equal absolute coordinates. The remaining $8k$ solutions therefore give exactly $k$ unordered representations with distinct positive entries.

Hence

$$
T(5^k)=D(5^{2k})=k.
$$

This supplies an admissible candidate for A006339($k$) and, when $k\geq1$, A093195($k$). The minimizations are over nonempty subsets of the positive integers, so the minima exist.

For $k=0$, A006339(0) is 1 and the odd formula gives $a(1)=2$. Separately, $a(0)=0$ by definition.

**Both conjectured identities hold for every index in their specified domains.**

## 3. Computational verification

I ran a Python 3 verification using direct enumeration of positive square pairs and an independently implemented Pythagorean-triangle counter.

For a second check of the minimizers, I used the classical sum-of-two-squares formula. Define $F(t)$ to be the least integer having $4t$ signed ordered representations as a sum of two integer squares. It can be computed from multiplicative partitions:

$$
F(t)=\min_{\substack{t=f_1\cdots f_s\\f_1\geq\cdots\geq f_s\geq2}}
\prod_{i=1}^{s}p_i^{f_i-1},
$$

where $p_1,p_2,\ldots=5,13,17,29,\ldots$ are the primes congruent to $1\pmod4$, and $F(1)=1$.

The classical representation formula gives the independently computable candidates

$$
a(n)=
\begin{cases}
0,&n=0,\\
2F(n),&n\text{ odd},\\
\min(F(n),F(n+1)),&n>0\text{ even}.
\end{cases}
$$

The following is the executed verification program.

```python

from math import isqrt
from functools import lru_cache

LIMIT = 1_000_000
R = [0] * (LIMIT + 1)
D = [0] * (LIMIT + 1)

# Direct enumeration of positive square pairs.
for x in range(1, isqrt(LIMIT) + 1):
    for y in range(x, isqrt(LIMIT - x*x) + 1):
        N = x*x + y*y
        if x == y:
            R[N] += 1
        else:
            R[N] += 2
            D[N] += 1

# Independent enumeration of Pythagorean triangles.
M = isqrt(LIMIT // 2)
T = [0] * (M + 1)

for m in range(1, M + 1):
    mm = m*m
    for a in range(1, m):
        b = isqrt(mm - a*a)
        if a < b and a*a + b*b == mm:
            T[m] += 1

assert all(
    R[2*m*m] == 2*T[m] + 1
    and D[m*m] == D[2*m*m] == T[m]
    for m in range(1, M + 1)
)

def isprime(n):
    return n >= 2 and all(
        n % d for d in range(2, isqrt(n)+1)
    )

primes = [
    p for p in range(5, 150)
    if p % 4 == 1 and isprime(p)
]

# Minimize products over multiplicative partitions.
@lru_cache(None)
def F(t):
    best = None

    def search(rem, upper, j, value):
        nonlocal best
        if rem == 1:
            if best is None or value < best:
                best = value
            return
        for f in range(2, min(rem, upper)+1):
            if rem % f == 0:
                v = value * primes[j]**(f-1)
                if best is None or v < best:
                    search(rem//f, f, j+1, v)

    search(t, t, 0, 1)
    return best

def predicted(n):
    if n == 0:
        return 0
    return 2*F(n) if n % 2 else min(F(n), F(n+1))

def first_indices(C):
    found = {}
    for n, c in enumerate(C):
        if c not in found:
            found[c] = n
    return found

minR = first_indices(R)
minD = first_indices(D)

verified_n = [
    n for n in range(101)
    if predicted(n) <= LIMIT
]
assert all(minR[n] == predicted(n) for n in verified_n)

verified_k = [
    k for k in range(1, 51)
    if min(F(2*k), F(2*k+1)) <= LIMIT
]
assert all(
    minD[k] == min(F(2*k), F(2*k+1))
    for k in verified_k
)

first15 = [predicted(n) for n in range(15)]

print(f"Direct search: 0 <= N <= {LIMIT:,}")
print(f"Bijection checks: 1 <= m <= {M}")
print(f"A328151 minima checked: {len(verified_n)} indices n = {verified_n}")
print(f"A093195 minima checked: {len(verified_k)} indices k = {verified_k}")
print(f"A328151(0..14) = {first15}")
```

## 4. Verdict

**Proved, unconditionally, for all stated indices.** The decisive ingredient is the explicit bijection $D(2m^2)=D(m^2)=T(m)$. It establishes the odd-index formula directly and removes the only obstruction in the even-index formula: a minimizer for a given number of distinct-square representations cannot be a diagonal case $2m^2$, since $m^2$ has the same number of such representations and is smaller. The result is elementary and uses known sequence definitions, not a new number-theoretic technique.
