# A365970: Status and Proof Attempt for Unimodal Columns and Antidiagonals

**Date:** 12 November 2026 **Sequence:** [OEIS A365970](https://oeis.org/A365970) **Verdict:** Open, as far as the sources searched establish. Unimodality is proved here for the complete columns $k=0,1,2$. No proof or counterexample for all columns and antidiagonals was found.

## 1. Status

### The conjecture

OEIS A365970, introduced by Peter Kagey in September 2023, defines $T(n,k)$ as the number of free, face-connected polyforms in the tetrahedral-octahedral honeycomb containing $n$ cells, exactly $k$ of which are octahedra.

The entry explicitly states:

> Conjecture: Columns and antidiagonals are unimodal.

The comment remains in the live entry.

A finite nonnegative sequence $(a_0,\ldots,a_m)$ is *unimodal* if there is an index $j$ such that

$$
a_0\le\cdots\le a_j\ge\cdots\ge a_m.
$$

Equal adjacent values are allowed.

The two precise assertions are:

- **Columns:** For every fixed $k\ge0$, $(T(n,k))_{n=k}^{\infty}$ is unimodal.
- **Antidiagonals:** For every $s\ge0$, the finite sequence \[ \bigl(T(n,s-n)\bigr)_{n=\lceil s/2\rceil}^{s} \] is unimodal.

These definitions use the triangular array's ordinary row and column coordinates. Trailing zeros in a column do not affect unimodality.

## 2. Proof and partial results

### Theorem 1. The columns $k=0,1,2$ are unimodal for all $n$.

This is an unconditional mathematical result. The argument uses the geometry of the honeycomb and finite group actions.

#### Preliminary lemma: finite support

Every tetrahedral face is adjacent to an octahedron, and every octahedral face is adjacent to a tetrahedron. Thus the face-adjacency graph of the honeycomb is bipartite.

Every octahedron has eight faces, so a connected polyform with $k$ octahedra and $n$ cells has at most $8k$ adjacency edges incident to octahedra.

A connected graph on $n$ vertices has at least $n-1$ edges. Therefore

$$
n-1\le8k,
$$

giving

$$
\boxed{T(n,k)=0\quad\text{when }n>8k+1.}
$$

This proves that **every fixed column has finite support**, although it does not by itself prove unimodality.

#### Column $k=0$

Two tetrahedra cannot share a face in the honeycomb. Therefore a connected polyform containing only tetrahedra has at most one cell.

Including the empty polyform,

$$
T(n,0)=
\begin{cases}
1,&n=0,1,\\
0,&n\ge2.
\end{cases}
$$

The column is

$$
1,1,0,0,\ldots
$$

and is unimodal. $\square$

#### Column $k=1$

A polyform with exactly one octahedron consists of that octahedron and a subset of the eight tetrahedra attached to its faces.

Every subset gives a connected polyform. Two such polyforms are equivalent precisely when their subsets are related by a symmetry of the octahedron.

The symmetry group is the full octahedral group of order 48. Its action on the eight octahedral faces is equivalent to its action on the eight vertices of a cube.

By Burnside's lemma, the generating polynomial for the numbers of orbits of subsets of the eight faces is

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_1(x)=\frac1{48}\bigl[&
(1+x)^8\\
&+6(1+x)^4(1+x^2)^2\\
&+8(1+x)^2(1+x^3)^2\\
&+13(1+x^2)^4\\
&+8(1+x^2)(1+x^6)\\
&+12(1+x^4)^2\bigr].
\end{aligned}
$$

Here the summands correspond to the six cycle types of the 48 symmetries acting on the eight faces. Expanding yields

$$
\boxed{
F_1(x)=1+x+3x^2+3x^3+6x^4+
3x^5+3x^6+x^7+x^8.
}
$$

For $1\le n\le9$,

$$
T(n,1)=[x^{n-1}]F_1(x),
$$

and $T(n,1)=0$ for $n>9$.

Thus the entire column is

$$
\boxed{1,1,3,3,6,3,3,1,1,0,0,\ldots}.
$$

It increases weakly to 6 and then decreases weakly. Hence column $k=1$ is unimodal. $\square$

#### Column $k=2$

This column also admits a complete finite enumeration.

Use the face-centered-cubic lattice realization of the honeycomb in which octahedron centers are integer triples with odd coordinate sum. Tetrahedron centers are half-integer triples.

An octahedron centered at $O=(a,b,c)$ has eight adjacent tetrahedra with centers

$$
O+\frac12(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1).
$$

Consider a connected polyform with exactly two octahedra.

Because tetrahedra never share faces, the two octahedra must have a common adjacent tetrahedron. Up to a symmetry of the honeycomb, the two octahedron centers can therefore be fixed at

$$
O=(0,0,1),\qquad P=(1,1,1).
$$

These octahedra have eight tetrahedral neighbors each, with exactly two neighbors in common.

Consequently:

- There are 14 distinct candidate tetrahedra.
- A connected polyform must contain at least one of the two common tetrahedra.
- The stabilizer of the unordered pair of octahedra has order 8.
- Two selections represent the same free polyform exactly when they belong to the same orbit under that stabilizer.

This reduces the enumeration to subsets of a 14-element set.

The cycle types of the eight pair-preserving symmetries are:

| Multiplicity | Cycles on 14 tetrahedra | Cycles on 12 noncommon tetrahedra |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $1^{14}$ | $1^{12}$ |
| 1 | $1^6 2^4$ | $1^4 2^4$ |
| 2 | $1^2 2^6$ | $2^6$ |
| 4 | $2^7$ | $2^6$ |

The notation $1^a2^b$ denotes $a$ fixed points and $b$ cycles of length two.

For each symmetry, subtracting the subsets containing neither common tetrahedron removes precisely the disconnected selections.

Burnside's lemma therefore gives the generating polynomial

$$
\boxed{
F_2(x)=
\frac{
((1+x)^2-1)
\left((1+x)^{12}+(1+x)^4(1+x^2)^4
+2(1+x^2)^6\right)
+4x^2(1+x^2)^6
}{8}.
}
$$

Its coefficients determine $T(n,2)$ for every $n\ge2$:

$$
T(n,2)=[x^{n-2}]F_2(x).
$$

Expanding gives the **complete column**:

| $n$ | $T(n,2)$ |
| --- | --- |
| 2 | 0 |
| 3 | 1 |
| 4 | 5 |
| 5 | 24 |
| 6 | 74 |
| 7 | 169 |
| 8 | 285 |
| 9 | 356 |
| 10 | 344 |
| 11 | 243 |
| 12 | 137 |
| 13 | 52 |
| 14 | 18 |
| 15 | 3 |
| 16 | 1 |

For $n>16$, the count is zero: only the 14 tetrahedra adjacent to the two octahedra can appear.

The coefficients increase strictly from $n=2$ to $n=9$, then decrease strictly through $n=16$, followed by zeros.

Thus column $k=2$ is unimodal. $\square$

The complete columns $k=1,2$ are consistent with all available inline OEIS values. The values for $k=2$ beyond $n=10$ are derived independently by the finite-orbit calculation above, rather than taken from the inaccessible b-file.

### Scope of the proof

The argument establishes three complete infinite-index assertions about columns, rather than merely checking a finite prefix.

It does not prove unimodality for columns $k\ge3$, nor for arbitrary antidiagonals.

The general conjecture therefore remains unresolved by this work.

## 3. Computational verification

I implemented and executed a Python 3 program that independently constructs the relevant finite group actions and enumerates subset orbits.

The program also checks the eleven complete rows explicitly displayed in OEIS A365970, including column prefixes and complete antidiagonals.

1. Reads all 66 displayed values for $0\le n\le10$.
2. Tests every available column prefix $0\le k\le10$.

4. Constructs all 48 signed coordinate permutations for $k=1$.
5. Constructs the eight transformations preserving the unordered octahedron pair for $k=2$.

## 4. Verdict

**The full A365970 conjecture remains apparently open as of 12 November 2026.** The live OEIS entry continues to state it as a conjecture, and the literature searches identified neither a complete proof nor a counterexample. This report proves the complete columns $k=0,1,2$ unimodal using the honeycomb's bipartite structure and Burnside orbit enumeration. It also proves the general finite-support bound $T(n,k)=0$ for $n>8k+1$, and computationally verifies all available inline column prefixes and complete antidiagonals. The mathematical confidence in the three-column result is high because the group actions and finite counts are explicit and independently reproducible. The novelty of the proof relative to unpublished or inaccessible work is uncertain; the resulting initial values are already recorded in OEIS, and this report makes no claim of research priority.
