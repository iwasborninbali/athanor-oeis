# OEIS A000978 — Status and proof attempt for the Wagstaff Lucas–Lehmer conjecture

**Investigation date:** 12 November 2026 **Target:** Tony Reix's conjecture, recorded in OEIS A000978 on 3 September 2007 **Overall finding:** The necessary direction is proved; the sufficient direction remains open in the literature located. No counterexample was found in the computational range tested.

## 1. Status and literature

### 1.1 Precise statement

For each prime $p>3$, define

$$
N_p=2^p+1,\qquad W_p=\frac{2^p+1}{3},
$$

and let

$$
f(x)=x^2-2.
$$

Choose either starting value

$$
s_0=\frac32\quad\text{or}\quad s_0=\frac14,
$$

interpreting fractions as modular inverses, and iterate

$$
s_{i+1}=f(s_i)\pmod{N_p}.
$$

The conjecture recorded in A000978 asserts

$$
\boxed{
W_p\text{ is prime}
\iff
s_{p-1}\equiv s_0\pmod{W_p}.
}
$$

Since $W_p\mid N_p$, computing the recurrence modulo $N_p$ and reducing at the end modulo $W_p$ gives exactly the same result as iterating directly modulo $W_p$.

The two seeds must initially be treated as separate formulations. They satisfy $f(3/2)=1/4$, but that identity alone does not establish equivalence of the two tests for composite moduli.

### 1.2 Findings in the literature

**Tony Reix (2007), OEIS A000978.** The entry presents the equivalence explicitly as a conjecture. No proof is attached.

**Tony Reix, *Mathématiques*, personal research website (2009 onward).** Reix discusses the modified Lucas–Lehmer cycle approach, distinguishes the proved necessary condition from conjectural sufficiency, and links to proofs by Anton Vrba and Robert Gerbicz. His longer formulation also requires that no earlier iterate return to the seed modulo $N_p$. We examine that extra condition in Section 2. Blog

**Tony Reix (14 January 2022), *Preuve de primalité des nombres de Wagstaff*, MathemaTeX forum.** Reix explicitly identifies the missing direction:

> Il reste maintenant à prouver la "sufficiency"

Translation: *It remains to prove sufficiency.*

**Djurre Germ Sikkema (2024), *Probable primality testing for Wagstaff primes*, bachelor's thesis, University of Groningen.** This work proves properties of Lucas–Lehmer-type probable-prime tests, including the Vrba and Gerbicz variants. Its abstract distinguishes these tests from a criterion valid in both directions.

**Alexey Dolotov (2026), *Three Brillhart-Lehmer-Selfridge primality proofs for Wagstaff numbers*, arXiv:2605.18555, submitted 18 May 2026.** The abstract gives particularly decisive evidence:

> Chebyshev/Lucas-type tests, while available as compositeness criteria, remain conjectural on the sufficiency side.

Dolotov supplies independent, unconditional primality proofs for three individual Wagstaff numbers, not a general proof of the Reix criterion.

**Additional sources.** The LLR software documentation also describes the Vrba–Reix algorithm as a necessary condition and probable-prime test. MathWorld's October 2026 Wagstaff-prime summary continues to distinguish such conjectural criteria from unconditional primality proofs.

## 2. Proof and partial results

### 2.1 The necessary implication — proved

**Theorem (known result; independent elementary derivation below).** For every prime $p>3$,

$$
W_p\text{ prime}
\implies
f^{p-1}(3/2)\equiv3/2\pmod{W_p}.
$$

The same implication holds with starting value $1/4$.

**Proof.**

Assume $q=W_p$ is prime.

First, because $p\ge5$,

$$
q=\frac{2^p+1}{3}\equiv3\pmod8.
$$

Also, $p\equiv1$ or $2\pmod3$. Since $2^3\equiv1\pmod7$, we obtain

$$
q\equiv1\text{ or }4\pmod7.
$$

By the supplementary laws of quadratic reciprocity,

$$
\left(\frac{-2}{q}\right)=1,
\qquad
\left(\frac{-7}{q}\right)
=\left(\frac{q}{7}\right)=1.
$$

Consequently, both $-1/2$ and $7/2$ are quadratic residues modulo $q$.

Choose $a,b\in\mathbb F_q$ with

$$
a^2=\frac72,\qquad b^2=-\frac12.
$$

Define

$$
u=\frac{a+b}{2},\qquad
v=\frac{a-b}{2}.
$$

Then

$$
uv=\frac{a^2-b^2}{4}=1
$$

and

$$
u^2+v^2=\frac{a^2+b^2}{2}=\frac32.
$$

Thus, setting $t=u^2$, we have $v^2=t^{-1}$ and

$$
\frac32=t+t^{-1}.
$$

The elementary identity

$$
(t+t^{-1})^2-2=t^2+t^{-2}
$$

gives, by induction,

$$
f^j(3/2)=t^{2^j}+t^{-2^j}.
$$

Now observe that

$$
\frac{q-1}{2}=\frac{2^{p-1}-1}{3}.
$$

By Fermat's little theorem, $u^{q-1}=1$. Therefore

$$
t^{(q-1)/2}=u^{q-1}=1.
$$

Since $2^{p-1}-1=3(q-1)/2$,

$$
t^{2^{p-1}-1}=1.
$$

Hence

$$
t^{2^{p-1}}=t,
\qquad
t^{-2^{p-1}}=t^{-1},
$$

and so

$$
f^{p-1}(3/2)
=t^{2^{p-1}}+t^{-2^{p-1}}
=t+t^{-1}
=\frac32\pmod q.
$$

This proves the assertion for the first seed.

For the second seed, note that

$$
f(3/2)=\frac14.
$$

Because iterates of $f$ commute with $f$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
f^{p-1}(1/4)
&=f^{p-1}(f(3/2))\\
&=f(f^{p-1}(3/2))\\
&=f(3/2)=\frac14\pmod q.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Q.E.D.**

This is a proof of the already-known necessary direction, not a claim of a new theorem. Reix's website explicitly credits earlier proofs of the necessary direction to Vrba and Gerbicz. Blog

### 2.2 A general relationship between the two starting values

**Proved.** For every odd modulus $m$, and any nonnegative integer $r$,

$$
f^r(3/2)\equiv3/2\pmod m
\implies
f^r(1/4)\equiv1/4\pmod m.
$$

This follows immediately by applying $f$ to the first congruence.

Thus, the $3/2$ test implies the $1/4$ test for every admissible $W_p$, whether prime or composite.

The reverse implication has not been established here. In particular, the two tests should not be described as universally equivalent merely because their observed results agree.

### 2.3 The additional condition in Reix's longer formulation

Reix's research page gives a version requiring no earlier return to the seed modulo $N_p$. Blog

**Proved observation:** This extra condition is automatic for either seed.

Indeed, $3\mid N_p$, and reduction of the recurrence modulo 3 gives

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
s_0=3/2:&0\longmapsto1\longmapsto2\longmapsto2\longmapsto\cdots,\\
s_0=1/4:&1\longmapsto2\longmapsto2\longmapsto\cdots.
\end{array}
$$

Therefore, for either starting value,

$$
s_i\not\equiv s_0\pmod3
\qquad(i\ge1).
$$

It follows that

$$
s_i\not\equiv s_0\pmod{N_p}
\qquad(i\ge1).
$$

Thus the prohibition on earlier returns modulo $N_p$ imposes no additional restriction.

This is an elementary observation about the published formulation. I found no prior explicit statement of it in the sources inspected, but cannot establish novelty from that absence.

### 2.4 The unresolved direction

The outstanding assertion is

$$
\boxed{
f^{p-1}(3/2)\equiv3/2\pmod{W_p}
\implies W_p\text{ is prime},
}
$$

and analogously for the $1/4$ seed.

One reason the preceding proof cannot simply be reversed is that it invokes Fermat's little theorem in the field $\mathbb F_{W_p}$, which requires $W_p$ to be prime.

For composite $W_p$, the ring has zero divisors, and the same cycle congruence does not immediately imply that the number has no nontrivial factors.

**Result:** No proof of sufficiency or counterexample was obtained. The problem remains the Vrba–Reix sufficiency conjecture, rather than being reduced here to another named open problem.

## 3. Computational verification

### 3.1 Program

The following Python 3 program was executed with SymPy.

It tests both starting values, verifies that iteration modulo $N_p$ and $W_p$ yields the same verdict, checks the one-way seed implication, and checks the quadratic-residue lemmas whenever `sympy.isprime` returns `True`.

```python

from sympy import primerange, isprime

def cycle(p, numerator, denominator, modulus):
    N = (1 << p) + 1
    W = N // 3
    m = N if modulus == 'N' else W
    s0 = numerator * pow(denominator, -1, m) % m
    s = s0
    for _ in range(p - 1):
        s = (s*s - 2) % m
    return (s - s0) % W == 0

rows = []
for p in primerange(5, 1001):
    W = ((1 << p) + 1) // 3
    a = cycle(p, 3, 2, 'N')
    b = cycle(p, 1, 4, 'N')

    assert a == cycle(p, 3, 2, 'W')
    assert b == cycle(p, 1, 4, 'W')
    assert not a or b  # f(3/2) = 1/4

    prime = isprime(W)
    if prime:
        assert pow(-2, (W-1)//2, W) == 1
        assert pow(-7, (W-1)//2, W) == 1

    rows.append((p, prime, a, b))

passing_a = [p for p, _, a, _ in rows if a]
passing_b = [p for p, _, _, b in rows if b]
seed_disagreements = [
    p for p, _, a, b in rows if a != b
]
primality_disagreements = [
    p for p, prime, a, b in rows
    if prime != a or prime != b
]
small = [row for row in rows if row[0] <= 61]

print('prime exponents checked:', len(rows))
print('exponent range:', rows[0][0], 'to', rows[-1][0])
print('passes 3/2:', len(passing_a))
print('passes 1/4:', len(passing_b))
print('passing exponents:', passing_a)
print('seed disagreements:', seed_disagreements)
print('disagreements with sympy.isprime:', primality_disagreements)
print('64-bit exact subset: exponents:', len(small), 'passes:', sum(a for _, _, a, _ in small))
print('64-bit exact subset mismatches:', [p for p, prime, a, b in small if prime != a or prime != b])
```

### 3.3 Interpretation and limits

Computed results

- All 166 prime exponents $5\le p\le997$ were checked using exact integer modular arithmetic.
- The recurrence passes for exactly 19 exponents, identical for both seeds.
- No disagreements between seeds, between the two iteration moduli, or with `sympy.isprime` were observed.
- The checks of the quadratic-residue lemmas passed for every candidate classified prime.

**Important qualification:** SymPy's primality testing is deterministic for inputs below $2^{64}$, but uses a strong Baillie–PSW probable-primality test for sufficiently large inputs. Thus, the 16-exponent subset $5\le p\le61$ gives an exact computational comparison with established primality decisions. For larger exponents, the comparison against `sympy.isprime` is not an unconditional primality certificate.

The modular recurrence calculations themselves are exact throughout the entire range.

The 19 passing exponents agree with the known A000978 exponents below 1000.

No composite Wagstaff number passing either cycle test was found in this range. This is evidence consistent with the conjecture, not a proof of sufficiency.
