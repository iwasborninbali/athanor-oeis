# Report: OEIS A000668 — Uniqueness of the prime divisor-sum collision at 31

**Date:** 12 November 2026 **Task:** [A000668-sigma-collision-31](https://github.com/iwasborninbali/athanor-oeis/blob/main/tasks/2026-11-12-A000668-sigma-collision-31/TASK.md)

**Verdict: OPEN.** The conjecture is a special case of the Goormaghtigh conjecture. No counterexample or unconditional proof of global uniqueness was found. A published 2024 computational result establishes uniqueness for every prime $p<10^{700}$.

## 1. Status

### 1.1 The conjecture

The OEIS entry [A000668](https://oeis.org/A000668) contains the following conjecture, attributed to Jaroslav Krizek, 19 January 2017:

> Conjecture: 31 is the only prime p such that p = sigma(x) = sigma(y) for distinct numbers x and y.

Here $\sigma(n)$ is the sum of the positive divisors of $n$.

The statement is mathematically well-posed, but it is not a conjecture exclusively about Mersenne primes. For example, $\sigma(9)=13$ is prime without being Mersenne.

### 1.2 A purported proof in OEIS is conditional

A particularly important finding is in [OEIS A023194](https://oeis.org/A023194), the sequence of integers whose divisor sums are prime.

On 17 August 2026, Bernard Schott added a comment asserting the uniqueness of 31 and beginning:

> Proof: according to the Goormaghtigh conjecture

This is not an unconditional proof: it invokes precisely a conjecture that remains unproved.

Related OEIS entries provide consistent evidence:

- [A065764](https://oeis.org/A065764) (17 January 2021, Bernard Schott) calls 31 the only *known* prime occurring twice among divisor sums of squares.
- [A119598](https://oeis.org/A119598) discusses the two known Goormaghtigh solutions, 31 and 8191.
- [A281622](https://oeis.org/A281622) restates the conjecture for shifted arguments.

### 1.3 Published mathematical results

The most directly relevant result is:

**Jon Grantham (2024), “No New Goormaghtigh Primes up to $10^{700}$,” *INTEGERS*, volume 24, article A98, published 25 October 2024.** DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.13992627](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.13992627); preprint: [arXiv:2410.03677](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03677).

Its abstract states:

> We show that there are no other Goormaghtigh primes less than $10^{700}$.

This is stronger than a search limited to prime bases: Grantham excludes additional prime-valued repunit collisions below that bound even when the bases are not prime.

## 2. Proof attempt and strongest partial result

### 2.1 Reduction to prime powers — proved

**Lemma.** If $\sigma(n)$ is prime, then $n=q^{r-1}$, where $q$ and $r$ are primes.

**Proof.**

If $n$ has at least two distinct prime factors, write $n=ab$ with $a,b>1$ and $\gcd(a,b)=1$. Multiplicativity gives

$$
\sigma(n)=\sigma(a)\sigma(b).
$$

Since both factors exceed 1, $\sigma(n)$ is composite.

Hence $n=q^k$ for a prime $q$, and

$$
\sigma(q^k)=1+q+\cdots+q^k
=\frac{q^{k+1}-1}{q-1}.
$$

If $k+1=uv$, where $u,v>1$, then

$$
\frac{q^{uv}-1}{q-1}
=
\frac{q^u-1}{q-1}
\left(1+q^u+\cdots+q^{u(v-1)}\right).
$$

Both factors exceed 1. Thus $\sigma(q^k)$ could not be prime.

Therefore $k+1=r$ is prime. $\square$

This lemma is established material, also explained in OEIS A023194 and the older PrimePuzzles discussion of this exact problem. It is **not a new result**.

### 2.2 Exact reduction to Goormaghtigh's equation — proved

Define the repunit polynomial

$$
R_r(q)=\frac{q^r-1}{q-1}
=1+q+\cdots+q^{r-1}.
$$

For a collision $\sigma(x)=\sigma(y)=P$ with $P$ prime, the lemma gives

$$
x=q^{r-1},\qquad y=s^{t-1},
$$

where $q,s,r,t$ are primes, so

$$
\boxed{
P=\frac{q^r-1}{q-1}
 =\frac{s^t-1}{s-1}.
}
$$

The bases $q,s$ must differ: for a fixed base, the repunit increases strictly with its length. The exponents $r,t$ must also differ: for a fixed length, the repunit increases strictly with its base.

The length-two case cannot yield a collision, since $R_2(q)=q+1$ is prime only when $q=2$, giving $P=3$ and $x=2$. Thus a collision must have distinct prime lengths $r,t\ge3$.

Conversely, every solution of the displayed equation with distinct prime bases, distinct prime lengths at least 3, and prime $P$, produces a divisor-sum collision by taking $x=q^{r-1}$ and $y=s^{t-1}$.

Therefore:

**The OEIS conjecture is exactly the prime-base, prime-value restriction of the Goormaghtigh conjecture.**

The full Goormaghtigh conjecture predicts that the only nontrivial repunit collisions are

$$
31=R_5(2)=R_3(5)
$$

and

$$
8191=R_{13}(2)=R_3(90).
$$

The first gives

$$
\boxed{\sigma(16)=\sigma(25)=31.}
$$

The second does not give a second divisor-sum collision, because 90 is composite and a prime divisor sum requires a prime-power argument.

Consequently, the full Goormaghtigh conjecture implies the OEIS conjecture. **The converse has not been established**: the restricted claim could hold even if there were other Goormaghtigh solutions involving composite bases or composite common values.

### 2.3 Strong unconditional partial result — known

Grantham (2024), building on Bennett–Garbuz–Marten (2020) and Bennett–Gherga–Kreso (2020), gives the following constraints on any hypothetical new divisor-sum collision:

1. Its prime value must satisfy $P\ge10^{700}$.
2. Both prime repunit lengths must be at least 53.
3. The larger of its two prime bases must exceed $10^5$.

For the second assertion, note that odd primes $r,t$ satisfy

$$
\gcd(r-1,t-1)\ge2.
$$

The small-parameter theorem quoted in Grantham's paper rules out new solutions with the smaller length at most 50. The next possible prime length is 53. The bound on the larger base follows from the other cited small-parameter result.

These are published results, **not new theorems from this investigation**. They provide a rigorous reduction of the remaining search space.

## 3. Computational check

I implemented an independent Python check that enumerates all possible $n\le10^{12}$ having prime divisor sum, using the proved prime-power characterization. A separate direct divisor-sum sieve checks the smaller range $n\le100{,}000$.

Python 3.13.5 · SymPy 1.14.0

```python

from collections import defaultdict
from math import isqrt
from sympy import isprime, primerange

LIMIT = 10**12
SMALL = 100000

hits = defaultdict(list)
hits[3].append(2)
candidates = 1

for q in primerange(2, isqrt(LIMIT) + 1):
    n, length = q*q, 3
    while n <= LIMIT:
        if isprime(length):
            candidates += 1
            s = (q*n - 1) // (q-1)
            if isprime(s):
                hits[s].append(n)
        n *= q
        length += 1

collisions = sorted(
    (p, sorted(ns))
    for p, ns in hits.items()
    if len(ns) > 1
)

print(f"LIMIT={LIMIT}")
print(f"prime-power candidates={candidates}")
print(f"n with prime sigma(n)={sum(map(len, hits.values()))}")
print(f"distinct prime sigma-values={len(hits)}")
print(f"collisions={collisions}")

sigma = [0] * (SMALL+1)
for d in range(1, SMALL+1):
    for k in range(d, SMALL+1, d):
        sigma[k] += d

brute = {
    n for n in range(1, SMALL+1)
    if isprime(sigma[n])
}
structured = {
    n for ns in hits.values()
    for n in ns if n <= SMALL
}

print(f"SMALL={SMALL}")
print(f"sieve prime-sigma n count={len(brute)}")
print(f"candidate/sieve agreement={brute == structured}")

assert collisions == [(31, [16, 25])]
assert brute == structured
```

## 4. Verdict

**Open, but reduced to a named open Diophantine problem.** The claim that 31 is the only prime occurring as the divisor sum of two distinct positive integers has not been proved unconditionally in the sources checked. It is precisely the prime-base, prime-value specialization of the Goormaghtigh conjecture; the unrestricted Goormaghtigh conjecture would imply it, but the converse is not established. The August 2026 OEIS A023194 comment labelled as a proof is conditional because it assumes that conjecture. Grantham's published 2024 work already excludes every other candidate below $10^{700}$. My independent computation confirms the result for arguments through $10^{12}$, but supplies no new global theorem or stronger bound. Confidence is high in the mathematical reduction and reproducible computation, and qualified regarding completeness of the literature search. **An appropriate OEIS comment would be:** “The uniqueness of $31=\sigma(16)=\sigma(25)$ remains conjectural. It follows from the Goormaghtigh conjecture. By Grantham (*INTEGERS* 24 (2024), A98), no other prime divisor-sum collision occurs with common value below $10^{700}$. The purported proof in A023194 relies on the unproved Goormaghtigh conjecture and should be described as conditional.”
