# OEIS A064591 — Status and proof attempt for Lagneau's reciprocal-divisor conjecture

**Investigation date:** 12 November 2026 **Status:** Open; reduced to two established open problems.

**Main result (proved):** The integers satisfying Michel Lagneau's reciprocal-divisor equation are exactly four times the ordinary perfect numbers, whether even or odd.

Consequently, Lagneau's conjecture is equivalent to the conjunction of:

1. Ligh and Wall's 1987 conjecture that every nonunitary perfect number is four times an even perfect number.
2. The nonexistence of odd perfect numbers.

No unconditional counterexample or complete proof was obtained. The reduction below is exact, not computational or heuristic.

## 1. Status and literature

### Original statement

For $n\geq1$, define

$$
s_0(n)=\sum_{\substack{d\mid n\\2\mid d}}\frac1d,
\qquad
s_1(n)=\sum_{\substack{d\mid n\\2\nmid d}}\frac1d.
$$

Write

$$
\sigma(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d,
\qquad
\sigma^*(n)=\sum_{d\parallel n}d,
$$

where $d\parallel n$ means $\gcd(d,n/d)=1$.

Michel Lagneau's conjecture, recorded in [OEIS A064591](https://oeis.org/A064591) on 21 January 2015, is

$$
\boxed{\sigma(n)-\sigma^*(n)=n
\iff 2s_0(n)-s_1(n)=1.}
$$

The OEIS entry continues to label this as a conjecture.

### Earlier work

**Ligh and Wall (1987).** Steve Ligh and Charles R. Wall, *Functions of Non-Unitary Divisors*, *The Fibonacci Quarterly* **25**(4), 333–338 (1987), p. 334, Conjecture 1:

> An integer is non-unitary perfect if and only if it is 4 times an even perfect number.

Their Theorem 1 proves that every integer $2^{p+1}(2^p-1)$, with $2^p-1$ prime, is nonunitary perfect. Their computation found no exceptions below $10^6$. The converse was explicitly conjectural.

**Hagis (1990).** Peter Hagis, Jr., *Odd Nonunitary Perfect Numbers*, *The Fibonacci Quarterly* **28**(1), 11–15 (1990), Proposition 2:

> If n is an odd nonunitary perfect number, then n > 10^15.

Hagis also proved structural restrictions on hypothetical odd nonunitary perfect numbers. His results do not establish their nonexistence.

**Zelinsky (2024).** Joshua Zelinsky, answer to [MathOverflow question 306760](https://mathoverflow.net/a/463487), 4 February 2024 (edited 5 February), begins:

> Not a complete answer

He proves the Ligh–Wall conjecture for nonunitary perfect numbers of the form $2^k p^a$, with $p$ an odd prime. This partial result is also referenced in the current OEIS entry.

**Odd perfect numbers.** The OEIS Wiki's article *Perfect numbers* states:

> It is not known whether odd perfect numbers exist or not!

MathWorld also identifies their existence as unresolved in its 2026 account.

## 2. Proof and reduction

### Theorem 1 — Complete classification of the reciprocal equation

**Proved.** For every positive integer $n$,

$$
\boxed{
2s_0(n)-s_1(n)=1
\iff
n=4P\text{ for some ordinary perfect number }P.
}
$$

Here $P$ may be even or odd.

**Proof.**

If $n$ is odd, then $s_0(n)=0$, while $s_1(n)>0$. Therefore the equation cannot hold.

Suppose $n$ is even and write

$$
n=2^a m,\qquad a\geq1,\quad m\text{ odd}.
$$

The odd divisors of $n$ are precisely the divisors of $m$, so

$$
s_1(n)=\frac{\sigma(m)}m.
$$

The even divisors are $2^j d$, where $1\leq j\leq a$ and $d\mid m$. Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
s_0(n)
&=\frac{\sigma(m)}m\sum_{j=1}^a2^{-j}\\
&=(1-2^{-a})\frac{\sigma(m)}m.
\end{aligned}
$$

Consequently,

$$
2s_0(n)-s_1(n)
=(1-2^{1-a})\frac{\sigma(m)}m.
$$

The required equation is therefore equivalent to

$$
(2^a-2)\sigma(m)=2^a m.
\tag{1}
$$

For $a=1$, the left-hand side is zero and the right-hand side is positive. Hence $a\geq2$.

Set

$$
t=2^{a-1}-1.
$$

Equation (1) becomes

$$
\boxed{t\sigma(m)=(t+1)m.}
\tag{2}
$$

There are two cases.

**Case A: $a=2$.**

Then $t=1$, so equation (2) gives

$$
\sigma(m)=2m.
$$

Thus $m$ is an odd perfect number.

Conversely, every odd perfect number $m$ satisfies equation (2) with $a=2$.

The solutions in this case are precisely

$$
n=4m,\qquad m\text{ odd perfect}.
$$

**Case B: $a\geq3$.**

Now $t=2^{a-1}-1\geq3$.

Since $\gcd(t,t+1)=1$, equation (2) implies

$$
t\mid m.
$$

Dividing equation (2) by $tm$ gives

$$
\frac{\sigma(m)}m=1+\frac1t.
\tag{3}
$$

But

$$
\frac{\sigma(m)}m=\sum_{d\mid m}\frac1d.
$$

Because $t\mid m$, both $1$ and $t$ divide $m$. Therefore

$$
\sum_{d\mid m}\frac1d\geq1+\frac1t,
$$

with equality only if $m$ has no divisors other than $1$ and $t$.

Equation (3) forces equality. Hence

$$
m=t,\qquad t\text{ prime}.
$$

It follows that

$$
n=2^a(2^{a-1}-1),
$$

where $2^{a-1}-1$ is prime.

Putting $p=a-1$, this is

$$
n=2^{p+1}(2^p-1),
$$

with $2^p-1$ prime.

By the Euclid–Euler characterization, these are precisely four times the even perfect numbers.

Combining cases A and B proves the theorem. $\square$

Equivalently, the entire solution set is

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\{n:2s_0(n)-s_1(n)=1\}
={}&\{4P:P\text{ even perfect}\}\\
&\cup\{4P:P\text{ odd perfect}\}.
\end{aligned}
}
$$

This classification is unconditional. It does not assume that odd perfect numbers exist or do not exist.

### Theorem 2 — Exact relationship with nonunitary perfection

**Proved.** For $n=2^a m$, where $a\geq1$ and $m$ is odd,

$$
\boxed{
\sigma(n)-\sigma^*(n)
=n(2s_0(n)-s_1(n))
+(2^a+1)\bigl(\sigma(m)-\sigma^*(m)\bigr).
}
\tag{4}
$$

**Proof.**

Multiplicativity gives

$$
\sigma(n)=(2^{a+1}-1)\sigma(m)
$$

and

$$
\sigma^*(n)=(2^a+1)\sigma^*(m).
$$

Subtracting and regrouping,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma(n)-\sigma^*(n)
={}&(2^a-2)\sigma(m)\\
&+(2^a+1)(\sigma(m)-\sigma^*(m)).
\end{aligned}
$$

From equation (1),

$$
n(2s_0(n)-s_1(n))=(2^a-2)\sigma(m).
$$

Substitution proves (4). $\square$

Since

$$
\sigma(m)-\sigma^*(m)\geq0,
$$

with equality exactly when $m$ is squarefree, equation (4) immediately yields:

**Corollary (proved).** For every even nonunitary perfect number $n=2^a m$,

$$
2s_0(n)-s_1(n)\leq1.
$$

Equality holds **if and only if the odd part $m$ is squarefree**.

In particular, every nonunitary perfect number with squarefree odd part satisfies Lagneau's equation and is four times an even perfect number.

If a nonunitary perfect number with nonsquarefree odd part exists, it violates Lagneau's conjecture.

### Theorem 3 — Exact logical status of the conjecture

Let $L$ denote Lagneau's conjecture, $W$ denote Ligh–Wall's conjecture, and $O$ denote the assertion that no odd perfect numbers exist.

Then

$$
\boxed{L\iff(W\land O).}
$$

**Proof.**

First suppose $L$ holds.

By Theorem 1, every nonunitary perfect number must be four times an ordinary perfect number.

We show that no odd perfect number can exist.

Suppose $m$ were odd perfect. It cannot be squarefree: if $m$ is a product of at least two distinct odd primes, then $4\mid\sigma(m)$, contradicting $\sigma(m)=2m$; if $m$ is prime, $\sigma(m)=m+1<2m$.

Therefore

$$
\sigma(m)-\sigma^*(m)>0.
$$

Putting $n=4m$, Theorem 1 gives $2s_0(n)-s_1(n)=1$, but equation (4) gives

$$
\sigma(4m)-\sigma^*(4m)
=4m+5(\sigma(m)-\sigma^*(m))
>4m.
$$

Thus $4m$ is not nonunitary perfect, contradicting $L$.

Hence $O$ must hold. Theorem 1 then implies that every nonunitary perfect number is four times an even perfect number. This is $W$.

Conversely, suppose $W$ and $O$ both hold.

By $O$ and Theorem 1, the reciprocal equation holds exactly for the integers that are four times even perfect numbers.

By $W$, those are exactly the nonunitary perfect numbers. The reverse inclusion of the even-perfect family is already proved by Ligh and Wall's Theorem 1 (1987).

Therefore $L$ follows.

This establishes the equivalence. $\square$

**Interpretation:** Lagneau's conjecture is not merely a reformulation of the definition of nonunitary perfection. It is a conjunction of the stronger classification conjecture of Ligh and Wall and the nonexistence of odd perfect numbers.

## 3. Computational verification

A Python 3 program was executed with exact integer arithmetic.

The complete executable program is available here:

[Download check_a064591.py](#file-0)

The program uses a smallest-prime-factor sieve to calculate $\sigma(n)$ and $\sigma^*(n)$ multiplicatively. Its principal comparison is:

```python
nonunitary = []
reciprocal = []

for n in range(1, N + 1):
    if sigma[n] - usigma[n] == n:
        nonunitary.append(n)

    if n % 2 == 0:
        a = (n & -n).bit_length() - 1
        m = n >> a

        assert (
            sigma[n] - usigma[n]
            == (2**a - 2)*sigma[m]
               + (2**a + 1)*(sigma[m] - usigma[m])
        )

        if (2**a - 2)*sigma[m] == n:
            reciprocal.append(n)
```

The full program additionally forms $s_0$ and $s_1$ independently by enumerating divisors and summing Python `Fraction` objects for $1\leq n\leq3000$.

## 4. Verdict

**Verdict — open, with an exact reduction.** Lagneau's 2015 conjecture is not currently established by the sources checked. The proved classification shows that $2s_0(n)-s_1(n)=1$ holds exactly when $n$ is four times an ordinary perfect number. Thus the conjecture is logically equivalent to Ligh and Wall's 1987 conjecture together with the nonexistence of odd perfect numbers. The identity in Theorem 2 additionally proves Lagneau's conjecture for nonunitary perfect numbers having squarefree odd part. Exact computation finds no discrepancy through $5\times10^6$. The reductions are elementary and fully checkable, but I claim no priority or novelty over unexamined literature. Confidence in the proofs and reported computation is high; confidence that the literature search is exhaustive is not.

Hence Lagneau's conjecture is equivalent to Ligh and Wall's conjecture that all nonunitary perfect numbers are four times even perfect numbers, together with the nonexistence of odd perfect numbers.
