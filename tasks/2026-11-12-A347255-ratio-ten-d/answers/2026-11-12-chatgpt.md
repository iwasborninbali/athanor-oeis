## 2. Proof and mathematical verification

**Status: PROVED (known result, independently reconstructed).**

We can verify the existing resolution directly, using the classical divergence of reciprocal primes in a reduced arithmetic progression.

### Step 1. Characterize the possible products

Let $S$ denote the set of integers representable as

$$
m=(10r+4)(10s+4),\qquad r,s\geq0.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
m=4(5r+2)(5s+2).
$$

Consequently,

$$
m\equiv16\pmod{20}.
$$

Thus $S$ is contained in the arithmetic progression $16\bmod20$, which has natural density $1/20$.

### Step 2. Establish an almost-everywhere converse

Suppose

$$
m=4u,\qquad u\equiv4\pmod5.
$$

Assume $u$ has a prime divisor $p\equiv2\pmod5$. Writing $u=pq$, we obtain

$$
q\equiv4\cdot2^{-1}\equiv2\pmod5.
$$

Therefore

$$
m=(2p)(2q),
$$

and both $2p$ and $2q$ end in 4. Hence $m\in S$.

We have proved the sufficient condition

$$
\boxed{
u\equiv4\pmod5,\quad
\exists p\mid u:\ p\text{ prime},\ p\equiv2\pmod5
\ \Longrightarrow\ 4u\in S.
}
$$

### Step 3. Show that the exceptions have density zero

Let $E$ be the set of positive integers with no prime divisor congruent to $2\bmod5$.

The classical prime-in-arithmetic-progressions result gives

$$
\sum_{\substack{p\text{ prime}\\p\equiv2\pmod5}}\frac1p
=\infty.
$$

This stronger form of Dirichlet's theorem is standard.

For any finite collection $P$ of primes congruent to $2\bmod5$, the Chinese remainder theorem shows that the proportion of integers divisible by none of them tends to

$$
\prod_{p\in P}\left(1-\frac1p\right).
$$

Since the reciprocal-prime sum diverges, this product tends to zero as $P$ grows.

It follows that

$$
\#\{u\leq X:u\in E\}=o(X).
$$

All numbers congruent to $16\bmod20$, except possibly those corresponding to $u\in E$, belong to $S$. Therefore

$$
\#(S\cap[1,X])=\frac X{20}+o(X).
$$

This verifies the density $1/20$ already recorded by Spezia in A347253.

### Step 4. Deduce the requested limit

Define

$$
A(X)=\#\{m\in S:m\leq X\}.
$$

By the preceding result,

$$
A(X)=\frac X{20}+o(X).
$$

The number of $n$-digit elements is

$$
a(n)=A(10^n-1)-A(10^{n-1}-1).
$$

Hence

$$
a(n)=\frac{10^n-10^{n-1}}{20}+o(10^n),
$$

so

$$
\boxed{a(n)\sim\frac9{20}\,10^{n-1}.}
$$

In particular, $a(n-1)>0$ for all sufficiently large $n$, and

$$
\frac{a(n)}{a(n-1)}
=
\frac{\frac9{20}10^{n-1}(1+o(1))}
{\frac9{20}10^{n-2}(1+o(1))}
\longrightarrow10.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a(n)}{a(n-1)}=10.
\quad\blacksquare}
$$

**Attribution:** This confirms the previously known asymptotic density and Spezia's published conclusion. The argument above is an independent reconstruction, not a claim of a new theorem.
