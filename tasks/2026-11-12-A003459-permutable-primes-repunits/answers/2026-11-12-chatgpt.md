## A precision issue

The literal indexed identity for *every* $n\ge23$ implicitly requires infinitely many repunit primes. Their infinitude is itself open.

The mathematically cleaner conjecture, which does not presuppose infinitude, is the structural statement above: **there are no non-repunit permutable primes greater than 991.**

These should be distinguished. Proving the structural conjecture would not establish that there are infinitely many repunit primes.

# 2. Proof attempt and strongest partial result

Write $P(N)$ for the assertion that every decimal permutation of the digits of $N$ is prime.

I obtain the following computationally certified consequence of the classical structural theorem.

**Partial result.** If a non-repunit permutable prime $N>991$ exists and $n$ is its decimal digit length, then

$$
\boxed{n\ge10^{4044}.}
$$

More precisely, $n$ must be divisible by an explicitly computable integer $L$ having 4045 decimal digits.

This is much stronger numerically than Richert's reported $6\times10^{175}$-digit bound. It is an application of Mavlo's existing primitive-root lemma, not a resolution of the conjecture. No claim of priority or conceptual novelty is made.

## 2.1. Structural reduction (known)

For a multidigit permutable prime, only the digits $1,3,7,9$ are possible: any even digit or a digit 5 can be moved to the units position.

The following classical observations use arithmetic modulo 7.

First, all four digits $1,3,7,9$ cannot occur simultaneously. Seven permutations of their rightmost positions produce seven different residues modulo 7, one of which is zero.

Second, three occurrences of a digit $a$ and two of a different digit $b$ are impossible. Put the five digits into the last five positions. Seven choices for the two positions occupied by $b$ give all residues modulo 7.

For seven or more digits, these observations leave, among cases with three distinct digits, only digit multisets of the form $a^{n-2}bc$.

To exclude these, consider a last-six-digit block $aaaaab$, with prefix $K$. Its permutations have the form

$$
10^6K+aR_6+(b-a)10^j,\qquad 0\le j<6.
$$

The six powers $10^j\pmod7$ are exactly the nonzero residues, and $R_6\equiv0\pmod7$. Avoiding a composite permutation therefore forces $K\equiv0\pmod7$.

Placing $b$ and $c$ alternately into the prefix forces two such prefixes to be divisible by 7, although their difference is $b-c$. Since distinct elements of $\{1,3,7,9\}$ differ by 2, 4, 6 or 8 in absolute value, this is impossible.

Thus, for $n\ge7$, any non-repunit permutable prime must have the multiset

$$
\boxed{a^{n-1}b,\quad a,b\in\{1,3,7,9\},\ a\ne b.}
$$

This is Mavlo's Theorem 1; the argument above follows the classical proof, not a new result.

## 2.2. A primitive-root divisibility lemma

Let

$$
B_{n,j}(a,b)=aR_n+(b-a)10^j,\qquad 0\le j<n.
$$

These are precisely the $n$ distinct placements of the exceptional digit $b$.

**Lemma (known, proved here).** Suppose $p>11$ is prime, $10$ is a primitive root modulo $p$, and $n>p-1$. If all $B_{n,j}(a,b)$ are prime, then

$$
p-1\mid n.
$$

**Proof.** Since $10$ is a primitive root modulo $p$,

$$
10^0,10^1,\ldots,10^{p-2}
$$

run through every nonzero residue modulo $p$.

Also $b-a\not\equiv0\pmod p$, since $|b-a|\le8$.

If $aR_n\not\equiv0\pmod p$, some $j\in\{0,\ldots,p-2\}$ must satisfy

$$
aR_n+(b-a)10^j\equiv0\pmod p.
$$

But $B_{n,j}$ has $n$ digits, so is larger than $p$ and would be composite. This contradicts the assumption.

Hence

$$
aR_n\equiv0\pmod p.
$$

Because $p>11$, $p\nmid a$, and therefore $R_n\equiv0\pmod p$. Consequently

$$
10^n=9R_n+1\equiv1\pmod p.
$$

The multiplicative order of 10 modulo $p$ is $p-1$. Thus $p-1\mid n$. $\square$

This is Mavlo's Lemma 5 / Slinko's Lemma 6 expressed directly in terms of the exceptional-digit position.

## 2.3. The starting modulus 11088

The exhaustive computation in Section 3 excludes every exceptional-digit class with $7\le n\le16$. The structural reduction excludes all other non-repunit classes for those lengths.

Thus any counterexample must have $n>16$.

Applying the lemma successively to $p=17,19,23,29$, for all of which 10 is a primitive root, gives

$$
16\mid n,\quad18\mid n,\quad22\mid n,\quad28\mid n.
$$

At each stage the established lower bound on $n$ makes the next application valid:

$$
n\ge32,\qquad n\ge144,\qquad n\ge1584.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{11088=\operatorname{lcm}(16,18,22,28)\mid n.}
$$

This independently recovers Mavlo's published Theorem 2.

## 2.4. Iterating the modulus

Suppose $L\mid n$ has already been established.

For any prime $p>11$ such that

$$
p-1<L,\qquad\operatorname{ord}_p(10)=p-1,
$$

we necessarily have $n\ge L>p-1$. The lemma gives $p-1\mid n$, so we may replace

$$
L\longleftarrow\operatorname{lcm}(L,p-1).
$$

Starting with $L=11088$, I processed primes $31\le p\le100000$ in increasing order and applied this rule whenever its hypotheses were satisfied.

The computation establishes that the final necessary divisor $L$ has 4045 decimal digits. Its first 20 digits are

`62846457655354596727`

and its final 20 digits are

`61300464495616000000`.

Therefore every hypothetical counterexample must have a digit length at least $10^{4044}$.

The argument is exhaustive for the stated finite set of primes. The large bound is a computer-assisted arithmetic consequence of a proved lemma, not an extrapolation from observing that small cases fail.

# 3. Computational verification

The following Python 3 program was executed using SymPy. It verifies the small permutable primes, exhausts the 120 exceptional-digit classes for lengths 7–16, produces Lucas primality certificates for $R_{19}$ and $R_{23}$, and reproduces the primitive-root modulus bound.

[Download the executed Python program](#file-1)

```

from itertools import combinations_with_replacement, permutations
from math import gcd, lcm, isqrt
from hashlib import sha256
from sympy import factorint, isprime, primerange

D = (1, 3, 7, 9)

# All digit multisets through six digits.
small = [2, 3, 5, 7]
for n in range(2, 7):
    for digits in combinations_with_replacement("1379", n):
        ps = {int("".join(t)) for t in permutations(digits)}
        if all(isprime(x) for x in ps):
            small.extend(ps)
small.sort()
print("absolute primes x < 10^6:", small)

# Every possible exceptional-digit class, n=7..16.
survivors = []
for n in range(7, 17):
    R = (10**n - 1) // 9
    for a in D:
        for b in D:
            if a != b and all(
                isprime(a*R + (b-a)*10**j)
                for j in range(n)
            ):
                survivors.append((n, a, b))
print("one-exception classes n=7..16 tested:",
      120, "survivors:", survivors)

# Lucas primality certificates.
def lucas_certificate(N):
    fac = factorint(N - 1)
    assert all(
        q > 1 and all(q % d for d in range(2, isqrt(q)+1))
        for q in fac
    )
    assert N-1 == __import__("math").prod(
        q**e for q, e in fac.items()
    )
    witnesses = []
    for q in sorted(fac):
        a = next(
            a for a in range(2, 100)
            if pow(a, N-1, N) == 1
            and gcd(pow(a, (N-1)//q, N)-1, N) == 1
        )
        witnesses.append((q, a))
    return witnesses

for n in (19, 23):
    N = (10**n - 1)//9
    print(f"Lucas certificate R_{n}:",
          lucas_certificate(N))

# Bootstrap the necessary digit-length divisor.
L = 11088
eligible = growth = 0
for p in primerange(31, 100001):
    if p-1 >= L:
        continue
    factors = factorint(p-1)
    if all(pow(10, (p-1)//q, p) != 1 for q in factors):
        eligible += 1
        newL = lcm(L, p-1)
        growth += (newL != L)
        L = newL

print("primitive-root primes used p<=100000:", eligible)
print("LCM increases:", growth)
print("necessary n multiple has decimal digits:", len(str(L)))
print("LCM first 20 digits:", str(L)[:20])
print("LCM last 20 digits:", str(L)[-20:])
print("LCM SHA256 (decimal):",
      sha256(str(L).encode()).hexdigest())
```
