# OEIS A094960 — Status and Proof Attempt

**Investigation date:** November 12, 2026 **Object:** OEIS A094960 — Bernoulli polynomials whose first derivative has integer coefficients.

## 1. Status and literature

**Verdict: The exact conjecture remains open in the sources located. Its asymptotic, or sufficiently-large-integer, version is already proved.**

The conjecture of Max Alekseyev (2012) states:

> For every integer $k>78$, there exists an odd prime $p$ such that the sum of the last two base-$p$ digits of $k$ is at least $p$.

The condition is unambiguous if leading zero digits are permitted. Writing

$$
k\bmod p^2=ap+b,\qquad 0\le a,b<p,
$$

the assertion is

$$
\boxed{\forall k\ge79,\ \exists p\ge3\text{ prime}:
\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor\bmod p+
(k\bmod p)\ge p.}
$$

The following distinctions are essential.

| Claim | Status |
| --- | --- |
| A094960 is finite | **Proved**, Kellner (2024) |
| Its complete set is $\{1,2,4,6,10,12,28,30,36,60\}$ | Open |
| The last-two-digit conjecture holds for all sufficiently large $k$ | **Proved**, via the 2018 asymptotic theorem |
| The conjecture holds for every $k>78$ | Open in the located literature |

### Decisive sources

**Bernd C. Kellner (2024).** *On the Finiteness of Bernoulli Polynomials Whose Derivative Has Only Integral Coefficients*, *Journal of Integer Sequences* 27, Article 24.2.8, published February 19, 2024.

The abstract states:

> “We then show that the number of those derivatives is indeed finite.”

Theorem 5 establishes that any additional A094960 member exceeds $10^7$. However, the paper explicitly states:

> “it is still an open task to establish Conjectures 1(ii) and 2(i).”

Here Conjecture 1(ii) is the exact completeness of A094960, while Conjecture 2(i) concerns the existence of a prime $p>\sqrt{k}$ satisfying a base-$p$ digit-sum inequality for all $k>192$.

**Olivier Bordellès, Florian Luca, Pieter Moree, and Igor E. Shparlinski (2018).** *Denominators of Bernoulli polynomials*, *Mathematika* 64, 519–541, DOI: [10.1112/S0025579318000153](https://doi.org/10.1112/S0025579318000153).

As quoted and applied in Kellner (2024), Corollary 1.6 establishes the asymptotic

$$
\#\{p>\sqrt{k}:s_p(k)\ge p\}
\sim \frac{2\sqrt{k}}{\log k},
$$

where $s_p(k)$ is the sum of all base-$p$ digits. Because $p>\sqrt{k}$ implies $k<p^2$, these are precisely the last two digits.

Consequently, the original last-two-digit conjecture is already known to hold beyond some finite threshold. **The unresolved issue is whether that threshold can be taken to be 78.** The published asymptotic does not supply such an explicit bound.

**Max Alekseyev (2025).** *Existence of an odd prime $p$ such that the sum of last two base-$p$ digits is at least $p$*, MathOverflow, question 499750, August 29, 2025.

Alekseyev restated exactly the conjecture and asked:

> “Is there a simple argument that covers (any of) those remaining cases?”

The question remains without a posted answer in the retrieved version. This is direct evidence that the original proposer still considered the problem unresolved in August 2025.

The live [OEIS A094960 entry](https://oeis.org/A094960) retains the conjecture and distinguishes the known finiteness theorem from the unresolved completeness assertion.

## 2. Proof attempt and partial results

### 2.1. Known theorem: the conjecture holds eventually

**Status: Proved previously, not a new result.**

Define

$$
D_k^+=\prod_{\substack{p>\sqrt{k}\\s_p(k)\ge p}}p.
$$

By Bordellès–Luca–Moree–Shparlinski (2018), Corollary 1.6,

$$
\omega(D_k^+)
=\frac{2\sqrt{k}}{\log k}
+O\left(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\log^2 k}\right).
$$

In particular,

$$
\lim_{k\to\infty}
\frac{\omega(D_k^+)\log k}{\sqrt{k}}=2.
$$

Because the limit is positive, there exists an integer $K_0$ such that

$$
k>K_0\implies \omega(D_k^+)\ge1.
$$

Therefore some prime $p>\sqrt{k}$ satisfies $s_p(k)\ge p$. Since $k<p^2$, its base-$p$ expansion has at most two digits. Thus its last two digits sum to at least $p$.

This proves

$$
\boxed{\exists K_0\ \forall k>K_0,\quad
\exists p\ge3\text{ prime}:a_p(k)+b_p(k)\ge p.}
$$

The published result does not establish $K_0=78$.

### 2.2. An exact prime-candidate criterion

**Status: Proved here as an elementary reformulation; no novelty or priority claim.**

**Lemma.** For an integer $k\ge1$, the following are equivalent:

1. There is an odd prime $p>\sqrt{k}$ for which the last two base-$p$ digits of $k$ sum to at least $p$.
2. There are integers $t\ge2$ and an odd prime $p\ge t$ such that

$$
\boxed{1\le tp-k\le t-1.}
$$

Equivalently, one can search only integers

$$
2\le t\le\lfloor\sqrt{k}\rfloor+1,
$$

with $t\nmid k$, for which

$$
\boxed{p=\left\lceil\frac{k}{t}\right\rceil}
$$

is an odd prime satisfying $p\ge t$.

**Proof.**

Suppose first that $p>\sqrt{k}$ is a witness. Write

$$
k=ap+b,\qquad 0\le a,b<p,\quad a+b\ge p.
$$

Since $k<p^2$, this is the complete base-$p$ expansion. The digit inequality requires $a\ge1$. Set $t=a+1$. Then $2\le t\le p$ and

$$
tp-k=(a+1)p-(ap+b)=p-b.
$$

The inequalities $0\le b<p$ and $a+b\ge p$ give

$$
1\le p-b\le a=t-1.
$$

Conversely, assume $p\ge t\ge2$ and $1\le tp-k\le t-1$. Set $r=tp-k$. Then

$$
k=(t-1)p+(p-r),
$$

where $0\le t-1<p$ and $1\le p-r<p$.

These are valid base-$p$ digits, and

$$
(t-1)+(p-r)\ge p.
$$

Furthermore $k<tp\le p^2$, so $p>\sqrt{k}$.

Finally, the interval $1\le tp-k\le t-1$ forces $p=\lceil k/t\rceil$ and $t\nmid k$. Since $p>\sqrt{k}$ and $t-1=\lfloor k/p\rfloor<\sqrt{k}$, we have $t\le\lfloor\sqrt{k}\rfloor+1$.

This proves the equivalence. $\square$

The reformulation reduces a search over primes potentially as large as $k$ to at most $\lfloor\sqrt{k}\rfloor$ explicit primality candidates. It does not, by itself, prove that one of those candidates is prime.

### 2.3. A correction to the published divisor argument

**Status: Proved here; correction to a stated supporting argument, not a counterexample to the main conjecture.**

The 2021 OEIS comment, repeated in Alekseyev's 2025 MathOverflow question, suggests reducing the problem to the cases where $k+1$ is prime, a power of 2, or a Giuga number. The justification says that outside these cases a suitable odd prime dividing $k+1$ can be chosen.

**That divisor justification is not valid without an additional exception.**

Let $N=k+1$ and let $p\ge3$ divide $N$. Then

$$
k=N-1
\equiv p\left(\frac Np-1\right)+(p-1)
\pmod{p^2}.
$$

The last digit is $p-1$, and the penultimate digit is

$$
a=\left(\frac Np-1\right)\bmod p.
$$

Therefore this particular prime $p$ is a witness exactly when

$$
\boxed{\frac Np\not\equiv1\pmod p.}
$$

Consider the infinite family

$$
\boxed{k=3\cdot4^m-1,\qquad m\ge3.}
$$

Here $N=3\cdot4^m$ is composite, is not a power of 2, and is not a Giuga number because it is not squarefree. Its only odd prime divisor is 3, yet

$$
\frac N3=4^m\equiv1\pmod3.
$$

Thus **no odd prime divisor of $k+1$ supplies the proposed witness** for any member of this family.

The smallest example with $k>78$ is $k=191$, since $k+1=192=2^6\cdot3$.

Nevertheless, the original digit conjecture holds at $k=191$: choose $p=7$.

$$
191\bmod49=44=6\cdot7+2,
\qquad 6+2=8\ge7.
$$

So 191 is a counterexample to the proposed divisor-based reduction, **not** to the main conjecture.

A correct restriction for the divisor argument is that, when it fails, $N=k+1$ must be prime, a power of 2, a Giuga number, or a multiple of 4 satisfying

$$
N/p\equiv1\pmod p
$$

for every odd prime $p\mid N$.

This last possibility is not empty and cannot simply be discarded. For $N$ odd, or $N\equiv2\pmod4$, the customary prime/power-of-2/Giuga distinction remains valid.

## 3. Computational verification

**Status: Computed, exhaustive over the stated finite range.**

I ran two independent checks:

- The original last-two-digit condition for every $1\le k\le10^9$, using all 52 odd primes from 3 through 241.
- The equivalence in Lemma 2.2, comparing its prime-candidate construction against direct digit calculations for every $1\le k\le20{,}000$.

### Program

Python 3, using NumPy and SymPy:

```python

from math import isqrt
from sympy import primerange
import numpy as np

N = 1_000_000_000
odd_primes = list(primerange(3, 242))
remaining = []

for lo in range(1, N + 1, 5_000_000):
    v = np.arange(
        lo, min(N + 1, lo + 5_000_000),
        dtype=np.int32
    )
    for p in odd_primes:
        r = v % (p * p)
        v = v[r // p + r % p < p]
        if len(v) == 0:
            break
    remaining.extend(v.tolist())

print('range: 1..1,000,000,000')
print(f'odd primes tested: {len(odd_primes)}, largest {odd_primes[-1]}')
print('no-witness integers:', remaining)
print('counterexamples above 78:', [k for k in remaining if k > 78])

M = 20_000
prime_set = set(primerange(3, M + 1))

for k in range(1, M + 1):
    direct = {
        p for p in prime_set
        if p * p > k and p <= k
        and k // p + k % p >= p
    }
    reformulated = {
        p for t in range(2, isqrt(k) + 2)
        if k % t
        and (p := (k + t - 1) // t) >= t
        and p in prime_set
    }
    assert direct == reformulated, (k, direct, reformulated)

print('large-prime lemma checked: 1..20,000')
```

The 16 reported no-witness integers are genuine exceptions within this range, not merely survivors of the 52-prime sieve. They are all at most 78; for any prime $p>k$, the last two digits of $k$ have sum $k<p$, so larger primes cannot help.

Thus the computation establishes the finite-range statement

$$
\boxed{\forall k\in[79,10^9]\cap\mathbb Z,\
\exists p\le241\text{ odd prime with }a+b\ge p.}
$$

This is stronger computational evidence than merely checking membership in A094960, but it remains a finite calculation.

### Reconciliation with A094960

Six of the 16 integers failing the two-digit test are not members of A094960. Their exclusion follows from Kellner's exact denominator formula

$$
\operatorname{denom}(B'_k(x))
=
\prod_{\substack{p\nmid k\\s_p(k)\ge p}}p.
$$

For the six extra exceptions, the following primes demonstrate nonintegrality:

| $k$ | Witness $p$ | $s_p(k)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 2 | 2 |
| 11 | 2 | 3 |
| 15 | 2 | 4 |
| 29 | 2 | 4 |
| 58 | 3 | 4 |
| 78 | 5 | 6 |

In particular, $58=(2011)_3$ and $78=(303)_5$. These have sufficiently large *total* digit sums despite failing the *last-two-digit* criterion for every prime.

Applying the denominator formula to all 16 survivors gives precisely

$$
\{1,2,4,6,10,12,28,30,36,60\},
$$

in agreement with A094960. This confirms the sequence within the tested range but does not establish its global completeness.

This investigation supplies an elementary prime-candidate equivalence and an independently reproducible finite-range verification. This does **not** refute the original conjecture.
