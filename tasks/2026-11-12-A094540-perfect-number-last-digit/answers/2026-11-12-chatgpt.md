# OEIS A094540 — Status and proof investigation

**Task:** Investigate J. Lowell's 2007 conjecture that the ratio of perfect numbers ending in 6 to those ending in 8 approaches $3/2$.

**Investigation date:** November 12, 2026.

## 1. Status and literature

### 1.1. The conjecture

The original statement appears in Eric W. Weisstein's OEIS entry [A094540](https://oeis.org/A094540), with a comment by J. Lowell dated June 26, 2007:

> Conjecture: Ratio of 6's to 8's approaches 1.5 as a limit as this sequence approaches infinity.

There are two distinct mathematical questions here.

**First, the even-perfect-number interpretation.** By the Euclid–Euler theorem, every even perfect number has the form

$$
E(p)=2^{p-1}(2^p-1),
$$

where $p$ and $2^p-1$ are prime.

For such numbers, the final decimal digit is determined by $p\bmod4$. The conjecture therefore concerns the distribution of Mersenne-prime exponents between the residue classes 1 and 3 modulo 4.

**Second, the literal OEIS interpretation.** A094540 is defined using *all* perfect numbers, not just even perfect numbers. Whether odd perfect numbers exist is an unsolved problem. The OEIS entry explicitly limits its published terms because an unknown odd perfect number could change the ordering.

Consequently, the two interpretations must not be treated as equivalent without an additional assumption about odd perfect numbers.

### 1.2. Current mathematical status

**Verdict: OPEN.** I found no established proof or counterexample to the $3/2$ limit.

The relevant open problems include:

- **Infinitely many Mersenne primes?** OEIS A000043 states: “It is believed (but unproved) that this sequence is infinite.”
- **Infinitely many Mersenne-prime exponents in each class modulo 4?** Neither infinitude nor the required relative frequency is established by the sources reviewed.
- **Odd perfect numbers?** Their existence remains unknown, independently of the Mersenne-prime problem.

The latest GIMPS information is particularly important. On September 4, 2026, the exponent $82\,589\,933$ was confirmed as the 51st Mersenne-prime exponent. The larger known exponent $136\,279\,841$, discovered in October 2024, still has **provisional rank 52** because possible intervening exponents have not all been eliminated.

### 1.3. Relevant published results and heuristics

**Ochem and Rao (2012).** Pascal Ochem and Michaël Rao, *Odd perfect numbers are greater than $10^{1500}$*, *Mathematics of Computation* **81** (279), 1869–1877, DOI: 10.1090/S0025-5718-2012-02563-4.

Decisive statement from the abstract:

> We modify their method to obtain $N>10^{1500}$.

This is a published result. Pascal Ochem's subsequent project page reports a computational improvement to $N>10^{2200}$, also reflected in MathWorld's 2026 update. That stronger bound is a reported computation, not a new result independently certified in this investigation.

**Wagstaff (1983).** Samuel S. Wagstaff Jr., *Divisors of Mersenne Numbers*, *Mathematics of Computation* **40** (161), 385–397. This is a foundational source for heuristic models of Mersenne-prime distribution, not a proof of the requested limit.

The Prime Pages' exposition of the Lenstra–Pomerance–Wagstaff heuristic gives the conditional probability estimate

$$
\Pr(2^p-1\text{ prime}\mid p\text{ prime})
\approx
\frac{e^\gamma\log(a_pp)}{p\log2},
$$

where $a_p=6$ for $p\equiv1\pmod4$ and $a_p=2$ for $p\equiv3\pmod4$. These factors differ only in a lower-order logarithmic term. Combined with the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions, the model suggests an eventual ratio of **1, rather than $3/2$**.

This is a heuristic prediction, not a refutation. The exposition also has an apparent interchange of the two residue classes in its later verbal derivation; its displayed formula agrees with the elementary congruence calculation.

## 2. Proof, reduction, and partial results

### 2.1. A precise formulation

Let

$$
\mathcal M=\{p\ge2:2^p-1\text{ is prime}\}
$$

and define

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_1(x)&=\#\{p\in\mathcal M:p\le x,\ p\equiv1\pmod4\},\\
M_3(x)&=\#\{p\in\mathcal M:p\le x,\ p\equiv3\pmod4\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The exponent $p=2$ is exceptional and contributes one additional perfect number ending in 6.

A nondegenerate, precise version of the conjecture is

$$
\boxed{
M_3(x)\longrightarrow\infty
\quad\text{and}\quad
\lim_{x\to\infty}
\frac{1+M_1(x)}{M_3(x)}=\frac32.
}
\tag{C}
$$

The infinitude condition explicitly excludes a merely finite, accidentally equal ratio.

### 2.2. The last-digit theorem

**Theorem (proved; elementary and known).** For every prime exponent $p$ for which $2^p-1$ is prime,

$$
\boxed{
E(p)\equiv
\begin{cases}
6\pmod{10},&p=2\text{ or }p\equiv1\pmod4,\\
8\pmod{10},&p\equiv3\pmod4.
\end{cases}
}
$$

**Proof.**

The powers of 2 modulo 10 repeat with period 4:

$$
2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 6,\ 2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 6,\ldots
$$

For $p=2$,

$$
E(2)=2(2^2-1)=6.
$$

Every other exponent in $\mathcal M$ is an odd prime.

If $p\equiv1\pmod4$, then

$$
2^{p-1}\equiv6,\qquad 2^p\equiv2\pmod{10}.
$$

Therefore

$$
E(p)\equiv6(2-1)\equiv6\pmod{10}.
$$

If $p\equiv3\pmod4$, then

$$
2^{p-1}\equiv4,\qquad 2^p\equiv8\pmod{10},
$$

so

$$
E(p)\equiv4(8-1)=28\equiv8\pmod{10}.
$$

These exhaust all possible Mersenne-prime exponents. $\square$

This proves the digit classification, but not the distribution of those digits. The modular calculation is already implicit in A094540's published formulas and is not claimed as a discovery.

### 2.3. Exact reduction to the Mersenne-prime distribution

**Theorem (proved).** Conjecture (C) is equivalent to the conjunction of

$$
M_3(x)\to\infty
\quad\text{and}\quad
\boxed{\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{M_1(x)}{M_3(x)}=\frac32.}
$$

Equivalently, it requires

$$
\boxed{
\lim_{x\to\infty}
\frac{M_1(x)}{M_1(x)+M_3(x)}=\frac35
}
$$

with infinitely many exponents in the second residue class.

**Proof.** By the last-digit theorem, the respective counts are exactly $1+M_1(x)$ and $M_3(x)$. When $M_3(x)\to\infty$,

$$
\frac{1+M_1(x)}{M_3(x)}
=
\frac{M_1(x)}{M_3(x)}
+\frac1{M_3(x)},
$$

and the final summand tends to zero. The proportion statement follows by dividing numerator and denominator by $M_3(x)$. The converse is immediate. $\square$

**Consequence:** This is a reduction to an unresolved distribution question about Mersenne primes. A proof of the nondegenerate conjecture would establish infinitely many Mersenne primes in *both* residue classes modulo 4.

It is therefore strictly stronger than merely proving that infinitely many Mersenne primes exist. I found no known theorem that provides this required $3:2$ split.

There is also an important distinction regarding odd perfect numbers. Define $C_d(Y)$ as the number of perfect integers not exceeding $Y$ and ending in digit $d$. Since every integer ending in 6 or 8 is even, **odd perfect numbers contribute to neither $C_6(Y)$ nor $C_8(Y)$**. Consequently, the ratio $C_6(Y)/C_8(Y)$, whenever defined, is governed exactly by the even perfect numbers.

Thus odd perfect numbers can alter the indexing of A094540, but they cannot alter these two counts at any fixed numerical cutoff. If the set of all perfect numbers is infinite, taking the ratio along its first $n$ terms is equivalent to taking numerical cutoffs tending to infinity, provided the denominator is eventually nonzero.

### 2.4. A possible extension of A094540

**Conditional result, using a reported computational bound.**

A094540 currently publishes only 17 terms. But the next Mersenne-prime exponent is

$$
p_{18}=3217\equiv1\pmod4.
$$

Its even perfect number

$$
E(3217)=2^{3216}(2^{3217}-1)
$$

has exactly **1,937 decimal digits**, so

$$
E(3217)<10^{2200}.
$$

If Ochem's subsequently reported lower bound $N>10^{2200}$ for odd perfect numbers is accepted, no odd perfect number can occur before this even perfect number.

Therefore the literal sequence has a certified extension relative to that bound:

$$
\boxed{a(18)=6.}
$$

The first 18 digits would then be

$$
6,8,6,8,6,6,8,8,6,6,8,8,6,8,8,8,6,6.
$$

This inference is elementary and the numerical bound is previously reported; it is not a new number-theoretic theorem. With only Ochem–Rao's published $10^{1500}$ bound, the guaranteed prefix remains the original 17 terms.

## 3. Computational verification

from collections import Counter

    2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, 61, 89, 107, 127, 521,
    607, 1279, 2203, 2281, 3217, 4253, 4423, 9689, 9941,
    11213, 19937, 21701, 23209, 44497, 86243, 110503,

    2976221, 3021377, 6972593, 13466917, 20996011,
    24036583, 25964951, 30402457, 32582657, 37156667,
    42643801, 43112609, 57885161, 74207281, 77232917,
    82589933
]
provisional_extra = 136279841

def last_digit(p):
    return (pow(2, p-1, 10) * (pow(2, p, 10)-1)) % 10

def check(label, exponents):
    ds = [last_digit(p) for p in exponents]
    c = Counter(ds)
    print(f"{label}: n={len(ds)}, sixes={c[6]}, "
          f"eights={c[8]}, ratio={c[6]}/{c[8]}")
    assert all(d == (6 if p == 2 or p % 4 == 1 else 8)
               for p, d in zip(exponents, ds))
    return ds

assert len(p51) == 51
assert p51 == sorted(p51)

known17 = [6,8,6,8,6,6,8,8,6,6,8,8,6,8,8,8,6]
assert check("A094540 first 17", p51[:17]) == known17

p18 = (1 << 3216) * ((1 << 3217) - 1)

      p51 + [provisional_extra])

    last_digit(p) == (6 if p == 2 or p % 4 == 1 else 8)

## 4. Verdict

**Status: OPEN, with an exact reduction but no proof or counterexample.** The nondegenerate $3/2$ conjecture requires infinitely many Mersenne-prime exponents in both odd residue classes modulo 4, with asymptotic proportions $3/5$ and $2/5$. That distribution is not known, and ordinary Mersenne-prime heuristics instead suggest equal limiting proportions. The elementary digit classification and Euclid–Euler correspondence are known mathematics; no genuinely new theorem is claimed here. The computational check confirms 32 endings in 6 versus 19 in 8 among the first 51 confirmed Mersenne-prime exponents, and 33 versus 19 when including the additional known exponent of provisional rank 52. A possible practical update to OEIS A094540 is the conditional extension $a(18)=6$, justified by Ochem's reported $10^{2200}$ lower bound for odd perfect numbers; this should be independently verified against the status of that computation before submission. **Suggested OEIS comment:** “For even perfect numbers $2^{p-1}(2^p-1)$, the last digit is 6 if $p=2$ or $p\equiv1\pmod4$, and 8 if $p\equiv3\pmod4$. The conjectured limiting ratio $3/2$, interpreted nondegenerately, is equivalent to an asymptotic $3:2$ distribution of Mersenne-prime exponents in these two classes, which is unproved.
