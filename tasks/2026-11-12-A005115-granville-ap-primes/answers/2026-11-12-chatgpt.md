# OEIS A005115 — Granville’s conjecture on arithmetic progressions of primes

**Research report — 12 November 2026**

## 1. Status

**Verdict: Apparently open.** I found no proof or counterexample to Granville’s conjecture

$$
\boxed{a(n)\le n!+1\qquad(n\ge3),}
$$

where $a(n)$ is the smallest possible final prime in an arithmetic progression of $n$ primes.

The statement is well-posed and is not equivalent to the Green–Tao theorem.

### Original source

Andrew Granville, *Prime Number Patterns*, **The American Mathematical Monthly**, 115(4) (2008), pp. 279–296, §2.1, p. 281, explicitly writes:

> “we conjecture that this bound should be improvable to $k!+1$, for each $k\ge3$.”

The same passage proposes the heuristic

$$
a(k)\approx
\left(e^{1-\gamma}\frac{k}{2}\right)^{k/2},
$$

where $\gamma$ is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. This is a heuristic prediction, not a proved asymptotic.

Ben Green and Terence Tao, *The primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions*, **Annals of Mathematics**, 167 (2008), pp. 481–547, prove:

> “We prove that there are arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions of primes.”

Their theorem guarantees that $a(n)$ exists for every $n$, but does not establish the factorial upper bound.

### Current evidence

The live OEIS entry A005115 continues to attribute the factorial inequality to Granville as a conjecture. Its recorded values extend through $n=26$, with the latest extension dated 21 February 2026.

Norman Luhn’s *Primes in Arithmetic Progression Records*, updated 10 November 2026, lists known examples through length 27. It does not report a general proof of Granville’s inequality.

Two related Erdős problems should be distinguished:

- **Problem 219**, the existence of arbitrarily long prime arithmetic progressions, is proved by Green–Tao.
- **Problem 200**, whether the longest prime progression below $N$ has length $o(\log N)$, remains open. It concerns a related growth question, but is not equivalent to Granville’s bound. Problems+1

## 2. Proof attempt and partial results

### Precise formulation

For $n\ge3$, define

$$
a(n)=
\min_{\substack{u,d\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\\
u,u+d,\ldots,u+(n-1)d\ \mathrm{all\ prime}}}
\bigl(u+(n-1)d\bigr).
$$

Green–Tao guarantees that the set being minimized is nonempty.

Granville's conjecture is equivalently:

$$
\forall n\ge3,\quad
\exists u,d\ge1:
\begin{cases}
u+jd\text{ is prime},&0\le j<n,\\
u+(n-1)d\le n!+1.
\end{cases}
$$

### Proposition — A necessary divisibility condition

Let

$$
Q(n)=\prod_{\substack{p<n\\p\text{ prime}}}p
$$

and let $r(n)$ be the least prime greater than or equal to $n$.

**Proved:** Every arithmetic progression of $n\ge3$ primes

$$
u,u+d,\ldots,u+(n-1)d
$$

satisfies

$$
u\ge r(n),\qquad Q(n)\mid d.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{a(n)\ge r(n)+(n-1)Q(n).}
$$

**Proof.**

First suppose $u<n$. Since $u$ is prime, the progression includes the term with index $j=u$. That term is

$$
u+ud=u(1+d),
$$

which is composite because $d\ge1$. This is a contradiction. Hence $u\ge n$, and since $u$ is prime, $u\ge r(n)$.

Now take any prime $p<n$. Suppose $p\nmid d$. The first $p$ terms

$$
u,u+d,\ldots,u+(p-1)d
$$

represent every residue class modulo $p$. Therefore one of them is divisible by $p$.

But every term is prime and at least $u\ge n>p$. A prime greater than $p$ cannot be divisible by $p$.

Thus $p\mid d$ for every prime $p<n$, proving $Q(n)\mid d$.

Finally,

$$
a(n)=u+(n-1)d
\ge r(n)+(n-1)Q(n).
$$

$\square$

This is the standard primorial obstruction for prime arithmetic progressions, presented here with its elementary proof. **No novelty is claimed.**

An additional observation is that if $n$ itself is prime and $u>n$, then $n\mid d$ as well. The only possible exception is $u=n$.

### Corollary — Exact early values

The lower bound, together with explicit prime progressions, proves:

| $n$ | Lower bound | Prime progression | $a(n)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 7 | 3, 5, 7 | 7 |
| 4 | 23 | 5, 11, 17, 23 | 23 |
| 5 | 29 | 5, 11, 17, 23, 29 | 29 |
| 6 | 157 | 7, 37, 67, 97, 127, 157 | 157 |

These are known values in OEIS A005115, not new discoveries.

By the prime number theorem,

$$
\log Q(n)=(1+o(1))n.
$$

The proposition therefore gives the asymptotic lower bound

$$
\log a(n)\ge (1+o(1))n.
$$

This is much weaker than the scale of Granville's heuristic, whose logarithm is approximately $(n/2)\log n$, and weaker still than the proposed factorial upper bound's logarithmic scale $n\log n$.

**The proof attempt does not establish the conjecture for all $n$.** The primorial obstruction restricts possible differences but does not guarantee enough primes below $n!+1$.

### An additional finite case: $n=27$

A previously known progression found by Rob Gahan in September 2019 is

$$
p_j=224584605939537911
+18135696597948930j,\quad 0\le j\le26.
$$

All 27 terms are prime, and the final term is

$$
p_{26}=696112717486210091<27!+1.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{a(27)\le696112717486210091<27!+1.}
$$

The progression is published as OEIS A327760. It is **not a new discovery**, and its endpoint is not asserted to be the minimum $a(27)$.

## 3. Computational verification

I ran an independent Python check using a sieve for exhaustive minimization through $n=13$, and deterministic primality testing of the known witnesses through $n=27$.

The test uses the starting terms from OEIS A113827 and differences from A093364 for $n\le26$, plus the published A327760 progression at $n=27$.

**Complete executed program:** [Download a005115_check.py](#file-0)

The essential exhaustive-search routine is:

```python

from math import prod

def least_end(n):
    q = prod(p for p in prime_list if p < n)
    for end in prime_list:
        if end < 3 + (n - 1)*q:
            continue
        for d in range(q, (end - 3)//(n - 1) + 1, q):
            if all(sieve[end - j*d] for j in range(1, n)):
                return end, end - (n - 1)*d, d
    raise AssertionError("No progression within sieve limit")
```

Here `prime_list` and `sieve` are generated by the complete script up to 725663. The restriction to multiples of $q$ is exhaustive because of the proved divisibility lemma.

For $n=14,\ldots,27$, the script tests each supplied progression term with `sympy.isprime`. Every tested integer is below $2^{64}$, within SymPy's deterministic primality-testing range. These checks verify witnesses, not minimality.
