# OEIS A072567 — Status and Proof Attempt for the Symmetric Zarankiewicz Conjecture

**Report date:** November 12, 2026 **Task:** [Athanor / A072567 / TASK.md](https://github.com/iwasborninbali/athanor-oeis/blob/main/tasks/2026-11-12-A072567-zarankiewicz-symmetric/TASK.md)

**Verdict:** The general conjecture remains unresolved in the sources located. It holds for infinitely many orders by a classical projective-plane construction, and I independently verified matching symmetric constructions for every $1\le n\le21$. An exhaustive enumeration also confirms the symmetric optimum for $1\le n\le8$. No general proof or counterexample was obtained.

## 1. Status and literature

### 1.1 Precise statement

Let $Z(n)$ be the maximum number of ones in an $n\times n$ binary matrix containing no all-one $2\times2$ submatrix. Thus

$$
Z(n)=z(n,n;2,2)=\mathrm{A072567}(n).
$$

Define the symmetric version

$$
S(n)=\max\left\{
\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}A_{ij}:
A=A^{\mathsf T},\ A_{ij}\in\{0,1\},
\ A\text{ has no all-one }2\times2
\right\}.
$$

**Conjecture (Max Alekseyev, 2022):**

$$
\boxed{S(n)=Z(n)\qquad\text{for every }n\ge1.}
$$

Diagonal ones are permitted. This is essential: the zero-diagonal problem corresponds to the different sequence OEIS A191965.

### 1.2 Status in OEIS

Max Alekseyev's comment in [OEIS A072567](https://oeis.org/A072567), dated April 3, 2022, states:

> Conjecture: the same number of 1s is achieved for symmetric n X n matrices (cf. A350189).

The corresponding comment in [OEIS A350189](https://oeis.org/A350189), also dated April 3, 2022, independently labels the equality a conjecture. That entry was still displaying this statement when indexed with a November 6, 2026 modification date.

I found no publication proving or refuting the general equality.

## 2. Proof attempt and partial results

### 2.1 Proved: equality for infinitely many orders

**Theorem.** For every prime power $q$,

$$
\boxed{
S(q^2+q+1)=Z(q^2+q+1)
=(q+1)(q^2+q+1).
}
$$

This is the classical projective-plane equality case, with its symmetric realization made explicit below. It is not claimed as new.

**Proof.**

First, let $A$ be any rectangle-free $n\times n$ binary matrix, and let $d_i$ be its row sums. Any pair of columns can occur together in at most one row, so

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\binom{d_i}{2}\le\binom n2.
$$

Writing $m=\sum_i d_i$, Cauchy–Schwarz gives

$$
\frac12\left(\frac{m^2}{n}-m\right)
\le\binom n2.
$$

Solving the quadratic inequality,

$$
m\le\frac n2\left(1+\sqrt{4n-3}\right).
$$

Now put $n=q^2+q+1$. Since $4n-3=(2q+1)^2$,

$$
Z(n)\le(q+1)n.
$$

For a matching symmetric construction, consider the projective plane $\mathrm{PG}(2,q)$. Its $q^2+q+1$ points are one-dimensional subspaces of $\mathbb F_q^3$.

Index rows and columns by these points and set

$$
A_{[u],[v]}=
\begin{cases}
1,&u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3=0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

This is well-defined on projective points, because scaling either vector does not change whether their dot product is zero.

The matrix is symmetric by construction.

For any nonzero $u$, the equation $u\cdot v=0$ defines a two-dimensional vector subspace, containing exactly $q+1$ projective points. Therefore every row has $q+1$ ones.

For two distinct projective points $[u]\ne[w]$, the vectors $u,w$ are linearly independent. The system

$$
u\cdot v=0,\qquad w\cdot v=0
$$

has a one-dimensional solution space. Consequently, the corresponding two rows have exactly one common column containing a one.

Thus $A$ is rectangle-free, symmetric, and contains precisely

$$
(q+1)(q^2+q+1)
$$

ones, attaining the upper bound. $\square$

This proves the conjecture for infinitely many $n$, including:

| $q$ | $n=q^2+q+1$ | $S(n)=Z(n)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 7 | 21 |
| 3 | 13 | 52 |
| 4 | 21 | 105 |
| 5 | 31 | 186 |
| 7 | 57 | 456 |
| 11 | 133 | 1596 |

The projective-plane equality values are also recorded in A072567, attributed there to Senya Karpenko (2014), using Reiman's inequality.

### 2.2 Proved lemma: deletion identity

Let $A$ be the symmetric projective-plane matrix of order $q$, of size $N=q^2+q+1$. Let $D$ be a subset of $k$ projective points, and delete the corresponding rows and columns.

Denote by $A[D]$ the principal submatrix indexed by $D$, and let $w(B)$ be the number of ones in a binary matrix $B$.

Then

$$
\boxed{
w(A[\overline D])
=(q+1)(N-2k)+w(A[D]).
}
$$

**Proof.** The original matrix has $(q+1)N$ ones. Deleting the $k$ rows removes $k(q+1)$ ones, and deleting the $k$ columns removes another $k(q+1)$. Entries within $D\times D$ were counted twice, so their number $w(A[D])$ must be added back.

The remaining principal submatrix is automatically symmetric and rectangle-free. $\square$

This identity reduces the search for smaller symmetric witnesses to finding small dense principal submatrices of a projective-plane polarity matrix.

### 3.1 Exhaustive enumeration

The C++ enumerator constructs symmetric matrices one vertex at a time, including possible diagonal ones.

When a new vertex is added, it considers every subset of existing vertices to which it may be adjacent, together with both choices for its diagonal entry.

It rejects an extension whenever two rows acquire more than one common neighbor. This is sufficient and necessary for the rectangle-free condition.

Every symmetric binary matrix is generated exactly once.

For unrestricted matrices, a second enumeration adds rows while tracking which pairs of columns have already appeared together.

### 3.2 Projective-plane verification

The Python checker constructs each projective-plane matrix directly from normalized three-coordinate vectors.

It verifies that:

1. The number of projective points is $q^2+q+1$.
2. Every row has exactly $q+1$ ones.
3. Every pair of distinct full rows has exactly one common one.
4. Every selected principal submatrix remains symmetric and rectangle-free.
5. Its number of ones matches the claimed target.

## 4. Verdict

**The general conjecture remains open in the literature located as of November 12, 2026.** There is no known counterexample in the searched sources, and this investigation did not produce one. The equality $S(q^2+q+1)=Z(q^2+q+1)$ is proved for every prime power $q$ by a standard projective-plane polarity construction; this is established mathematics rather than a new theorem. The principal-submatrix deletion identity and explicit finite-field certificates verify matching symmetric witnesses for every $1\le n\le21$, using published values of A072567, while independent exhaustive enumeration establishes the symmetric maxima through $n=8$. These are reproducible confirmations, not a resolution of the general conjecture. The only definite OEIS correction identified is the apparent $17$-for-$16$ typo at $n=6$ in A350189's explanatory comment. I am highly confident in the elementary proof and the reported computations, but the literature search is not exhaustive enough to certify that no unindexed or inaccessible recent proof exists.
