# OEIS A374242 — Status and proof attempt

**Research date:** November 12, 2026 **Target:** The conjecture that every term of OEIS A374242 is odd.

## 1. Status and literature search

**Status: Open as far as the sources examined establish.** I found neither a published proof nor a counterexample. The OEIS entry still records the statement as a conjecture, although that alone cannot establish that no independent proof exists.

### Precise formulation

For $n\geq 3$, let $S_{n-1}$ denote the permutations of $\{1,\ldots,n-1\}$. Given $p=(p_1,\ldots,p_{n-1})\in S_{n-1}$, define

$$
T_n(p)=(t_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n},\qquad
t_{ij}=\begin{cases}
1,&i=j,\\
p_{|i-j|},&i\ne j.
\end{cases}
$$

Then

$$
a(n)=\min_{\substack{p\in S_{n-1}\\\det T_n(p)\ne0}}
|\det T_n(p)|.
$$

The conjecture is

$$
\boxed{\forall n\geq3,\quad a(n)\equiv1\pmod2.}
$$

This is well-defined: there are finitely many permutations, and the displayed set of nonsingular matrices is nonempty. Indeed, the identity permutation $p_k=k$ gives determinant $(-1)^{n-2}(n-2)$ for $n\geq3$, as proved below.

## 2. Proof attempt and rigorous partial results

**Correction to Section 1:** The preliminary determinant formula for $p_k=k$ was incorrect; in particular, its determinant is zero at $n=6$. The following exact formula replaces it and supplies a valid proof of nonemptiness.

### Theorem A — An infinite subsequence is exactly 1

**Proved.** For the natural-order permutation $p=(1,2,\ldots,n-1)$, write $D_n=\det T_n(p)$. Then

$$
\boxed{
D_n=
\begin{cases}
1,&n\equiv0\pmod4,\\
(n+1)/2,&n\equiv1\pmod4,\\
0,&n\equiv2\pmod4,\\
-(n-1)/2,&n\equiv3\pmod4.
\end{cases}}
\qquad(n\geq3).
$$

In particular,

$$
\boxed{a(4k)=1\quad\text{for every }k\geq1.}
$$

This proves the OEIS conjecture on an infinite arithmetic progression, rather than merely checking finitely many terms.

#### Proof of Theorem A

Let $R_n$ be the unit lower-bidiagonal matrix whose subdiagonal entries are $-1$. Thus $\det R_n=1$.

Applying successive row and column differences gives

$$
R_nT_n(1,2,\ldots,n-1)R_n^{\mathsf T}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&v^{\mathsf T}\\
v&C_{n-1}
\end{pmatrix},
$$

where $v=(0,1,\ldots,1)^{\mathsf T}$, and $C_r$ is the $r\times r$ tridiagonal matrix with zero diagonal and $-1$ immediately above and below it.

Its determinant follows from the continuant recurrence:

$$
\det C_r=-\det C_{r-2},\quad
\det C_0=1,\quad\det C_1=0.
$$

Therefore

$$
\det C_{2j}=(-1)^j,\qquad
\det C_{2j+1}=0.
$$

**Case 1: $n=2m$.** The matrix $C_{2m-1}$ has a one-dimensional kernel spanned by

$$
z=(1,0,-1,0,1,0,\ldots,(-1)^{m-1})^{\mathsf T}.
$$

Its adjugate is

$$
\operatorname{adj}(C_{2m-1})
=(-1)^{m-1}zz^{\mathsf T}.
$$

Using the bordered-determinant identity,

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{2m}
&=\det C_{2m-1}
-v^{\mathsf T}\operatorname{adj}(C_{2m-1})v\\
&=-(-1)^{m-1}
\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(-1)^j\right)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The sum is $-1$ when $m$ is even and $0$ when $m$ is odd. Hence

$$
D_{4k}=1,\qquad D_{4k+2}=0.
$$

**Case 2: $n=2m+1$.** Now $C_{2m}$ is invertible, so

$$
D_{2m+1}
=(-1)^m(1-v^{\mathsf T}C_{2m}^{-1}v).
$$

To evaluate the quadratic form, solve $Cx=v$. Its equations are

$$
-x_{i-1}-x_{i+1}=v_i,
\qquad x_0=x_{2m+1}=0.
$$

Successive substitution gives

$$
v^{\mathsf T}x=
\begin{cases}
-m,&m\text{ even},\\
-(m-1),&m\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
$$

Consequently $D_{4k+1}=2k+1$ and $D_{4k+3}=-(2k+1)$. This completes the proof. $\square$

### Theorem B — A uniformly bounded odd determinant in the remaining even dimensions

**Proved.** For every $n=4k+2\geq6$, choose the permutation

$$
p=(1,2,\ldots,n-3,n-1,n-2).
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{\det T_n(p)=-9.}
$$

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{a(4k+2)\leq9\qquad(k\geq1).}
$$

Together with Theorem A, this proves that **every even dimension $n\geq4$ admits an admissible matrix with odd determinant of absolute value at most $9$**.

#### Proof of Theorem B

Put $n=2m$, where $m\geq3$ is odd. By the reversal symmetry of a symmetric Toeplitz matrix, write its determinant as

$$
\det T=\det(A+B)\det(A-B),
$$

where $A$ is its leading $m\times m$ block and $B_{ij}=T_{i,\,2m+1-j}$.

For the natural-order permutation, $B_{ij}=2m+1-i-j$. Swapping the last two permutation entries changes only

$$
B_{11}\mapsto B_{11}-1,\qquad
B_{12},B_{21}\mapsto B_{12}+1,B_{21}+1.
$$

Thus the modified matrix has $B'=B+K$, where

$$
K=\begin{pmatrix}
-1&1&0&\cdots\\
1&0&0&\cdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Apply the difference matrix $R_m$ from Theorem A to $A+B'$ and $A-B'$. Both transformations preserve determinants.

Let $e_1$ be the first coordinate vector. Direct subtraction gives the first transformed block

$$
U=
\begin{pmatrix}
2m-1&1&-e_1^{\mathsf T}\\
1&-3&0\\
-e_1&0&C_{m-2}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Because $m-2$ is odd, $\det C_{m-2}=0$. Expanding along the second row and using the $(1,1)$-cofactor of $C_{m-2}$ gives

$$
\det U=3(-1)^{(m-3)/2}.
$$

For the other block, put $s=m-1$, which is even. The transformation yields

$$
V=
\begin{pmatrix}
3-2m&z^{\mathsf T}\\
z&H_s
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
z=(-1,3,2,\ldots,2)^{\mathsf T},
$$

where

$$
H_s=
\begin{pmatrix}
3&-2&0&\cdots&0\\
-2&0&-1&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&-1&0&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&-1\\
0&\cdots&0&-1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The same continuant recurrence gives

$$
\det H_s=4(-1)^{s/2},\qquad
\det H_{s-1}=3(-1)^{(s-2)/2}.
$$

In particular, $H_s$ is invertible and

$$
(H_s^{-1})_{ss}
=\frac{\det H_{s-1}}{\det H_s}=-\frac34.
$$

Writing $\mathbf1$ for the all-ones vector, observe that

$$
H_s(-\mathbf1)=z-e_s.
$$

Hence $H_s^{-1}z=-\mathbf1+H_s^{-1}e_s$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
z^{\mathsf T}H_s^{-1}z
&=-\sum_i z_i-1+(H_s^{-1})_{ss}\\
&=-(2s-2)-1-\frac34\\
&=\frac14-2s.
\end{aligned}
$$

The Schur complement therefore yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det V
&=\det H_s
\left(3-2m-z^{\mathsf T}H_s^{-1}z\right)\\
&=4(-1)^{s/2}\cdot\frac34\\
&=3(-1)^{(m-1)/2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally,

$$
\det T=\det U\det V
=9(-1)^{(m-3)/2+(m-1)/2}=-9.
$$

This proves Theorem B. $\square$

### Theorem C — The parity restriction for all admissible determinants

**Proved.** Let $M$ be any integer centrosymmetric matrix, meaning $M_{ij}=M_{n+1-i,n+1-j}$. If $n$ is odd, additionally suppose its central entry is odd. Then

$$
\boxed{\det M\not\equiv2\pmod4.}
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\boxed{2\mid\det M\ \Longrightarrow\ 4\mid\det M.}
$$

This applies to every matrix defining A374242.

#### Proof

For $n=2m$, reorder coordinates into reversal pairs. Centrosymmetry gives the block representation

$$
M\sim
\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\B&A\end{pmatrix},
$$

and therefore

$$
\det M=\det(A+B)\det(A-B).
$$

Since $A+B\equiv A-B\pmod2$, the two determinant factors have the same parity. If their product is even, both are even, so their product is divisible by four.

For $n=2m+1$, separating the central coordinate gives

$$
M\sim
\begin{pmatrix}
A&B&u\\
B&A&u\\
w^{\mathsf T}&w^{\mathsf T}&c
\end{pmatrix},
$$

where $c$ is odd. Changing to symmetric and antisymmetric reversal coordinates gives

$$
\det M
=
\det(A-B)
\det\begin{pmatrix}
A+B&u\\
2w^{\mathsf T}&c
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Modulo two, the second factor equals

$$
c\det(A+B)\equiv\det(A-B)\pmod2.
$$

Again both factors have the same parity, proving the assertion. $\square$

### What these results do and do not establish

For all $n\geq3$, Theorems A and B provide an explicit nonsingular matrix with odd determinant. Theorem C proves that a competing nonzero even determinant must have absolute value at least $4$.

The following unconditional bounds result:

| Dimension | Proven bound or exact result |
| --- | --- |
| $n\equiv0\pmod4$ | $a(n)=1$ |
| $n\equiv1\pmod4,\ n\geq5$ | $1\leq a(n)\leq(n+1)/2$ |
| $n\equiv2\pmod4,\ n\geq6$ | $1\leq a(n)\leq9$ |
| $n\equiv3\pmod4$ | $1\leq a(n)\leq(n-1)/2$ |

**Crucial distinction:** the existence of an odd determinant does not imply that the *minimum nonzero absolute determinant* is odd. An even determinant of absolute value $4$ or $8$, for instance, could defeat the witness $-9$ in Theorem B. Theorem C does not exclude those possibilities.

Thus the full conjecture is not proved by these results. The exact formulas and congruence theorem are independently derived here; their originality relative to all mathematical literature has not been established.

## 4. Verdict

**The conjecture remains unresolved in this investigation, but substantial unconditional partial results have been established.** Exact proofs show that $a(4k)=1$ for every $k\geq1$; that a determinant of $-9$ is attainable for every dimension $n\equiv2\pmod4$, $n\geq6$; and that every even determinant in the defining matrix family is divisible by $4$. Together these prove the existence of an odd determinant in every admissible dimension, but not necessarily the oddness of the minimum. An exhaustive exact computation verifies the conjecture for $3\leq n\leq11$, and separate checks support both explicit determinant identities. I have high confidence in the stated algebraic results and their proofs, while making no claim that they are previously unpublished. The literature search found no resolution of A374242, but its coverage was not exhaustive. No OEIS submission or edit was made.

Every even determinant of an integer centrosymmetric matrix with odd central entry (when applicable) is divisible by $4$. These observations do not resolve the conjecture that $a(n)$ is always odd.
