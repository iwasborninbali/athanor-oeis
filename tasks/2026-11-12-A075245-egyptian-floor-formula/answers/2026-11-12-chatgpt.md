# OEIS A075245: Status, Counterexample, and Computational Verification

## 1. Status: Refuted

**Finding:** The conjectured formula for OEIS A075245 is false. The smallest counterexample found is $n=409$, within the range already covered by the OEIS b-file. There is also a separate ambiguity in the sequence definition concerning ties.

### The published conjecture

M. F. Hasler, July 2, 2022, *OEIS A075245*, Formula section, writes:

> “Conjecture: a(n) = floor(n/4) + d”

The entry specifies $d=1$, except for certain $n\equiv1\pmod8$, where $d=2$. Its Comments section additionally claims this pattern holds through $n=2000$.

The precise mathematical assertion to test is therefore

$$
\boxed{
a(n)\in\left\{
\left\lfloor\frac n4\right\rfloor+1,
\left\lfloor\frac n4\right\rfloor+2
\right\}\quad(n\ge3),
}
$$

with the second value permitted only when $n\equiv1\pmod8$.

This assertion is refuted below.

### Definition and tie ambiguity

For each $n\ge3$ having a solution, define

$$
M(n)=\max\left\{z:
\frac4n=\frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z,\quad
1\le x<y<z,\quad x,y,z\in\mathbb Z
\right\}.
$$

To make $a(n)$ unambiguous, use the tie-breaking convention

$$
a(n)=\min\{x:\exists y,\ (x,y,M(n))\text{ is a solution}\}.
$$

This agrees with the OEIS Maple implementation, which iterates over increasing $x$ and updates its answer only for a strictly larger $z$.

The convention matters. For example, at $n=17$, both

$$
(5,30,510),\qquad (6,15,510)
$$

attain the maximum $z=510$. Thus the informal definition of A075245 does not always determine a unique $x$.

### Source and literature status

Sources examined include:

- T. D. Noe (2002), *OEIS A075245*, sequence originally introduced; M. F. Hasler (2022), Comments, Formula, and computational table.
- *OEIS A073101*, concerning all distinct-denominator solutions; *A075246* and *A075247*, recording the associated $y,z$.
- Thomas Bloom, *Erdős Problems*, Problem 242, concerning the broader Erdős–Straus existence conjecture, and the OEIS wiki's Erdős–Straus discussion.
- Web searches for A075245, maximum-denominator Egyptian-fraction results, arXiv papers, and MathSciNet/zbMATH-indexed material.

## 2. Counterexample: $n=409$

**Proved:** The maximizing $x$ cannot equal either of the two values allowed by the conjecture.

**Computed independently:** The actual maximizing triple is

$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=(105,\ 3906,\ 7987770).}
$$

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{a(409)=105
=\left\lfloor\frac{409}{4}\right\rfloor+3,}
$$

whereas the conjecture permits only $103$ or $104$.

### Exact divisor reduction

For a fixed $n,x$, write

$$
A=4x-n,\qquad B=nx.
$$

Then

$$
\frac4n-\frac1x=\frac AB.
$$

The remaining equation

$$
\frac AB=\frac1y+\frac1z
$$

is equivalent to

$$
(Ay-B)(Az-B)=B^2.
$$

Let $k=Ay-B$. Every solution with $0<x<y<z$ satisfies

$$
\boxed{
k\mid B^2,\qquad
k\equiv-B\pmod A,\qquad
0<k<B,
}
$$

and

$$
y=\frac{B+k}{A},\qquad
z=\frac{B(B+k)}{Ak}.
$$

Conversely, any positive $k<B$ satisfying those divisibility conditions and producing integral $y,z$ with $x<y$ gives a valid solution.

For fixed $x$,

$$
z=\frac BA+\frac{B^2}{Ak}
$$

is strictly decreasing as $k$ increases. Therefore, the smallest admissible $k$ gives the largest $z$ for that $x$.

### Case $x=103$: impossible

Since $x>n/4=102.25$, $103$ is the smallest possible first denominator.

For $n=409,\ x=103$,

$$
A=3,\qquad B=409\cdot103=42127.
$$

Both $103$ and $409$ are primes congruent to $1\pmod3$. Hence every divisor of $B^2$ is congruent to $1\pmod3$.

But an admissible $k$ would have to satisfy

$$
k\equiv-42127\equiv2\pmod3.
$$

This is impossible.

Thus **no solution exists with $x=103$**.

### Case $x=104$: maximum $z=4976712$

Here

$$
A=7,\qquad
B=409\cdot104=42536=2^3\cdot13\cdot409.
$$

Any admissible $k$ must be a divisor of

$$
B^2=2^6\cdot13^2\cdot409^2
$$

and satisfy $k\equiv3\pmod7$.

The positive integers below $52$ in that residue class are

$$
3,10,17,24,31,38,45.
$$

None divides $B^2$. Thus $k\ge52$.

At $k=52$,

$$
y=\frac{42536+52}{7}=6084,
$$

$$
z=\frac{42536(42536+52)}{7\cdot52}
=4976712.
$$

The triple $(104,6084,4976712)$ is valid. Since $z$ decreases with $k$, this is the maximum for $x=104$.

### Case $x=105$: a strictly better solution

Here

$$
A=11,\qquad B=409\cdot105=42945.
$$

Choose $k=21$. Then

$$
y=\frac{42945+21}{11}=3906,
$$

$$
z=\frac{42945(42945+21)}{11\cdot21}
=7987770.
$$

Thus the exact identity

$$
\boxed{
\frac4{409}
=\frac1{105}+\frac1{3906}+\frac1{7987770}
}
$$

holds, with

$$
105<3906<7987770.
$$

Moreover,

$$
7987770>4976712.
$$

We have now excluded $x=103$ entirely and shown that $x=104$ cannot yield the global maximum.

**Therefore the conjecture is disproved by a finite arithmetic certificate, independently of any exhaustive computer search.**

The exhaustive calculation in Section 3 additionally establishes that no $x\ge106$ gives a larger $z$, so the actual A075245 value under the specified tie-breaking convention is $a(409)=105$.

## 3. Computational verification

Two independent integer-arithmetic implementations were used.

### A. Exhaustive search for $3\le n\le500$

The search bounds are mathematically exhaustive:

$$
\frac n4<x<\frac{3n}{4},\qquad
\max\left(x,\frac BA\right)<y<\frac{2B}{A}.
$$

For each admissible pair $(x,y)$, the only possible third denominator is

$$
z=\frac{By}{Ay-B}.
$$

The following Python function checks every possible pair, without floating-point arithmetic.

```python

def maximum(n):
    best_z, winners, pairs = 0, [], 0

    for x in range(n // 4 + 1, (3*n - 1) // 4 + 1):
        A, B = 4*x - n, n*x
        lo = max(x + 1, B // A + 1)
        hi = (2*B - 1) // A

        for y in range(lo, hi + 1):
            pairs += 1
            D = A*y - B
            if (B*y) % D == 0:
                z = B*y // D
                if z > y:
                    if z > best_z:
                        best_z = z
                        winners = [(x, y, z)]
                    elif z == best_z:
                        winners.append((x, y, z))

    return winners, pairs
```

## 4. Verdict

**The A075245 floor-formula conjecture is refuted with mathematical certainty.** At $n=409$, an elementary divisibility argument proves that neither of its proposed values for $x$ can maximize $z$; the counterexample $(105,3906,7987770)$ requires no computational assumption. Independent exhaustive search additionally establishes that $a(409)=105$ and that 409 is the first counterexample through 500. The extended exact computation finds ten violations through 2000. These results identify a contradiction in the existing OEIS formula and an ambiguity in its tie-breaking convention; they do not establish priority for a previously unknown numerical discovery. Before an OEIS correction, the existing b-file should be checked against these results, the incorrect formula and empirical comment should be revised, and the tie-breaking rule should be stated explicitly.
