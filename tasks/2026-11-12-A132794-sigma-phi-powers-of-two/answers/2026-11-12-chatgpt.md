# OEIS A132794 — Status and Proof Investigation

**Investigation date:** 12 November 2026 **Subject:** Jon Maiga's conjecture concerning Euler's totient function, the divisor-sum function, and Mersenne primes.

## 1. Status and literature search

**Verdict: Apparently open.** I found no published proof or counterexample to the full conjecture. There is, however, a known and elementary characterization of all solutions that are powers of two. The unresolved part is whether solutions with other prime factors exist.

### Precise statement

Let $\sigma(n)$ denote the sum of positive divisors of $n$, and let $\varphi(n)$ denote Euler's totient. Define Chowla's function

$$
C(n)=\sigma(n)-n-1
=\sum_{\substack{d\mid n\\1<d<n}}d.
$$

OEIS A132794 consists of positive integers satisfying

$$
\boxed{C(\varphi(n))=\varphi(C(n)),}
\tag{1}
$$

with $C(n)>0$, since $\varphi(0)$ is not defined in the standard number-theoretic convention.

Thus only composite integers are eligible.

The conjecture is the set equality

$$
\boxed{
\{n\geq4:C(\varphi(n))=\varphi(C(n))\}
=
\{2^{p+1}:2^p-1\text{ is prime}\}.
}
\tag{2}
$$

Here $p$ ranges over Mersenne-prime exponents (OEIS A000043).

### Findings from the sources

**Jon Maiga, 2018.** In *Euler Phi, Chowla and Mersenne primes*, published on his personal mathematical website on 13 December 2018, Maiga proves that every Mersenne-prime exponent produces a solution. His argument also characterizes the solutions within powers of two.

The decisive result is the reduction

$$
2^{k-1}-2=\varphi(2^{k-1}-1),
$$

which holds precisely when $2^{k-1}-1$ is prime, for $k\geq3$. This is already known material, not a new result of this investigation.

**OEIS A132794, 2007–2026.** The entry was created by Paolo P. Lava and Giorgio Balzarotti on 31 August 2007. Its comment by Jon Maiga dated 14 December 2018 explicitly states:

> Conjecture: a(n)=2^(A000043(n)+1)

The entry still displays eight terms and retains the `hard` keyword. Giovanni Resta's comment of 1 December 2019 gives the search bound $a(9)>6.5\cdot10^{11}$. The live entry continues to present the equality as a conjecture, rather than a theorem.

**Related OEIS entries and literature.** I checked A132793, A048050, A000043, A033632, A001229, A018784, and related work on compositions of $\sigma$ and $\varphi$. One relevant reference is Jean-Marie De Koninck and Florian Luca, *On the composition of the Euler function and the sum of divisors function*, *Colloquium Mathematicum* 108(1) (2007), 31–51. That paper concerns the compositions $\sigma(\varphi(n))$ and $\varphi(\sigma(n))$, not a demonstrated resolution of (2).

**Recent-preprint search.** I searched indexed arXiv and Zenodo results by `A132794`, by the defining equation, and by combinations of *Chowla function*, *Euler totient*, *divisor sum*, and *Mersenne*, with specific searches covering 15 October–12 November 2026. I found no relevant preprint resolving the conjecture. I also searched web-indexed MathSciNet and zbMATH-related material, but did not perform a comprehensive authenticated database search.

These negative findings are evidence of the current status, not proof that no unpublished or unindexed solution exists.

**Classification:** Open as far as the checked sources establish; not known to be refuted or equivalent to a named open problem. It involves Mersenne primes, but proving (2) would not itself settle whether infinitely many Mersenne primes exist.

## 2. Proof and partial results

### 2.1. Complete characterization of powers of two

**Status: Proved; known since Maiga (2018).**

For every integer $k\geq3$,

$$
\boxed{
2^k\in A132794
\iff
2^{k-1}-1\text{ is prime}.
}
\tag{3}
$$

**Proof.** We have

$$
\varphi(2^k)=2^{k-1},
\qquad C(2^j)=2^j-2 \quad(j\geq1).
$$

Consequently,

$$
C(\varphi(2^k))=2^{k-1}-2
$$

and

$$
\varphi(C(2^k))
=\varphi(2^k-2)
=\varphi\bigl(2(2^{k-1}-1)\bigr).
$$

Since $2^{k-1}-1$ is odd, multiplicativity of $\varphi$ gives

$$
\varphi\bigl(2(2^{k-1}-1)\bigr)
=\varphi(2^{k-1}-1).
$$

Put $m=2^{k-1}-1$. Since $k\geq3$, we have $m\geq3$. Equation (1) becomes

$$
\varphi(m)=m-1.
$$

For every integer $m>1$, this equality holds if and only if $m$ is prime: if $m$ is composite, it has a proper divisor greater than 1, which is not relatively prime to $m$.

Thus (3) follows. The smaller powers $2$ and $4$ are not solutions: the former makes $\varphi(C(n))$ undefined, while the latter gives $0\neq1$. $\square$

This proves the conjecture's inclusion in one direction and excludes every other power of two. It does not exclude integers with odd prime factors.

### 2.2. A necessary parity condition

**Status: Proved; elementary deduction.**

**Lemma.** Every solution $n$ of (1) satisfies

$$
\boxed{
\varphi(n)=u^2
\quad\text{or}\quad
\varphi(n)=2u^2
}
\tag{4}
$$

for some positive integer $u$.

**Proof.** The integer $4$ is not a solution. Every other composite $n\geq6$ has $C(n)>2$, so $\varphi(C(n))$ is even.

Also $\varphi(n)$ is even. Thus equation (1) implies

$$
\sigma(\varphi(n))-\varphi(n)-1\equiv0\pmod2,
$$

so $\sigma(\varphi(n))$ is odd.

For any positive integer $m$, $\sigma(m)$ is odd precisely when the odd part of $m$ is a square: even divisors contribute zero modulo 2, and an odd integer has an odd number of divisors exactly when it is a square.

Equivalently, $m$ must be a square or twice a square. Taking $m=\varphi(n)$ proves (4). $\square$

This gives a useful necessary condition for any counterexample to the conjecture. It is not sufficient.

### 2.3. No odd prime power is a solution

**Status: Proved.**

**Proposition.** If $p$ is an odd prime and $a\geq2$, then

$$
\boxed{
C(\varphi(p^a))>\varphi(C(p^a)).
}
\tag{5}
$$

**Proof.** Since

$$
\varphi(p^a)=p^{a-1}(p-1),
$$

each of

$$
p,p^2,\ldots,p^{a-1},p-1
$$

is a proper divisor of $\varphi(p^a)$, greater than 1. These divisors are distinct because $p-1$ is not a power of $p$.

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
C(\varphi(p^a))
&\geq p+p^2+\cdots+p^{a-1}+(p-1)\\
&=C(p^a)+(p-1)\\
&>C(p^a)\\
&\geq\varphi(C(p^a)).
\end{aligned}
$$

This proves (5). Odd primes themselves are excluded because $C(p)=0$. $\square$

Together with (3), this completely classifies the prime-power solutions:

$$
\boxed{
n\text{ a prime power and }n\in A132794
\iff
n=2^{p+1},\quad 2^p-1\text{ prime}.
}
\tag{6}
$$

### 2.4. No odd integer with at most three prime factors is a solution

**Status: Proved.** Prime factors here are counted with multiplicity, so $\Omega(n)\leq3$.

The cases involving one distinct prime factor are covered by Proposition 2.3. For the remaining cases, write $C=C(n)$.

**Case A: $n=pq$, with distinct odd primes $p,q$.**

Here $C=p+q$, which is even, and

$$
\varphi(n)=(p-1)(q-1).
$$

Both $p-1$ and $q-1$ are distinct, nontrivial proper divisors of $\varphi(n)$. Hence

$$
C(\varphi(n))
\geq p+q-2
>\frac{p+q}{2}
\geq\varphi(C).
$$

**Case B: $n=p^2q$, with distinct odd primes $p,q$.**

We have

$$
C=p+p^2+q+pq.
$$

This is even. Furthermore, $p(p-1)$ and $p(q-1)$ are distinct nontrivial proper divisors of

$$
\varphi(n)=p(p-1)(q-1).
$$

Therefore

$$
C(\varphi(n))\geq p(p-1)+p(q-1)
=p(p+q-2).
$$

Direct subtraction gives

$$
2p(p+q-2)-C
=p^2+pq-5p-q.
$$

If $p=3$, then $q\geq5$ and this is $2q-6>0$. If $p\geq5$, then

$$
p^2+pq-5p-q=p(p-5)+q(p-1)>0.
$$

Consequently,

$$
C(\varphi(n))>\frac C2\geq\varphi(C).
$$

**Case C: $n=pqr$, with distinct odd primes $3\leq p<q<r$.**

Now

$$
C=p+q+r+pq+pr+qr,
$$

which is even.

The three numbers

$$
(p-1)(q-1),\quad
(p-1)(r-1),\quad
(q-1)(r-1)
$$

are distinct nontrivial proper divisors of $\varphi(n)$. Thus, with $S$ denoting their sum,

$$
C(\varphi(n))\geq S.
$$

Expanding,

$$
2S-C=pq+pr+qr-5(p+q+r)+6.
$$

The right-hand side is increasing in each variable throughout $p\geq3,\ q\geq5,\ r\geq7$, and its minimum occurs at $(3,5,7)$, where it equals $2$. Hence $2S>C$, giving

$$
C(\varphi(n))>\frac C2\geq\varphi(C).
$$

All possibilities for an odd composite number with $\Omega(n)\leq3$ have now been excluded. $\square$

**Corollary.** Any counterexample to the proposed classification must be either an even integer with an odd prime factor, or an odd integer with at least four prime factors counted with multiplicity.

The parity condition (4) applies in both cases.

These elementary exclusions were derived during this investigation. I did not find them stated in the checked A132794 sources, but I make no claim of publication priority or mathematical novelty beyond those sources.

## 3. Computational verification

**Status: Computed.** An exhaustive search was performed for

$$
\boxed{4\leq n\leq10,000,000.}
$$

The program independently constructs two arithmetic-function tables:

- $C(n)$, by adding every divisor $d\geq2$ to its proper multiples.
- $\varphi(n)$, using the standard prime sieve.

It tests equation (1), verifies the parity equivalence used in Lemma 2.2, and counts the signs of the difference between both sides for odd and even composite integers.

### C++17 program

```

#include <bits/stdc++.h>
using namespace std;

int main(int argc, char** argv) {
    uint32_t N = argc > 1 ? stoul(argv[1]) : 10000000;

    vector<uint32_t> ch(N + 1, 0);
    for (uint32_t d = 2; d <= N / 2; ++d)
        for (uint32_t j = 2*d; j <= N; j += d)
            ch[j] += d;

    uint32_t maxch = *max_element(ch.begin(), ch.end());
    uint32_t M = max(N, maxch);

    vector<uint32_t> phi(M + 1);
    iota(phi.begin(), phi.end(), 0u);
    for (uint32_t p = 2; p <= M; ++p)
        if (phi[p] == p)
            for (uint32_t j = p; j <= M; j += p)
                phi[j] -= phi[j] / p;
    phi[1] = 1;

    vector<uint8_t> sq2(N + 1, 0);
    for (uint64_t k = 1; k*k <= N; ++k) {
        sq2[k*k] = 1;
        if (2*k*k <= N) sq2[2*k*k] = 1;
    }

    uint64_t composite = 0, parity_even = 0;
    uint64_t mismatches = 0;
    uint64_t odd_gt = 0, odd_lt = 0;
    uint64_t even_gt = 0, even_lt = 0;
    uint32_t odd_lt_first = 0;
    vector<uint32_t> solutions;

    for (uint32_t n = 4; n <= N; ++n) {
        uint32_t s = ch[n];
        if (s == 0) continue;

        ++composite;
        uint32_t lhs = ch[phi[n]];
        uint32_t rhs = phi[s];

        bool even = !(lhs & 1);
        if (even) ++parity_even;
        if (even != bool(sq2[phi[n]])) ++mismatches;

        if (lhs == rhs) {
            solutions.push_back(n);
        } else if (n & 1) {
            if (lhs > rhs) ++odd_gt;
            else {
                ++odd_lt;
                if (!odd_lt_first) odd_lt_first = n;
            }
        } else {
            if (lhs > rhs) ++even_gt;
            else ++even_lt;
        }
    }

    cout << "N=" << N
         << " max_ch=" << maxch
         << " composite=" << composite
         << " parity_even=" << parity_even
         << " parity_mismatches=" << mismatches << "\n";

    cout << "solutions=";
    for (size_t i = 0; i < solutions.size(); ++i)
        cout << (i ? "," : "") << solutions[i];
    cout << "\n";

    cout << "odd_gt=" << odd_gt
         << " odd_lt=" << odd_lt
         << " odd_lt_first=" << odd_lt_first << "\n";

    cout << "even_gt=" << even_gt
         << " even_lt=" << even_lt << "\n";
}
```

**Status: Open, with rigorously proved partial results.** The characterization of powers of two by Mersenne-prime exponents is known from Jon Maiga (2018), and no proof or counterexample to the full OEIS A132794 conjecture was found in the sources checked through 12 November 2026, including indexed searches for recent preprints. This investigation establishes elementary necessary conditions and exclusions: every solution has a totient that is a square or twice a square; all prime-power solutions have the conjectured form; and no odd integer with at most three prime factors, counted with multiplicity, can be a solution. The computation confirms all seven solutions up to $10^7$ and finds no odd composite for which $C(\varphi(n))\leq\varphi(C(n))$ in that range.
