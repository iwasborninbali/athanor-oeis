# OEIS A048211 — Status and proof attempt for the resistance growth-ratio conjecture

**Investigation date:** November 12, 2026 **Sequence:** [OEIS A048211](https://oeis.org/A048211) **Task:** [Athanor TASK.md](https://github.com/iwasborninbali/athanor-oeis/blob/main/tasks/2026-11-12-A048211-resistance-growth-ratio/TASK.md)

## 1. Status

**Verdict: Open, as far as the literature located in this investigation establishes.** The conjecture is mathematically well-posed once the constant is specified as an unknown positive real number. I found neither a proof nor a counterexample, nor an established equivalence to a named open problem.

### Precise statement

Let $R_n$ be the set of effective resistances obtainable using exactly $n$ identical one-ohm resistors, with series and parallel operations only, and let

$$
a(n)=|R_n|.
$$

The conjecture is that there exists a real constant $K>0$ such that

$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a(n)}{a(n-1)}=K,}
$$

with $K$ numerically estimated to be approximately $2.54$.

The original OEIS formulation, attributed to Bill McEachen on June 8, 2024, is:

> “Conjecture: a(n) ~ K * a(n-1), K approx 2.54.”

It remains labelled a conjecture in the live entry.

This is distinct from the stronger assertion $a(n)\sim C K^n$ for some $C>0$. Convergence of consecutive ratios does not imply that stronger asymptotic.

### Literature and status evidence

**Sameen Ahmed Khan (2010).** *The bounds of the set of equivalent resistances of n equal resistors combined in series and in parallel*, arXiv:1004.3346, April 20, 2010.

Khan developed upper bounds using Farey sequences and Fibonacci numbers. The abstract explicitly calls $a(n)\sim 2.55^n$ an approximate formula obtained from finite computations, not a proved asymptotic.

**Sameen Ahmed Khan (2012, 2016).** Relevant papers include *Farey sequences and resistor networks*, *Proceedings of the Indian Academy of Sciences (Mathematical Sciences)* **122** (2012), 153–162, and *Beginning to Count the Number of Equivalent Resistances*, *Indian Journal of Science and Technology* **9**(44) (2016), 1–7. These are cited in the OEIS bibliography; the established results concern counting methods and bounds, not the claimed ratio limit.

**Mehdi Nikopour Deilami and Bohdan Zhelyabovskyy (2024).** *On the Average Resistance of n-circuits*, arXiv:2410.07261, October 8, 2024, studies the average resistance over series-parallel circuit structures. Its asymptotic conjecture concerns that average, not the number of distinct resistance values.

**Sameen Ahmed Khan (2025).** *Number theoretic techniques in the sets of equal resistor networks*, *Far East Journal of Mathematical Sciences* **142**(4), 677–696, published online October 2, 2025, DOI [10.17654/0972087125037](https://doi.org/10.17654/0972087125037).

This is the most directly relevant recent published treatment I located. Theorem 1 gives a lower exponential bound, and the later Farey-sequence argument supplies a stronger upper exponential bound. The paper's concluding remarks explicitly state that finite numerical results:

> “provide no clues to the behaviour for large n.”

The author consequently presents the approximate $2.53^n$ growth as numerical evidence, not a proved limiting rate.

**Related OEIS entries.** A176497 counts the cross-set contributions beyond the obvious series and parallel extensions, and A176502 supplies a Farey-based upper bound for series-parallel circuits. A051389 counts resistance values that first appear at a given resistor count. None of these entries establishes the ratio conjecture.

### Established asymptotic bounds

Writing $\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$, the cited results establish the exponential bounds

$$
\frac14(1+\sqrt2)^n<a(n)
$$

and, asymptotically,

$$
a(n)=O(\varphi^{2n}).
$$

In particular,

$$
\boxed{
1+\sqrt2
\leq \liminf_{n\to\infty}a(n)^{1/n}
\leq \limsup_{n\to\infty}a(n)^{1/n}
\leq \varphi^2.
}
$$

Numerically, the endpoints are approximately $2.414213562$ and $2.618033989$. These are **known bounds**, not new results of this investigation.

If the conjectured ratio limit exists, it necessarily satisfies

$$
\boxed{2.414213562\ldots\le K\le 2.618033989\ldots.}
$$

*Proof of the conditional statement:* if $a(n)/a(n-1)\to K>0$, taking logarithms of the telescoping product

$$
a(n)=a(1)\prod_{j=2}^{n}\frac{a(j)}{a(j-1)}
$$

and applying Cesàro averaging gives $a(n)^{1/n}\to K$. Apply the established root bounds.

## 2. Proof attempt and partial results

### 2.1 Exact recursive formulation

For positive rationals $x,y$, define

$$
S(x,y)=x+y,\qquad
P(x,y)=\frac{xy}{x+y}.
$$

The sets satisfy

$$
R_1=\{1\},
$$

and, for $n\ge2$,

$$
\boxed{
R_n=
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n-1}
\left\{
S(x,y),P(x,y):
x\in R_i,\ y\in R_{n-i}
\right\}.
}
$$

**Status: Proved.** Every series-parallel network with at least two resistors has a top-level series or parallel decomposition into two nonempty networks. Conversely, combining any such pair produces a permitted $n$-resistor network.

This recurrence is also the standard exhaustive construction recorded in OEIS A048211.

### 2.2 A universal lower bound on consecutive growth

For every $x\in R_n$, both

$$
f(x)=1+x,\qquad g(x)=\frac{x}{1+x}
$$

belong to $R_{n+1}$.

Both maps are injective on positive rationals. Moreover,

$$
f(x)>1,\qquad 0<g(x)<1.
$$

Their images are therefore disjoint, each of cardinality $a(n)$.

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{a(n+1)\ge2a(n)\quad(n\ge1).}
$$

**Status: Proved, known.** This is the series/parallel-set decomposition discussed in OEIS A176497.

It follows that every consecutive ratio is at least $2$, although this alone is far from proving convergence.

### 2.3 Exact occurrence of unit resistance

The following elementary structural result can be proved without relying on asymptotic assumptions.

**Proposition.** For the sets $R_n$,

$$
\boxed{
1\in R_n
\iff n=1,\ n=4,\ \text{or }n\ge6.
}
$$

**Proof.**

First, construct one-ohm networks at three starting sizes, using $\parallel$ for the parallel operation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n=4:\quad&
(1+1)\parallel(1+1)=1,\\[2mm]
n=6:\quad&
(1+1+1)\parallel\bigl(1+(1\parallel1)\bigr)
=3\parallel\frac32=1,\\[2mm]
n=8:\quad&
(1+1+1+1)\parallel
\bigl(1+(1\parallel1\parallel1)\bigr)
=4\parallel\frac43=1.
\end{aligned}
$$

Each expression uses exactly the indicated number of unit resistors.

The four-resistor realization of $1$ can replace any individual unit resistor in an existing circuit without changing its effective resistance. This increases the resistor count by exactly three.

Starting at $n=4$ therefore also gives $1\in R_7$. Starting at $n=6,7,8$ and repeatedly adding three covers every $n\ge6$.

For the exclusions, the cases $n=2,3$ follow immediately from the recurrence. For $n=5$, the top-level split is either $1+4$ or $2+3$ resistors. A split involving a single one-ohm resistor cannot give one ohm under either operation.

For a $2+3$ split, the relevant sets are

$$
R_2=\left\{\frac12,2\right\},
\qquad
R_3=\left\{\frac13,\frac23,\frac32,3\right\}.
$$

A series connection yielding $1$ would require $1/2+1/2$, and a parallel connection yielding $1$ would require $2\parallel2$. Neither is possible with the available three-resistor component. Thus $1\notin R_5$, completing the proof. $\square$

A further exact consequence follows from the reciprocal symmetry of $R_n$.

**Lemma.** If $x\in R_n$, then $1/x\in R_n$.

*Proof.* Swap every series connection with a parallel connection. Inductively, the effective resistance is inverted, since

$$
P(1/x,1/y)=\frac1{x+y},
\qquad
S(1/x,1/y)=\frac1{P(x,y)}.
$$

The unit resistor is unchanged. $\square$

Every resistance other than $1$ consequently belongs to a two-element reciprocal pair. The proposition gives the exact parity classification

$$
\boxed{
a(n)\text{ is odd}
\iff n=1,\ n=4,\ \text{or }n\ge6.
}
$$

It also strengthens the elementary growth inequality to

$$
\boxed{a(n+1)\ge2a(n)+1\quad(n\ge5).}
$$

**Status:** These statements are proved here by explicit constructions and induction. No claim of priority or novelty is made; they do not resolve the limiting-ratio conjecture.

### 2.4 Audit of a published recurrence inequality

There is a small but definite error in the formulation of a lower-bound inequality in the 2025 paper.

On page 685, equation (15), Khan states

$$
a(n)>2a(n-1)+a(n-2),\qquad n\ge6.
$$

But the exact published values give

$$
a(6)=53=2(22)+9=2a(5)+a(4).
$$

Therefore **the printed strict inequality is false at $n=6$**.

The strict inequality holds for every $7\le n\le18$ in the exact computations below. This does not establish it for all $n\ge7$, nor does the boundary-case error refute the paper's exponential lower bound or the OEIS conjecture.

The main obstruction remains the number of overlaps among different circuit constructions. In the terminology of OEIS A176497,

$$
a(n+1)=2a(n)+c(n+1),
$$

where $c(n+1)$ counts resistance values outside the two obvious extension sets. Establishing sufficiently strong asymptotic control of $c(n)$ would be one possible route toward proving ratio convergence. No such control is proved here.

## 3. Computational check

**Status: Exact computation for $1\le n\le18$.**

I implemented the recursive set construction in C++17, using reduced numerator–denominator pairs. Integers are packed into 64-bit keys, and every result is reduced by its greatest common divisor.

The program was compiled with `g++ -O3 -std=c++17` and executed successfully. It independently reproduces the OEIS counts through $n=18$, checks reciprocal closure, checks the unit-resistance proposition, and evaluates the exceptional published inequality.

### Reproducible program

```
#include <algorithm>
#include <cstdint>
#include <iomanip>
#include <iostream>
#include <numeric>
#include <unordered_set>
#include <vector>
using namespace std;

using U = uint64_t;

U enc(unsigned p, unsigned q) {
    auto d = gcd(p,q);
    return (U(p/d)<<32) | (q/d);
}

int main() {
    const int N=18;
    vector<vector<U>> R(N+1);
    R[1]={enc(1,1)};

    cout << "n count cross one ratio\n1 1 - 1 -\n";

    for (int n=2; n<=N; ++n) {
        unordered_set<U> T;

        for (int i=1; i<=n/2; ++i)
            for (U x:R[i]) for (U y:R[n-i]) {
                unsigned a=x>>32,b=(uint32_t)x;
                unsigned c=y>>32,d=(uint32_t)y;
                unsigned s=a*d+c*b;

                T.insert(enc(s,b*d)); // series
                T.insert(enc(a*c,s)); // parallel
            }

        R[n].assign(T.begin(),T.end());

        // Reciprocal closure.
        for (U x:R[n])
            if (!T.count(enc((uint32_t)x,x>>32)))
                return 1;

        // Exact unit-resistance characterization.
        if (bool(T.count(enc(1,1))) !=
            (n==4 || n>=6))
            return 2;

        cout << n << ' ' << T.size() << ' '
             << T.size()-2*R[n-1].size() << ' '
             << T.count(enc(1,1)) << ' '
             << fixed << setprecision(9)
             << double(T.size())/R[n-1].size()
             << '\n';
    }

    cout << "reciprocity n=2..18: PASS\n";
    cout << "unit-resistance theorem n=1..18: PASS\n";
    cout << "Khan (15), strict lower at n=6: "
         << R[6].size() << " > "
         << 2*R[5].size()+R[4].size()
         << " is " << boolalpha
         << (R[6].size() >
             2*R[5].size()+R[4].size())
         << '\n';
}
```

## 4. Verdict

**Open; no proof or counterexample to the limiting-ratio conjecture was found or obtained.** The claim is well-posed as the existence of a finite positive limit of $a(n)/a(n-1)$, and the rigorous published exponential bounds confine any such limit to $[1+\sqrt2,\varphi^2]$. The independently executed exact enumeration confirms the first 18 terms and the elementary structural results proved above. This report additionally identifies a boundary-case error at $n=6$ in the strict recurrence inequality printed in Khan (2025), but that does not settle the conjecture. The parity characterization is proved here without a claim of mathematical priority. Confidence is high in the elementary proofs, computations, and identified boundary-case error; the open-status conclusion is qualified by the stated literature-search limitations. The known bounds imply that any such limit lies between 1+sqrt(2) and phi²; the computed ratio at n=30 is approximately 2.54394234.”* No OEIS submission or repository modification was made.
