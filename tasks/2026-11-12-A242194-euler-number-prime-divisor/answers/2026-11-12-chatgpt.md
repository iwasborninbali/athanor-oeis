## 1. Status

### Precise statement

Let $E_{2n}$ denote the classical signed Euler numbers defined by

$$
\frac{1}{\cosh t}
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{2n}\frac{t^{2n}}{(2n)!}.
$$

For $n\ge1$, define

$$
P_n=\{p\text{ prime}:p\mid E_{2n},
\quad p\nmid E_{2k}\ \text{for all }1\le k<n\}.
$$

The sequence is

$$
a(n)=
\begin{cases}
\min P_n,&P_n\ne\varnothing,\\
1,&P_n=\varnothing.
\end{cases}
$$

Sun's conjecture is equivalent to

$$
\boxed{\forall n\ge2,\quad P_n\ne\varnothing.}
$$

Thus the conjecture asserts that every Euler number $E_{2n}$, $n\ge2$, possesses a prime divisor absent from all preceding nonconstant even-indexed Euler numbers.

The assertion that $a(n)$ is prime is not automatic: the definition permits $a(n)=1$.

### Original source

**Zhi-Wei Sun**, *New Observations on Primitive Roots Modulo Primes*, *Nanjing University Journal of Mathematics Biquarterly* **36** (2019), no. 2, pp. 108–133, §5, Conjecture 5.2.

The decisive passage is explicitly labeled *Conjecture 5.2* and states that

> the Euler number $E_{2n}$ has a prime divisor $p$ not dividing any $E_{2k}$ with $0<k<n$.

The quantifier in the original covers every $n=2,3,\ldots$.

The work originated as Sun's 2014 preprint [arXiv:1405.0290](https://arxiv.org/abs/1405.0290).

## 2. Proof attempt and partial results

The full conjecture remains unresolved by the arguments below. Nevertheless, the following statements can be proved.

### Theorem 1 — A congruence for Euler numbers

**Proved.** For every odd prime $p$ and integer $n\ge0$,

$$
\boxed{
E_{2n}\equiv
\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(-1)^j(2j+1)^{2n}\pmod p.
}
\tag{1}
$$

Here, when $n=0$, the zeroth power is interpreted as $1$, including at residues divisible by $p$.

**Proof.**

Introduce the Euler polynomials $\mathcal E_m(x)$ by

$$
\frac{2e^{xt}}{e^t+1}
=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathcal E_m(x)\frac{t^m}{m!}.
$$

They satisfy

$$
\mathcal E_m(x)+\mathcal E_m(x+1)=2x^m
\tag{2}
$$

and

$$
E_{2n}=2^{2n}\mathcal E_{2n}(1/2).
\tag{3}
$$

Their coefficients lie in $\mathbb Z[1/2]$. Therefore, for an odd prime $p$,

$$
\mathcal E_m(x+p)\equiv\mathcal E_m(x)\pmod p
$$

whenever $x$ is a rational number whose denominator is not divisible by $p$.

Multiply (2), with $x$ replaced by $x+j$, by $(-1)^j$, and sum from $j=0$ to $p-1$.

The intermediate polynomial terms cancel:

$$
2\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(-1)^j(x+j)^m
=\mathcal E_m(x)+\mathcal E_m(x+p).
$$

Since $p$ is odd,

$$
\mathcal E_m(x)
\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(-1)^j(x+j)^m\pmod p.
$$

Taking $m=2n$, $x=1/2$, and multiplying by $2^{2n}$ gives (1). $\square$

### Theorem 2 — Every primitive prime divisor is large

**Proved.** If $p$ is a primitive prime divisor of $E_{2n}$, then

$$
\boxed{p\ge2n+1.}
\tag{4}
$$

**Proof.**

For $n\ge1$, Fermat's little theorem applied to (1) yields

$$
E_{2(n+(p-1)/2)}
\equiv E_{2n}\pmod p.
\tag{5}
$$

Indeed, the nonzero bases $2j+1$ have multiplicative order dividing $p-1$, and the zero bases contribute zero for positive exponents.

Hence the sequence

$$
E_2,E_4,E_6,\ldots\pmod p
$$

is periodic with period dividing $(p-1)/2$.

Every occurrence of zero modulo $p$ at a positive index must therefore have an occurrence among the first $(p-1)/2$ positive indices.

If $p$ first divides $E_{2n}$ at index $n$, then

$$
n\le\frac{p-1}{2},
$$

which is equivalent to (4). $\square$

Consequently, every term of A242194 satisfies

$$
\boxed{a(n)=1\quad\text{or}\quad a(n)\ge2n+1.}
$$

This is a necessary condition, not a proof that the second alternative always occurs.

### Theorem 3 — Infinitely many terms of A242194 are prime

**Proved.** There are infinitely many positive integers $n\ge2$ for which $a(n)$ is prime.

The key congruence is

$$
\boxed{
E_{p-1}\equiv1-(-1)^{(p-1)/2}\pmod p
}
\tag{6}
$$

for every odd prime $p$.

**Proof of (6).**

Set $2n=p-1$ in (1):

$$
E_{p-1}
\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(-1)^j(2j+1)^{p-1}\pmod p.
$$

Exactly one residue in the sum is zero modulo $p$, namely the term indexed by

$$
j=\frac{p-1}{2}.
$$

By Fermat's little theorem, each remaining power equals $1$ modulo $p$. Therefore

$$
E_{p-1}
\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(-1)^j
-(-1)^{(p-1)/2}
\pmod p.
$$

The alternating sum of $p$ terms is $1$, establishing (6). $\square$

In particular,

$$
\boxed{
p\equiv1\pmod4\implies p\mid E_{p-1}.
}
\tag{7}
$$

Now Dirichlet's theorem guarantees infinitely many primes $p\equiv1\pmod4$.

For each such prime, (7) guarantees that $p$ divides at least one even-indexed Euler number. Let $n(p)$ be the smallest positive index at which this happens.

Since $E_2=-1$,

$$
n(p)\ge2.
$$

By definition, $p$ is a primitive prime divisor of $E_{2n(p)}$.

Suppose only finitely many indices arose this way, say

$$
n(p)\in\{n_1,\ldots,n_r\}.
$$

Then every prime $p\equiv1\pmod4$ would divide the fixed nonzero integer

$$
\prod_{i=1}^{r} E_{2n_i}.
$$

But a nonzero integer has only finitely many prime divisors, contradicting Dirichlet's theorem.

Therefore infinitely many distinct indices $n(p)$ occur, and every such index satisfies $a(n(p))>1$.

By definition, $a(n(p))$ is prime. $\square$

### Why this does not prove the full conjecture

Theorem 3 guarantees infinitely many successful indices, but does not establish success for every index.

A tempting stronger assertion would be that for every prime $p\equiv1\pmod4$, the prime $p$ first divides the Euler sequence at $n=(p-1)/2$.

That assertion is false.

For example,

$$
61\equiv1\pmod4,\qquad E_6=-61.
$$

Thus $61$ already occurs at $n=3$, although (7) also guarantees

$$
61\mid E_{60}.
$$

So the congruence does not guarantee that $61$ is primitive at $n=30$.

**Remaining gap:** The arguments provide infinitely many primitive-divisor occurrences, but give no mechanism forcing a new prime divisor at every individual $n\ge2$.

These deductions are presented as self-contained partial results, not as claims of novelty in the mathematical literature. Congruences for Euler numbers have an established history, and no comprehensive priority search for these particular deductions was performed.

## 3. Computational verification

The following Python 3 program independently constructs the Euler numbers from their binomial recurrence, factors the first 18 nonconstant even-indexed terms, identifies newly occurring primes, and checks the modular identities.

It uses SymPy for integer factorization and prime enumeration.

```python

from math import comb
from sympy import factorint, primerange

E = [1]
for n in range(1, 101):
    E.append(-sum(comb(2*n, 2*k)*E[k] for k in range(n)))

seen, a = set(), []
for n in range(1, 19):
    factors = set(factorint(abs(E[n])))
    fresh = factors - seen
    a.append(min(fresh) if fresh else 1)
    seen |= factors

print('a(1)..a(18) =', a)
print('prime for n=2..18:', all(x > 1 for x in a[1:]))

P = list(primerange(3, 102))
failures = 0

for p in P:
    h = (p-1)//2
    for n in range(101):
        s = sum((-1)**j * pow(2*j+1, 2*n, p)
                for j in range(p))
        failures += (E[n]-s) % p != 0

    for n in range(1, 101-h):
        failures += (E[n+h]-E[n]) % p != 0

    failures += E[h] % p != (1-(-1)**h) % p

print('modular primes:', len(P), '(3..101); n=0..100')
print('modular failures:', failures)
print('61 divides E_6 and E_60:', E[3] % 61 == E[30] % 61 == 0)
```
