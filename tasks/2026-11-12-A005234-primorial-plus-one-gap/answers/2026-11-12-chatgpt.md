# OEIS A005234 — Status and Proof Attempt for the Primorial-Plus-One Prime Gap Conjecture

**Research date:** November 12, 2026 **Task:** [A005234 primorial-plus-one gap](https://github.com/iwasborninbali/athanor-oeis/blob/main/tasks/2026-11-12-A005234-primorial-plus-one-gap/TASK.md)

**Verdict:** Open, as far as the sources searched establish. No proof or counterexample was found. The conjecture holds for the first 19 known qualifying values of $p$, according to published computational data; the first 13 were independently checked here. An elementary reduction clarifies the conjecture but does not resolve it.

## 1. Status and literature search

### Precise statement

For a prime $p$, define its primorial

$$
P=p^\#=\prod_{\substack{\ell\le p\\\ell\text{ prime}}}\ell.
$$

Let $q$ be the least prime strictly greater than $P+1$.

Arkadiusz Wesołowski's conjecture is

$$
\boxed{P+1\text{ prime}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q-P<ep.}
\tag{1}
$$

This interpretation of "the next prime" is unambiguous.

### Primary source

**Arkadiusz Wesołowski, February 20, 2013, OEIS A005234**, states:

> Conjecture: if p# + 1 is a prime number, then the next prime is less than p# + exp(1)*p.

The live OEIS entry still labels the statement a conjecture.

The same entry contains a weaker conjecture by **Thomas Ordowski, April 7, 2013**, replacing $ep$ by $p^2$. Neither entry supplies a proof.

### Related literature

**Chris K. Caldwell and Yves Gallot**, *On the Primality of $n!\pm1$ and $2\times3\times5\times\cdots\times p\pm1$*, *Mathematics of Computation* **71** (2002), 441–448, DOI [10.1090/S0025-5718-01-01315-1](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-01-01315-1), MR 2002g:11011.

Their work concerns primality searches and heuristic counts for factorial and primorial primes, not the proposed $ep$ bound.

**Antonín Čejchan, Michal Křížek, and Lawrence Somer**, *On Remarkable Properties of Primes Near Factorials and Primorials*, *Journal of Integer Sequences* **25** (2022), Article 22.1.4, is directly relevant to the arithmetic of prime offsets.

Its abstract states:

> Then we establish further sufficient conditions also for the case when n! is replaced by q#.

Those conditions concern the primality of offsets, not an unconditional guarantee that a prime occurs within the linear interval in (1). This paper is also represented in zbMATH-indexed bibliographic data.

The related sequences are [A005235](https://oeis.org/A005235) (Fortunate numbers), [A014545](https://oeis.org/A014545) (indices of primorial-plus-one primes), and [A006794](https://oeis.org/A006794) (primorial-minus-one primes). The latter contains a corresponding conjecture for the preceding prime, attributed to Wesołowski on June 19, 2016.

## 2. Proof attempt and partial results

### Lemma: a sufficiently small prime offset must itself be prime

**Proved (elementary; known in the literature).**

Let $p\ge3$ be prime and $P=p^\#$. If

$$
1<m<p^2
$$

and $P+m$ is prime, then $m$ is prime.

**Proof.** Suppose $m$ is composite. Since $m<p^2$, some prime divisor $\ell\mid m$ satisfies $\ell<p$. Consequently, $\ell\mid P$, and hence

$$
\ell\mid(P+m).
$$

But $P+m>\ell$, contradicting the primality of $P+m$. Therefore $m$ is prime. $\square$

This is the standard roughness argument underlying sufficient conditions for Fortunate-number primality; it is not a novel result.

### Exact reformulation

**Proved.**

For every prime $p\ge3$, the target conjecture is equivalent to:

$$
\boxed{
P+1\text{ prime}
\ \Longrightarrow\
\exists r\text{ prime},\quad
p<r<ep,\quad P+r\text{ prime}.
}
\tag{2}
$$

Indeed, if $q=P+m$ is the next prime and $m<ep$, then $m>p$, because every integer $m$ with $2\le m\le p$ shares a prime divisor with $P$.

Moreover,

$$
ep<p^2
$$

for $p\ge3$, so the lemma forces $m$ to be prime.

Conversely, if a prime $r\in(p,ep)$ makes $P+r$ prime, then the first prime after $P+1$ is no greater than $P+r$, establishing (1).

The case $p=2$ is verified directly.

This reduction exposes the central obstacle: **a prime offset candidate is not automatically a prime value of $P+r$**. An elementary sieve identifies permissible offsets but does not prove that one produces a prime within $ep$.

The reformulation is closely related to Fortune's conjecture, which asks whether the least prime-producing offset greater than 1 is always prime, but it is not equivalent to that general conjecture.

### Finite partial result

**Computed / tabulated, not a general theorem.**

Let $m=q-P$. For the first 19 known qualifying values, $p\le42209$, the published Fortunate-number data give

$$
\boxed{m<2.2p<ep.}
$$

The largest observed ratio in this finite set is

$$
\max\frac mp=\frac{67}{31}
=2.16129032258\ldots,
$$

attained at $p=31$.

The six largest cases in this range are:

These values were extracted from the A005235 b-file as reproduced in the [fortunate-primes repository](https://github.com/mitselek/fortunate-primes/blob/main/OEIS/b005235.txt). The current official [OEIS b-file](https://oeis.org/A005235/b005235.txt), updated in September 2026, contains 5300 terms and describes the probabilistic primality tests and composite witnesses used for its newest extension.

The first 19 observations are therefore useful numerical evidence, but they are not independent primality certificates for every large integer involved.

## 4. Verdict

**The Wesołowski conjecture remains apparently open as of November 12, 2026.** I found neither a proof nor a counterexample in the inspected OEIS material, related literature, arXiv, Zenodo, or recent-preprint searches. The elementary reduction to simultaneous primality of $p^\#+1$ and $p^\#+r$, for some prime $p<r<ep$, is valid but follows known divisibility principles and is not presented as new. Independently executed computations confirm the bound for the first 13 qualifying values; published A005235 data support it for the first 19, through $p=42209$, with the stronger observed bound $q-p^\#<2.2p$. **No general theorem or new counterexample is claimed.** A suitable OEIS comment, if independently verified, would be: “For the first 19 listed values of $p$, through $p=42209$, the next prime $q$ after $p^\#+1$ satisfies $q-p^\#<2.2p<ep$, using the Fortunate-number data in A005235. This finite verification does not settle Wesołowski's conjecture.” No OEIS submission or edit was made.
