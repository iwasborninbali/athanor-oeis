# A002827 — Unitary-perfect numbers are Zumkeller numbers

**Research report — 12 November 2026**

**Result: A complete elementary proof is given below.** The conjecture recorded in OEIS A002827 follows unconditionally, including for any currently unknown unitary-perfect numbers. I found no earlier published proof of this particular implication in the sources examined; originality remains subject to independent literature review.

## 1. Status and literature

### Precise statement

Let $D(n)$ be the set of positive divisors of $n$, and let

$$
D^*(n)=\{d\mid n:\gcd(d,n/d)=1\}.
$$

Write

$$
\sigma(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d,\qquad
\sigma^*(n)=\sum_{d\in D^*(n)}d.
$$

A positive integer $n$ is *unitary perfect* if $\sigma^*(n)=2n$. It is *Zumkeller* if $D(n)$ admits a partition into two subsets of equal sum.

The conjecture is therefore

$$
\boxed{\sigma^*(n)=2n\ \Longrightarrow\
n\text{ is Zumkeller}.}
$$

Equivalently, OEIS A002827 is a subsequence of A083207.

### Published status

Ivan N. Ianakiev, in an OEIS A002827 comment dated 20 January 2020, stated:

> Conjecture: Subsequence of A083207 (Zumkeller numbers). Verified for all present terms.

This wording is preserved in the [repository's OEIS snapshot](https://github.com/iwasborninbali/athanor-oeis/blob/main/tasks/2026-11-12-A002827-unitary-perfect-zumkeller/context/A002827.seq).

The distinction from *unitary* Zumkeller numbers is essential. Bhabesh Das, **“On unitary Zumkeller numbers,”** *Notes on Number Theory and Discrete Mathematics* **30** (2024), no. 2, pp. 436–442, Proposition 2.1(ii), states:

> unitary perfect numbers are unitary Zumkeller numbers.

That follows immediately from the definition, but does not establish an equal-sum partition of *all* divisors.

OEIS [A290466](https://oeis.org/A290466) likewise calls unitary Zumkeller numbers “seemingly a subsequence” of ordinary Zumkeller numbers. This is a stronger, separate assertion.

The most directly relevant existing theorem is:

**Pankaj Jyoti Mahanta, Manjil P. Saikia, and Daniel Yaqubi (2020),** “Some properties of Zumkeller numbers and $k$-layered numbers,” *Journal of Number Theory* **217**, pp. 218–236, Theorem 2.6:

For an odd prime $p$, $2^\alpha p^\beta$ is Zumkeller precisely when

$$
\beta\text{ is odd},\qquad p\le 2^{\alpha+1}-1.
$$

The decisive condition is quoted in the paper as:

> if and only if $p\le 2^{\alpha+1}-1$ and $\beta$ is an odd number.



Earlier relevant literature includes Yuejian Peng and K. P. S. Bhaskara Rao, **“On Zumkeller numbers,”** *Journal of Number Theory* **133** (2013), no. 4, pp. 1135–1155, and M. V. Subbarao and L. J. Warren, **“Unitary perfect numbers,”** *Canadian Mathematical Bulletin* **9** (1966), pp. 147–153.

**Literature-search conclusion:** I examined the OEIS entries, the relevant OEIS Wiki material, web and arXiv search results, and the accessible full texts and abstracts cited above. Searches aimed at MathSciNet and zbMATH did not locate an earlier proof, but I did not directly inspect their complete subscription databases. Thus the source-level status remains *conjectured*, while the mathematical statement is **proved by the argument below**.

## 2. Proof

### Theorem

**Every unitary-perfect positive integer is a Zumkeller number.**

We prove this without assuming that a unitary-perfect number must be divisible by $3$, or that only five such numbers exist.

### Lemma 1 — A constructive Zumkeller criterion

Let $a,b\ge1$, $b$ odd, and $p$ an odd prime satisfying

$$
p\le2^{a+1}-1.
$$

Then $2^ap^b$ is Zumkeller. Moreover, if $t$ is coprime to $2p$, then $2^ap^bt$ is Zumkeller.

**Proof.** First consider $m=2^ap$. We have

$$
\sigma(m)=(2^{a+1}-1)(p+1).
$$

Set

$$
E=\frac{\sigma(m)-2m}{2}
 =\frac{2^{a+1}-1-p}{2}.
$$

Since $p$ is odd and $3\le p\le2^{a+1}-1$,

$$
0\le E<2^a.
$$

Consequently, $E$ is a sum of distinct powers among $1,2,\ldots,2^{a-1}$, all divisors of $m$ distinct from $m$.

The set consisting of $m$ together with these powers has sum

$$
m+E=\frac{\sigma(m)}2.
$$

Its complement among the divisors of $m$ has the same sum. Thus $m$ is Zumkeller.

Now write $b=2k+1$. The divisors of $2^ap^b$ form the disjoint union

$$
D(2^ap^b)
=\bigsqcup_{j=0}^{k}p^{2j}D(2^ap).
$$

Scale the established partition of $D(2^ap)$ by $p^{2j}$ in each block. Taking the unions of corresponding parts gives an equal-sum partition of all divisors of $2^ap^b$.

Finally, if $\gcd(2^ap^b,t)=1$, then

$$
D(2^ap^bt)=
\bigsqcup_{d\mid t}dD(2^ap^b).
$$

Applying the same scaling construction proves the last assertion. $\square$

This lemma is **known**, as a consequence of the cited Mahanta–Saikia–Yaqubi theorem. The construction above is included to make the proof self-contained.

### Lemma 2 — The forced odd-exponent prime

Suppose $n$ is unitary perfect and

$$
n=2^a\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_i^{e_i},
$$

where $a\ge3$ and the $p_i$ are distinct odd primes.

Then at least one $p_i$ satisfies

$$
e_i\text{ odd}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
p_i\le2^{a+1}-1.
$$

**Proof.**

Because $n$ is unitary perfect,

$$
(2^a+1)\prod_{i=1}^{r}(p_i^{e_i}+1)=2n.
$$

Each factor $p_i^{e_i}+1$ is even. The factor $2^a+1$ is odd, while $v_2(2n)=a+1$. Hence

$$
\boxed{r\le a+1.} \tag{1}
$$

Dividing the unitary-perfect identity by $n$, we obtain

$$
2=(1+2^{-a})
\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1+p_i^{-e_i}). \tag{2}
$$

We use the elementary inequality

$$
\prod_{i=1}^{s}(1+x_i)
\le\frac1{1-\sum_i x_i},
\qquad
x_i\ge0,\quad \sum_i x_i<1. \tag{3}
$$

Indeed, $1+x_i\le(1-x_i)^{-1}$, and induction gives

$$
\prod_i(1-x_i)\ge1-\sum_i x_i.
$$

First consider the factors in (2) whose exponents $e_i$ are even. For these, $e_i\ge2$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{e_i\text{ even}}p_i^{-e_i}
&\le\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac1{(2k+1)^2}\\
&<\frac14\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac1{k(k+1)}\\
&=\frac14.
\end{aligned}
$$

The middle inequality follows from

$$
(2k+1)^2>4k(k+1).
$$

Applying (3) gives

$$
\boxed{
\prod_{e_i\text{ even}}(1+p_i^{-e_i})<\frac43.
} \tag{4}
$$

Now suppose, for contradiction, that every prime whose exponent is odd satisfies

$$
p_i>2^{a+1}-1.
$$

Then $p_i\ge2^{a+1}$. By (1),

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{e_i\text{ odd}}p_i^{-e_i}
&\le\frac{r}{2^{a+1}}\\
&\le\frac{a+1}{2^{a+1}}\\
&\le\frac14,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the last inequality holds for every $a\ge3$.

Another application of (3) yields

$$
\boxed{
\prod_{e_i\text{ odd}}(1+p_i^{-e_i})\le\frac43.
} \tag{5}
$$

Finally, since $a\ge3$,

$$
1+2^{-a}\le\frac98.
$$

Combining (2), (4), and (5) gives

$$
2
<
\frac98\cdot\frac43\cdot\frac43
=2,
$$

a contradiction.

Therefore there exists an odd-exponent prime factor $p_i$ with

$$
p_i\le2^{a+1}-1.
$$

$\square$

**Status:** Lemma 2 is proved here. I did not locate this particular argument in the consulted literature.

### Lemma 3 — The cases $v_2(n)=1,2$

If $n$ is unitary perfect and $v_2(n)=1$, then $n=6$ or $90$. If $v_2(n)=2$, then $n=60$.

These are known classifications from Subbarao and Warren (1966). Here is an independent elementary derivation.

Write the odd prime-power components as $q_1,\ldots,q_r$. Since each $q_i+1$ is even, $r\le a+1$.

**Case $a=1$.** The equation is

$$
\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(1+\frac1{q_i}\right)=\frac43,
\qquad r\le2.
$$

If $r=1$, then $q_1=3$, giving $n=6$.

If $r=2$, write $x=q_1,y=q_2$. Rearrangement gives

$$
(x-3)(y-3)=12.
$$

Here $x,y>3$ are odd, so both factors on the left are positive and even. The only possibilities are $(2,6)$ and $(6,2)$, giving

$$
\{x,y\}=\{5,9\},\qquad n=90.
$$

**Case $a=2$.** Now

$$
\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(1+\frac1{q_i}\right)=\frac85,
\qquad r\le3.
$$

For $r=1$, the equation would force $q_1=5/3$, impossible.

For $r=2$, the equation becomes

$$
(3x-5)(3y-5)=40.
$$

The two factors are positive even divisors of $40$, and each is congruent to $1\pmod3$. Thus the factors are $4$ and $10$, giving $\{x,y\}=\{3,5\}$, hence $n=60$.

If $r=3$, the three even factors $q_i+1$ have product with 2-adic valuation exactly $3$. Therefore each has valuation $1$, so $q_i\equiv1\pmod4$. Upon sorting, the three distinct odd prime powers satisfy

$$
q_1\ge5,\quad q_2\ge9,\quad q_3\ge13.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\prod_{i=1}^{3}\left(1+\frac1{q_i}\right)
\le\frac65\frac{10}{9}\frac{14}{13}
=\frac{56}{39}<\frac85,
$$

a contradiction. $\square$

### Completion of the theorem

First, unitary-perfect numbers are even. This is a classical result, but it also follows directly: for odd $n$, every prime-power component contributes an even factor to $\sigma^*(n)=2n$. Since $v_2(2n)=1$, an odd unitary-perfect $n$ would have exactly one prime factor, say $n=p^e$. But then

$$
1+p^e=2p^e
$$

is impossible. The case $n=1$ also fails.

Thus write $n=2^am$ with $a\ge1$ and $m$ odd.

If $a\ge3$, Lemma 2 supplies an odd prime $p$ appearing to odd exponent $b$, with

$$
p\le2^{a+1}-1.
$$

We may write

$$
n=2^ap^bt,\qquad \gcd(2p,t)=1.
$$

Lemma 1 then proves that $n$ is Zumkeller.

If $a=1$ or $2$, Lemma 3 reduces $n$ to $6,60,90$.

The integers $6$ and $60$ satisfy Lemma 1, using respectively $(a,p)=(1,3)$ and $(2,3)$.

For $90$,

$$
\sigma(90)=(1+2)(1+3+9)(1+5)=234.
$$

The subset $\{90,18,9\}$ has sum $117=\sigma(90)/2$, so $90$ is Zumkeller.

All cases are covered. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\text{Every unitary-perfect number is Zumkeller.}}
$$

$\blacksquare$

The proof also establishes the stronger structural result that every unitary-perfect number other than $90$ contains a Zumkeller *unitary divisor* of the form $2^ap^b$, where $b$ is odd and $p\le2^{a+1}-1$.
