# 1. Status: Open; stronger than the infinitude of Mersenne primes

**Verdict as of 12 November 2026:** The conjecture in [OEIS A119591](https://oeis.org/A119591) remains open in the sources I could verify. It implies the classical open problem of whether infinitely many Mersenne primes exist. More strongly, it implies that Mersenne-prime exponents occur infinitely often in every arithmetic progression $p\equiv1\pmod r$.

## Precise statement

Define

$$
a(n)=\min\{k\ge1:2n^k-1\text{ is prime}\}.
$$

The conjecture is

$$
\boxed{\forall n\ge2,\ \exists k\ge1:
2n^k-1\text{ is prime}.}
\tag{C}
$$

The restriction $n\ge2$ is essential: for $n=1$, the expression always equals $1$, which is not prime. This agrees with the OEIS offset of 2.

## Sources and established status

**Eric Chen (2015), OEIS A119591**, states:

> Conjecture: a(n) is defined for all n.

The entry still presents this as a conjecture. Chen's September 2021 update reports several exceptionally large least exponents, including $a(303)=40174$ and $a(581)>400000$. These are reported computations, not a general existence theorem.

The critical related entry is [OEIS A279095](https://oeis.org/A279095), by **Jon E. Schoenfield (2017)**, which states:

> Equivalently, smallest k such that k*n + 1 is a Mersenne exponent (A000043).

Eric Chen explicitly recorded the identity $a(2^n)=\mathrm{A279095}(n)$ in A119591 in 2021. Thus the connection to Mersenne primes is **already known**, not a new discovery.

For the underlying open problem, **Carl Pomerance, “Cyclotomic primes,” *Journal of Number Theory* 276 (2025), pp. 198–208, DOI [10.1016/j.jnt.2025.02.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnt.2025.02.013)**, writes:

# 2. Proof attempt and unconditional partial results

## Theorem 1. The conjecture implies infinitely many Mersenne primes in every progression $1\bmod r$

**Proved.**

Let

$$
\mathcal M=\{p\ge2:2^p-1\text{ is prime}\}.
$$

I claim that (C) implies

$$
\boxed{\forall r\ge1,\quad
\#\{p\in\mathcal M:p\equiv1\pmod r\}=\infty.}
\tag{1}
$$

### Proof

Assume (C).

Fix any positive integer $r$. For each positive integer $t$, apply (C) to

$$
n=2^{rt}.
$$

There must be some $k_t\ge1$ for which

$$
\begin{aligned}
2n^{k_t}-1
&=2(2^{rt})^{k_t}-1\\
&=2^{rtk_t+1}-1
\end{aligned}
$$

is prime.

Set

$$
p_t=rtk_t+1.
$$

Then $p_t\in\mathcal M$, and

$$
p_t\equiv1\pmod r.
$$

Moreover, $k_t\ge1$ implies

$$
p_t\ge rt+1.
$$

As $t$ increases without bound, the exponents $p_t$ are unbounded. Therefore infinitely many distinct Mersenne-prime exponents satisfy $p\equiv1\pmod r$.

Since $r$ was arbitrary, (1) follows. $\square$

### Corollary

Taking $r=1$, we obtain

$$
\boxed{\text{(C)}\implies
\text{infinitely many Mersenne primes}.}
$$

Thus proving A119591 would settle a famous unsolved problem.

Conversely, merely proving infinitely many Mersenne primes would **not** establish A119591: it would not guarantee a suitable exponent in each progression $1\bmod r$, let alone handle bases that are not powers of two.

## Theorem 2. Exact equivalence for the powers-of-two subfamily

**Proved, using the previously known OEIS identity.**

The following statements are equivalent:

1. $a(2^r)$ exists for every integer $r\ge1$.
2. For every integer $r\ge1$, infinitely many Mersenne-prime exponents $p$ satisfy $p\equiv1\pmod r$.

**Proof.** The implication $1\Rightarrow2$ is the preceding argument, applied only to powers of two.

For $2\Rightarrow1$, choose such a Mersenne-prime exponent $p$ for a given $r$. Then $k=(p-1)/r$ is a positive integer and

$$
2(2^r)^k-1=2^p-1
$$

is prime. Hence $a(2^r)$ exists. $\square$

This establishes the exact mathematical strength of the powers-of-two restriction. The basic identity is in OEIS A119591/A279095; I do not claim novelty for the equivalence or its elementary proof.

## Theorem 3. No finite set of primes can cover all exponents

**Proved.**

For every fixed integer $n\ge2$ and every finite nonempty set $S$ of primes, there are infinitely many positive integers $k$ such that

$$
\gcd\left(2n^k-1,\prod_{q\in S}q\right)=1.
$$

### Proof

Let

$$
L=\operatorname{lcm}\{q-1:q\in S\}.
$$

For any $k$ divisible by $L$, consider a prime $q\in S$.

If $q\nmid n$, Fermat's little theorem yields

$$
n^k\equiv1\pmod q,
$$

and therefore

$$
2n^k-1\equiv1\pmod q.
$$

If $q\mid n$, then

$$
2n^k-1\equiv-1\pmod q.
$$

In neither case does $q$ divide $2n^k-1$.

Every positive multiple of $L$ has this property, proving the result. $\square$

**Consequence:** Unlike classical Riesel-number constructions using finite prime coverings for $b2^k-1$, no fixed finite collection of primes can establish that all $2n^k-1$ are composite. This does not rule out a counterexample whose terms have different, unbounded prime divisors.

## Proposition 4. An infinite family with compulsory even exponents

**Proved.**

For every $m\ge2$, if $n=2m^2$, then $2n^k-1$ is composite whenever $k$ is odd.

Indeed, writing $k=2j+1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
2(2m^2)^k-1
&=(2^{j+1}m^k)^2-1\\
&=(2^{j+1}m^k-1)(2^{j+1}m^k+1).
\end{aligned}
$$

Both factors exceed $1$.

Thus, for these bases, any prime witness must have even exponent. The proposition does not establish whether such an even exponent always exists.

All results in this section are elementary deductions. **No complete proof or counterexample to (C) was obtained.**

# 3. Computational verification

I used Python 3 with SymPy 1.14.0. Two exact verification methods were employed:

- `sympy.isprime`, on inputs below $2^{64}$, where its primality result is definitive.
- The Lucas–Lehmer test, which gives a necessary-and-sufficient primality criterion for $2^p-1$ when $p$ is prime. 1.14.0 documentation+1

The following is the complete executed program.

```python

from math import lcm
from sympy import isprime, primerange

def LL(p):
    if p == 2:
        return True
    m, s = (1 << p) - 1, 4
    for _ in range(p - 2):
        s = (s*s - 2) % m
    return s == 0

A = [next(k for k in range(1, 11) if isprime(2*n**k - 1))
     for n in range(2, 29)]
print('a(2)..a(28) =', A)
print('largest integer tested =', 2*28**10 - 1)

exps = [p for p in primerange(2, 522) if LL(p)]
print('Mersenne exponents p <= 521 =', exps)
print('(a(2^r), p) for r=1..16 =',
      [next(((p-1)//r, p) for p in exps if (p-1) % r == 0)
       for r in range(1, 17)])

primes = (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19)
L = lcm(*(p-1 for p in primes))
assert all((2*pow(n, L, p)-1) % p != 0
           for n in range(2, 101) for p in primes)
print('finite-prime noncoverage check: n=2..100, L=', L, ': PASS', sep='')

assert all(2*(2*m*m)**k - 1 ==
           (2**((k+1)//2)*m**k-1)*(2**((k+1)//2)*m**k+1)
           for m in range(2, 21) for k in range(1, 16, 2))
print('odd-exponent factorization: m=2..20, odd k=1..15: PASS')
```
