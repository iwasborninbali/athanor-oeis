# 1. Status — OEIS A002981

**Verdict: Open as of November 12, 2026, to the extent verified.** I found neither a proof nor a counterexample to the conjecture that two consecutive factorials cannot both yield primes when 1 is added.

The precise statement is

$$
\boxed{\forall n\ge3,\quad
\neg\bigl(\operatorname{Prime}(n!+1)\land
\operatorname{Prime}((n+1)!+1)\bigr).}
$$

It is well-posed. It is not equivalent to the still-open infinitude-of-factorial-primes problem, and I found no established reduction to another named open problem.

### Primary evidence

**Mohammed Bouayoun (March 3, 2004), OEIS A002981**, explicitly introduces the statement with:

> "we can conjecture that for n > 2 if n! + 1 is prime then (n+1)! + 1 is not prime."

The comment remains in the OEIS entry updated October 27, 2026.

**Peter (July 13, 2021), Mathematics Stack Exchange, question 4197086**, explicitly writes:

> "It is open whether this is true for every sufficiently large n."

The same discussion contains a proposed argument using Euler's theorem, but its final congruence does not establish compositeness. It is not a valid proof. Stack Exchange

**Antonín Čejchan, Michal Křížek and Lawrence Somer (2022)**, *On Remarkable Properties of Primes Near Factorials and Primorials*, *Journal of Integer Sequences* **25**, Article 22.1.4, investigate prime gaps around factorials. Their stated theorem concerns a sufficient condition for $p-n!$ to be prime; it does not settle the consecutive-factorial-prime conjecture.

The known indices in A002981 have no consecutive pair above 2. The largest currently listed is 422429, also recorded by Wolfram MathWorld and PrimePages. This is evidence from known examples, not an exhaustive proof.

# 2. Proof attempt and partial results

## 2.1. A necessary condition for consecutive factorial primes

**Proved, but already implicit in the OEIS comment by Farideh Firoozbakht (2003).**

**Proposition.** If $n\ge3$ and both $n!+1$ and $(n+1)!+1$ are prime, then $n+1$ and $n+2$ are both composite.

**Proof.**

Wilson's theorem states that, for every prime $p$,

$$
(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p.
$$

If $n+1$ is prime, applying Wilson's theorem with $p=n+1$ gives

$$
n!+1\equiv0\pmod{n+1}.
$$

Since $n!+1>n+1$ for $n\ge3$, the number $n!+1$ is composite, contradicting the hypothesis.

Similarly, if $n+2$ is prime, then

$$
(n+1)!+1\equiv0\pmod{n+2},
$$

and $(n+1)!+1>n+2$, contradicting its primality.

Therefore both $n+1$ and $n+2$ must be composite. $\square$

In particular, the conjecture is **proved for every $n=p-2$, where $p\ge5$ is prime**. This eliminates infinitely many indices, though not all possible indices. The underlying Wilson obstruction is known material.

## 2.2. Exact relationship with Bouayoun's `nextprime` equivalence

There are two mathematical claims in Bouayoun's 2004 comment, and distinguishing them matters.

Define

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(n)&:\quad n!+1\text{ is prime},\\
Q(n)&:\quad(n+1)!+1\text{ is prime},\\
a_n&=\operatorname{nextprime}(n!)-n!,\\
b_n&=\operatorname{nextprime}((n+1)!)-(n+1)!.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here `nextprime(x)` means the smallest prime strictly greater than $x$.

The proposed inequality is exactly

$$
B(n):\quad b_n>(n+1)a_n.
$$

**Proved observations:**

For $n\ge3$,

$$
a_n=1\iff P(n).
$$

Also,

$$
b_n=1\iff Q(n).
$$

Since every integer

$$
(n+1)!+k,\qquad 2\le k\le n+1,
$$

is divisible by $k$, we have

$$
b_n=1\quad\text{or}\quad b_n\ge n+2.
$$

Consequently, if $P(n)$ holds, then $a_n=1$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
B(n)
&\iff b_n>n+1\\
&\iff b_n\ne1\\
&\iff\neg Q(n).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{P(n)\implies\bigl(B(n)\iff\neg Q(n)\bigr).}
$$

This is an unconditional identity. It shows that the implication $P(n)\Rightarrow B(n)$, asserted within Bouayoun's equivalence, is **itself equivalent to the consecutive-factorial-prime conjecture** when universally quantified over $n\ge3$.

Moreover, the full biconditional $P(n)\iff B(n)$ is equivalent to the conjunction of:

1. The consecutive-factorial-prime conjecture, $P(n)\Rightarrow\neg Q(n)$.
2. The additional prime-gap assertion that, whenever $P(n)$ and $Q(n)$ are both false, \[ b_n\le(n+1)a_n. \]

The second assertion is not established here.

**Conclusion:** Bouayoun's `nextprime` equivalence cannot serve as an independent proof of the consecutive-prime conjecture. Its forward implication already contains the unresolved statement. This decomposition is elementary; I make no priority or novelty claim for it.

## 2.3. Result of the proof attempt

No general proof or counterexample was obtained.

The established results are the Wilson obstruction and the exact logical decomposition above. Neither excludes all indices for which $n+1$ and $n+2$ are composite.

I also found no justified reduction to a named open problem. In particular, the present conjecture should not be confused with the question of whether infinitely many primes of the form $n!+1$ exist.

## 3.1. Certified primality test through $n=501$

Instead of relying exclusively on probable-prime testing, I used an elementary, certificate-based approach:

- **Compositeness:** failure of a strong Miller–Rabin test to base 2 is a rigorous compositeness witness.
- **Primality:** numbers surviving that test are certified using Pocklington's theorem, taking advantage of the completely known factorization of $N-1=n!$.

For Pocklington's theorem, choose a fully factored divisor $F\mid N-1$ with $F^2>N$. For every prime $q\mid F$, find an integer $a$ satisfying

$$
a^{N-1}\equiv1\pmod N
$$

and

$$
\gcd\left(a^{(N-1)/q}-1,N\right)=1.
$$

These conditions prove $N$ prime: every prime divisor $r$ of $N$ must satisfy $F\mid r-1$, hence $r>\sqrt N$, which is impossible for composite $N$.

The calculation deliberately uses only odd prime factors of $n!$ in $F$, avoiding unnecessarily expensive quadratic-nonresidue searches.

Executable Python 3 program

```python

from math import gcd, isqrt

LAST_N = 501

def primes_upto(k):
    return [p for p in range(2, k + 1)
            if all(p % d for d in range(2, isqrt(p) + 1))]

def mr2_pass(N):
    if N == 2:
        return True
    if N < 2 or N % 2 == 0:
        return False

    d, s = N - 1, 0
    while d % 2 == 0:
        d //= 2
        s += 1

    x = pow(2, d, N)
    if x in (1, N - 1):
        return True

    for _ in range(s - 1):
        x = x*x % N
        if x == N - 1:
            return True
    return False

def certify_prime(N, n):
    if N in (2, 3):
        return 0

    F, factors = 1, []

    for q in primes_upto(n):
        if q == 2:
            continue

        v, m = 0, n
        while m:
            m //= q
            v += m

        F *= q**v
        factors.append(q)

        if F*F > N:
            break

    assert F*F > N

    high = 0
    for q in factors:
        a = 2
        while not (
            pow(a, N - 1, N) == 1
            and gcd(pow(a, (N - 1)//q, N) - 1, N) == 1
        ):
            a += 1
            assert a <= 100
        high = max(high, a)

    return high

f = 1
survivors, cert_bases = [], []

for n in range(LAST_N + 1):
    if n:
        f *= n
    N = f + 1

    if mr2_pass(N):
        survivors.append(n)
        cert_bases.append(certify_prime(N, n))

pairs = [
    n for n in survivors
    if n >= 3 and n + 1 in survivors
]

print("certified range: 0 <= n <=", LAST_N)
print("prime indices:", survivors)
print("pairs with n >= 3:", pairs)
print("maximum Pocklington witness base:", max(cert_bases))
print("base-2 strong test composite certificates:",
      LAST_N + 1 - len(survivors))
print("Pocklington prime certificates (n>=3):",
      len(survivors) - 3)
```

pairs with n >= 3: []
maximum Pocklington witness base: 7
base-2 strong test composite certificates: 486
Pocklington prime certificates (n>=3): 13

The three remaining prime cases, $n=0,1,2$, give $N=2,2,3$, whose primality is immediate.

## 3.2. Check of the `nextprime` equivalence

A separate SymPy check tested Bouayoun's displayed biconditional for every integer $3\le n\le120$.

```python

from math import factorial
from sympy import isprime, nextprime

wrong = []
for n in range(3, 121):
    f = factorial(n)
    lhs = isprime(f + 1)
    rhs = (
        nextprime((n + 1)*f)
        > (n + 1)*nextprime(f)
    )
    if lhs != rhs:
        wrong.append(n)

print("nextprime equivalence mismatches n=3..120:", wrong)
```

**Exact output:**

```
nextprime equivalence mismatches n=3..120: []
```

This gives 118 tested indices with zero observed mismatches.

**Limitation:** SymPy uses probable-prime methods for sufficiently large integers, so this second check is computational evidence rather than an independently certified primality census. It does not establish either direction of the biconditional globally.
