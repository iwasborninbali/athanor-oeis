# OEIS A000769: Status and Proof Attempt

**Research report — 12 November 2026**

**Verdict: OPEN.** The conjecture that A000769 eventually vanishes is a classical unsolved problem in discrete geometry. I found neither a proof nor a counterexample. I independently verified the recently discovered $78\times78$ configuration and exhaustively reproduced the sequence through $n=8$. No resolution of the asymptotic conjecture follows from these computations.

## 1. Status and literature

### 1.1 Precise statement

Let

$$
G_n=\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}^2
$$

and define

$$
D(n)=\max\{|S|:S\subseteq G_n,\
\text{no three points of }S\text{ are collinear}\}.
$$

Every horizontal row contains at most two selected points. Therefore

$$
\boxed{D(n)\le 2n.}
$$

OEIS [A000769](https://oeis.org/A000769) counts the configurations attaining this upper bound, identifying configurations equivalent under the eight symmetries of the square.

For $n\ge2$,

$$
a(n)>0\iff D(n)=2n.
$$

Consequently, the conjecture is precisely

$$
\boxed{
\exists N\ \forall n\ge N:\ D(n)<2n.
}
$$

It is well-posed and is the **eventual nonattainment conjecture** for the classical no-three-in-line problem.

An important distinction: this conjecture is not logically equivalent to asking whether $D(n)=2n$ for every $n$. A single failure would disprove the latter statement but would not establish eventual nonattainment. Conversely, disproving eventual nonattainment would require configurations attaining $2n$ for infinitely many $n$.

### 1.2 Decisive literature

**Guy and Kelly (1968).** Richard K. Guy and Patrick A. Kelly, *The No-Three-Line Problem*, University of Calgary Research Paper 33 (1968); condensed version, *Canadian Mathematical Bulletin* **11** (1968), 527–531.

Their heuristic suggests that the maximum eventually falls below $2n$. Guy subsequently corrected the predicted asymptotic constant to

$$
c=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt3}\approx1.813799.
$$

This is a **heuristic conjecture**, not a proved asymptotic bound. The corrected prediction is recorded in A000769 and MathWorld.

**Prellberg (2026).** Thomas Prellberg, *Constraint Satisfaction Programming for the No-three-in-line Problem*, arXiv:2602.07751, February 2026.

Decisive abstract:

> Using a constraint satisfaction approach, we exhibit configurations of $2n$ points on the $n\times n$ grid for all $n\le60$ with no three collinear.

This establishes a finite range, not the asymptotic conjecture.

**Ghosal, Goenka, Grebennikov, Keevash, Kwan and Pham (2026).** *No-$(k+1)$-in-line problem for $k\ge3$*, arXiv:2607.05255, July 2026.

Decisive sentence:

> This problem is still open.

The authors prove the maximum is $kn$ for sufficiently large $n$ when $k\ge3$. They explicitly exclude $k=2$, the classical no-three-in-line problem. Thus their theorem does not resolve A000769.

## 2. Proof attempt and partial results

### 2.1 Necessary structure of every extremal configuration

**Proposition 1 — proved (elementary, not claimed as new).**

If $S\subseteq G_n$ consists of $2n$ points with no three collinear, then:

1. Every row contains exactly two selected points.
2. Every column contains exactly two selected points.
3. $S$ is the union of the graphs of two permutations $p,q$ of $\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$, where $p(i)\ne q(i)$ for all $i$.

**Proof.** Each row contains at most two points. There are $n$ rows and $2n$ points, so every row must contain exactly two. Repeating the argument for columns proves the second assertion.

Construct a bipartite graph whose left vertices are the rows, whose right vertices are the columns, and whose edges are the selected points. Every vertex has degree two.

Every connected component of a finite simple 2-regular bipartite graph is an even cycle. Colour its edges alternately with two colours. Each colour class is a perfect matching of the bipartite graph, hence defines a permutation. The two matchings are disjoint. ∎

Thus every exact $2n$-point configuration can be represented as

$$
S=\{(i,p(i)),(i,q(i)):0\le i<n\}.
$$

The remaining condition is that every triple of distinct selected points has nonzero determinant:

$$
\det
\begin{pmatrix}
x_2-x_1&y_2-y_1\\
x_3-x_1&y_3-y_1
\end{pmatrix}\ne0.
$$

This reduces the search from arbitrary subsets of $n^2$ cells to pairs of disjoint permutations, but does not by itself solve the asymptotic problem.

### 2.2 A nontrivial asymptotic restriction

**Proposition 2 — proved as a consequence of a known 2026 theorem.**

Suppose $S$ is an exact $2n$-point configuration. Write $S_0,S_1$ for its two checkerboard colour classes, where the colour of $(x,y)$ is determined by $x+y\bmod2$.

Aujla, Prellberg and Sandhu (2026) establish that the maximum number of points confined to either one colour is at most

$$
\alpha n+O(1),\qquad
\alpha\approx1.5768233968738.
$$

Therefore

$$
|S_0|\le\alpha n+O(1),\qquad
|S_1|\le\alpha n+O(1).
$$

Since $|S_0|+|S_1|=2n$, subtraction yields

$$
\boxed{
|S_i|\ge0.4231766031262\,n-O(1)
\quad(i=0,1).
}
$$

Thus any family of exact $2n$-point configurations would necessarily occupy both checkerboard colours in linear proportions. This deduction uses the published checkerboard bound; **no originality is claimed for the underlying theorem**.

For finite $n\ge6$, the same paper gives the simpler bound $|S_i|\le2n-4$, implying that each colour must contain at least four selected points.

### 2.3 What these results do not prove

Neither proposition forces a deficit in the unrestricted maximum $D(n)$. In particular, the checkerboard restriction does not apply to a configuration using both colours, and the permutation decomposition does not rule out suitable permutations for arbitrarily large $n$.

I therefore obtain no new infinite-family construction, universal deficit, or proof of eventual vanishing.
