# OEIS A308766 — Status and Proof Attempt

**Report date:** 12 November 2026 **Verdict:** Open conjecture; one direction proved, the other unresolved. **New result:** No new theorem or counterexample. An independently executed exhaustive check confirms the conjecture for lengths 1–30.

## 1. Status

### Precise statement

For a positive integer $n$, define

$$
M(n)=\min\left\{|S|:
\begin{array}{l}
S\subseteq\{0,\ldots,n\},\\
\{1,\ldots,n\}\subseteq S-S
\end{array}\right\}.
$$

Here $S-S$ denotes all differences between elements of $S$.

Let

$$
B(n)=\operatorname{round}\left(\sqrt{3n+\frac94}\right),
$$

where rounding is to the nearest integer, with ties to even, as in Mathematica.

The conjecture is

$$
\boxed{\forall n\ge1,\quad M(n)-B(n)\in\{0,1\}.}
$$

Equivalently,

$$
B(n)\le M(n)\le B(n)+1.
$$

The range $n\ge1$ matters: at $n=0$, $M(0)=1$, whereas the unmodified rounding expression gives 2. OEIS A309407 explicitly assigns the exceptional value $B(0)=1$.

### Literature findings

**Original conjecture — still open.**

Ed Pegg Jr, *OEIS A308766* (2019), states:

> Conjecture: Minimal marks k - round(sqrt(9 + 12*k)/2) is always 0 or 1.

The entry provides verified exceptional lengths through 213 but no proof for arbitrary lengths.

**Upper bound — already proved.**

Ed Pegg Jr, *Hitting All the Marks: Exploring New Bounds for Sparse Rulers and a Wolfram Language Proof*, Wolfram Blog, 12 February 2020, Section “Proof That E ≤ 1”, gives a constructive computational argument establishing

$$
\boxed{M(n)\le B(n)+1\qquad(n\ge1).}
$$

The argument uses Wichmann-type rulers and extensions, including a finite catalogue for smaller lengths. OEIS A326499 also records Pegg's proof of $E\le1$, dated 16 October 2019.

The unresolved direction is therefore

$$
\boxed{M(n)\ge B(n)\qquad(n\ge1).}
$$

MathWorld's *Sparse Ruler* entry explicitly acknowledges that even a negative excess has not been excluded:

> it has not even been ruled out that the excess could be E=-1



**Exact computations — published through length 244, not merely 213.**

Their abstract reports:

They distinguish determining the minimum mark count through 244 from enumerating all minimal rulers, completed through 213. The associated OEIS A103298 entry links a table through 244.

**Recent mathematical literature.**

Aleksi Saarela and Aleksi Vanhatalo, *A Connection Between Unbordered Partial Words and Sparse Rulers*, *The Electronic Journal of Combinatorics* **33**(1) (2026), #P1.40, published 27 February 2026, DOI: 10.37236/13806.

The authors give a self-contained proof of the Wichmann construction and establish, in Theorem 19, the bound

$$
M(n)\le\sqrt{3n}+4\qquad(n\ge213).
$$

They explicitly identify Pegg's construction as providing the best upper bound. Their work does not establish the conjectured lower bound.

**Related open problem.**

Erdős Problem #170 asks for the value of

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{M(n)}{\sqrt n}.
$$

Erdős and Gál proved existence of this limit in 1948. The best classical bounds place it between approximately $1.56$ and $\sqrt3$. The Erdős Problems database still labels the question **OPEN**. Problems

As proved in Section 2, A308766's conjecture would determine that constant to be $\sqrt3$. This is a one-way implication, not a proved equivalence.

## 2. Proof attempt and rigorous partial results

### Proposition 1 — Elementary universal lower bound

**Proved.** For every $n\ge1$,

$$
M(n)\ge
\left\lceil\frac{1+\sqrt{8n+1}}2\right\rceil.
$$

**Proof.**

Suppose a complete ruler has $m$ distinct marks.

Each positive distance must arise from at least one unordered pair of distinct marks.

There are exactly

$$
\binom m2=\frac{m(m-1)}2
$$

such pairs.

Consequently, covering all $n$ positive distances requires

$$
n\le\frac{m(m-1)}2.
$$

Solving the quadratic inequality and using integrality gives the claimed bound. $\square$

This is classical, not new.

A stronger known inequality comes from John Leech, *On the representation of 1, 2, ..., n by differences*, *Journal of the London Mathematical Society* **31** (1956), 160–169. It implies approximately

$$
M(n)>\sqrt{2.43n}.
$$

See also Saarela–Vanhatalo (2026), Theorem 11, for a precise trigonometric formulation.

Neither lower bound reaches the conjectured $\sqrt{3n}+O(1)$ level.

### Proposition 2 — The conjecture would determine the Erdős #170 constant

**Proved conditional implication.**

If

$$
M(n)-B(n)\in\{0,1\}
$$

for every positive integer $n$, then

$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{M(n)}{\sqrt n}=\sqrt3.}
$$

**Proof.**

By the definition of nearest-integer rounding,

$$
\left|B(n)-\sqrt{3n+\frac94}\right|\le\frac12.
$$

Therefore,

$$
B(n)=\sqrt{3n+\frac94}+O(1)
     =\sqrt{3n}+O(1).
$$

Under the conjecture,

$$
B(n)\le M(n)\le B(n)+1.
$$

Hence,

$$
M(n)=\sqrt{3n}+O(1).
$$

Dividing by $\sqrt n$,

$$
\frac{M(n)}{\sqrt n}
=\sqrt3+O(n^{-1/2}).
$$

Letting $n\to\infty$ proves the proposition. $\square$

**Interpretation.** This reduces the open asymptotic question in Erdős Problem #170 to a consequence of A308766's conjecture.

The converse has not been established. Knowing only the limit does not, by itself, control every finite error to within one mark. Thus A308766 is a substantially sharper finite-$n$ assertion.

### Proposition 3 — Verified properties of Wichmann's construction

**Known theorem, with an independent finite check in Section 3.**

For positive $r$ and nonnegative $s$, consider the consecutive gap sequence

$$
W(r,s)=
1^r,\ (r+1),\ (2r+1)^r,\ (4r+3)^s,\
(2r+2)^{r+1},\ 1^r,
$$

where $a^b$ means $b$ consecutive gaps of size $a$.

Its number of marks is

$$
m(r,s)=4r+s+3,
$$

and its length is

$$
L(r,s)=4r(r+s+2)+3(s+1).
$$

These two formulas follow directly by counting and summing the gaps.

Wichmann's theorem establishes that this construction covers every distance from 1 to $L(r,s)$. Saarela and Vanhatalo (2026), Section 3.2, give a self-contained verification of the construction.

Therefore,

$$
M(L(r,s))\le4r+s+3.
$$

This supplies an infinite family of constructive upper bounds.

It does **not** establish matching lower bounds: verifying that a ruler is complete is not the same as proving that it uses the fewest possible marks.

### Why these results do not complete the conjecture

The obstacle is global minimality. Even one complete ruler with fewer than $B(n)$ marks would disprove the conjecture, while proving that no such ruler exists requires ruling out every possible placement with fewer marks.

Published constructions establish upper bounds. Exhaustive searches establish exact answers on finite ranges. Neither provides a universal lower bound of $B(n)$.

Also, the better asymptotic constructions for *unrestricted* difference bases must not be confused with this problem: here every mark must lie in $[0,n]$. The two quantities have different known asymptotic bounds.

## 3. Independent computational check

I wrote and executed a Python program using only the standard library.

It performs three checks:

1. **Exact exhaustive minimization** for $1\le n\le30$, enumerating every admissible subset of each possible cardinality until a complete ruler is found.
2. **Construction verification** for all 210 Wichmann parameter pairs $1\le r\le10,\ 0\le s\le20$, checking coverage, length, and mark counts.
3. **Witness verification** for the known 13-mark ruler of length 51, the first A308766 term.

The rounding implementation uses integer arithmetic to avoid floating-point ambiguity at half-integers.

```python

from itertools import combinations
from math import isqrt

def baseline(n):
    if n == 0:
        return 1
    x = 12*n + 9
    q = isqrt(x)//2
    t = (2*q + 1)**2
    return q + (x > t or (x == t and q%2 == 1))

def complete(A, n):
    mask = 0
    for i, x in enumerate(A):
        for y in A[i+1:]:
            mask |= 1 << (y-x-1)
    return mask == (1 << n)-1

def minimum(n):
    for m in range(2,n+2):
        for mid in combinations(range(1,n),m-2):
            if complete((0,)+mid+(n,),n):
                return m

N=30
ans = [minimum(n) for n in range(1,N+1)]
print('n=1..%d: %s' % (N, ','.join(map(str,ans))))
print('excesses:',','.join(str(ans[n-1]-baseline(n)) for n in range(1,N+1)))
print('negative excess lengths:',[n for n in range(1,N+1) if ans[n-1]<baseline(n)])

def wichmann(r,s):
    gaps= ([1]*r + [r+1] + [2*r+1]*r +
           [4*r+3]*s + [2*r+2]*(r+1) + [1]*r)
    a=[0]
    for g in gaps:
        a.append(a[-1]+g)
    return a

checks=0
for r in range(1,11):
    for s in range(21):
        a=wichmann(r,s)
        n=a[-1]
        assert n==4*r*(r+s+2)+3*(s+1)
        assert len(a)==4*r+s+3
        assert complete(a,n)
        checks+=1
print('Wichmann verified (r=1..10,s=0..20):',checks)
print('length/marks W(10,20):',wichmann(10,20)[-1],len(wichmann(10,20)))
print('rounding checks: ', [(n,baseline(n)) for n in (6,18,36,90)])

a=[0,1,2,3,4,10,16,22,28,34,40,46,51]
print('n=51 witness: valid=%s marks=%d baseline=%d' % (complete(a,51),len(a),baseline(51)))
```
