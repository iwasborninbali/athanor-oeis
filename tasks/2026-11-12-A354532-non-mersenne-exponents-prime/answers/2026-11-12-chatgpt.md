$$
1,9,67,137,727,\ldots
$$

Its defining property is that $k$ is not a Mersenne-prime exponent and, for every prime divisor $p$ of $2^k-1$,

$$

# 2. Proof attempt and partial results

## 2.1 Precise formulation

Let

$$
M_k=2^k-1.
$$

Define $P(k)$ to mean

$$
\forall p\mid M_k,\quad
p\text{ prime}\Longrightarrow
P^+(2M_k+p)\le p,
$$

where $P^+(n)$ is the largest prime factor of $n>1$.

Since

$$
2M_k+p=p\left(2\frac{M_k}{p}+1\right),
$$

the condition is equivalent to requiring

$$
2\frac{M_k}{p}+1
$$

to be $p$-smooth for every prime divisor $p$ of $M_k$.

The conjecture is therefore

$$
\boxed{k>9\ \land\ P(k)\Longrightarrow k\text{ is prime}.}
$$

This formulation includes all Mersenne exponents in $P(k)$, but they are prime already. The $k=1$ case is vacuous because $M_1=1$.

## 2.2 Even exponents: complete exclusion beyond 2

**Known result, with an elementary proof.**

If $k$ is even, then $3\mid M_k$. Hence $P(k)$ requires

$$
2^{k+1}+1=3^a
$$

for some positive integer $a$.

For $k\ge4$, reduction modulo 8 gives $3^a\equiv1\pmod8$, so $a$ is even. Write $a=2b$. Then

$$
(3^b-1)(3^b+1)=2^{k+1}.
$$

Both factors are powers of 2 differing by 2, so they must be 2 and 4. Consequently $3^b=3$, $a=2$, and $k=2$, contradicting $k\ge4$.

Thus

$$
\boxed{P(k)\text{ and }k\text{ even}\Longrightarrow k=2.}
$$

This agrees with the existing OEIS statement and is not claimed as new.

## 2.3 Odd multiples of 3: a strong necessary condition

**Proved here.** The following is the main partial result of this investigation.

**Proposition.** If $k>9$, $3\mid k$, and $P(k)$ holds, then

$$
\boxed{k\equiv807353703\pmod{1694851494}.}
$$

In particular,

$$
\boxed{k\ge807353703.}
$$

### Proof

Assume $k>9$, $3\mid k$, and $P(k)$. By Section 2.2, $k$ is odd, so $k\equiv3\pmod6$.

Because $7\mid2^k-1$, the defining condition with $p=7$ requires

$$
T=2^{k+1}+5
$$

to be 7-smooth.

Since $k+1\equiv4\pmod6$,

$$
T\equiv2^4+5\equiv3\pmod9.
$$

Therefore $v_3(T)=1$. Moreover, $T$ is odd, $5\nmid T$, and $7\mid T$. Hence 7-smoothness implies

$$
\boxed{2^{k+1}+5=3\cdot7^b}
\tag{1}
$$

for some $b\ge1$.

As $k+1\ge4$, reducing (1) modulo 8 gives

$$
5\equiv3(-1)^b\pmod8.
$$

Thus **$b$ is odd**.

Since $k>9$ and $k\equiv3\pmod6$, we have $k\ge15$, so

$$
2^{k+1}+5\ge65541>3\cdot7^5.
$$

Consequently $b\ge7$, which implies

$$
2^{k+1}\equiv-5\pmod{7^7}.
$$

For every $e\ge1$,

$$
\operatorname{ord}_{7^e}(2)=3\cdot7^{e-1}.
$$

Indeed, $\operatorname{ord}_7(2)=3$, and the lifting-the-exponent identity gives

$$
v_7(2^{3m}-1)=1+v_7(m).
$$

Thus a solution to $2^t\equiv-5\pmod{7^e}$, when it exists, occupies one residue class modulo $3\cdot7^{e-1}$.

Successive lifting gives the following exact residues:

| Divisibility required | $t=k+1$ modulo $\operatorname{ord}_{7^e}(2)$ | Required odd $k$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $7^7\mid T$ | $163915\pmod{352947}$ | $516861\pmod{705894}$ |
| $7^9\mid T$ | $11811166\pmod{17294403}$ | $11811165\pmod{34588806}$ |
| $7^{11}\mid T$ | $807353704\pmod{847425747}$ | $807353703\pmod{1694851494}$ |

These residues are independently reproducible by the program in Section 3.

The first congruence implies $k\ge516861$. Equation (1) then forces $b>7$, hence $b\ge9$.

The second congruence implies $k\ge11811165$. Equation (1) now forces $b>9$, hence $b\ge11$.

The third congruence therefore applies:

$$
k\equiv807353703\pmod{1694851494}.
$$

This proves the proposition.

### Scope

This excludes all multiples of 3 strictly between 9 and 807353703, subject to the defining condition.

It is not a proof of the full conjecture. Composite exponents not divisible by 3 remain possible, and the argument does not eliminate every sufficiently large multiple of 3.

I found no equivalent named open problem or published result establishing the complete conjecture. The equation

$$
2^{k+1}+5=3\cdot7^b
$$

is an exponential Diophantine subproblem, but reducing one class of exponents to it does not establish equivalence of the original conjecture to a known open problem.

## 2.4 An arithmetic correction in OEIS

There is a verifiable error in the example for $k=9$.

The entry prints

$$
2(2^9-1)+7=7^3.
$$

The correct identity is

$$
\boxed{2(2^9-1)+7=1029=3\cdot7^3.}
$$

This remains 7-smooth and does not change membership in A354532.

Indeed, $2^9-1=7\cdot73$, and the two required checks are

$$
2\frac{511}{7}+1=147=3\cdot7^2
$$

and

$$
2\frac{511}{73}+1=15=3\cdot5.
$$

Both satisfy the appropriate smoothness conditions.

# 3. Computational verification

The following Python 3 program uses SymPy to check every composite exponent $4\le k\le960$ and independently lift the modular roots used above.

For each composite $k$, choose the smallest prime divisor $d\mid k$, then the smallest prime factor $p\mid2^d-1$. Because $d\mid k$, this $p$ certainly divides $2^k-1$.

If

$$
2\frac{2^k-1}{p}+1
$$

is not $p$-smooth, the exponent is rigorously excluded. If it is smooth, the test is only inconclusive until the other prime factors are checked.

```python

from functools import lru_cache
from math import isqrt
from sympy import factorint, isprime, primerange

@lru_cache(None)
def primes_upto(p):
    return tuple(primerange(2, p + 1))

def smooth(n, p):
    for q in primes_upto(p):
        while n % q == 0:
            n //= q
        if n == 1:
            break
    return n == 1

survivors = []
count = 0

for k in range(4, 961):
    if isprime(k):
        continue

    count += 1
    d = next(q for q in primerange(2, isqrt(k) + 1)
             if k % q == 0)
    p = min(factorint((1 << d) - 1))

    M = (1 << k) - 1
    if smooth(2 * (M // p) + 1, p):
        survivors.append(k)

print(f"composite k in [4, 960]: {count}")
print(f"excluded by one prime factor: {count - len(survivors)}")
print(f"survivors of necessary test: {survivors}")

M = (1 << 9) - 1
print(f"k=9, p=7: {2*(M//7)+1} = 3 * 7^2")
print(f"k=9, p=73: {2*(M//73)+1} = 3 * 5")

def root(e):
    r, period = 1, 3
    for j in range(2, e + 1):
        mod = 7**j
        r = next(r + t*period for t in range(7)
                 if (pow(2, r + t*period, mod) + 5) % mod == 0)
        period *= 7

    t = r if r % 2 == 0 else r + period
    return r, period, t - 1, 2*period

for e in (7, 9, 11):
    r, period, k0, modulo = root(e)
    print(f"7^{e}: t = {r} (mod {period}); "
          f"odd k = {k0} (mod {modulo})")
```
