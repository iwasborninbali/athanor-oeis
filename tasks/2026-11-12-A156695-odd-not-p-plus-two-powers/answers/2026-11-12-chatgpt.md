# OEIS A156695 — Status, proof attempt, and computational verification

**Investigation date:** 12 November 2026 **Target:** [OEIS A156695](https://oeis.org/A156695) **Conjecture:** Every term greater than 5 is divisible by 255.

## 1. Status

**Verdict: Open, as far as the literature search establishes.** I found neither a proof nor a counterexample to the divisibility-by-255 conjecture. The statement is mathematically well-posed, but existing infinitude and counting theorems do not settle it.

The precise conjecture is

$$
\boxed{
\forall n\in\mathbb Z_{>5},\quad
n\text{ odd},\quad
255\nmid n
\implies
\exists p\text{ prime},\ a,b\ge1:
n=p+2^a+2^b.
}
\tag{C}
$$

The exponents may be equal. The exceptional set is

$$
E=\{n\ge1:n\text{ odd},\
n\ne p+2^a+2^b
\text{ for all primes }p,\ a,b\ge1\}.
$$

### Existing literature

**Roger Crocker (1971).** *On the sum of a prime and of two powers of two*, *Pacific Journal of Mathematics* **36**(1), 103–107, Theorem I, p. 103. DOI: [10.2140/pjm.1971.36.103](https://doi.org/10.2140/pjm.1971.36.103).

Crocker proves that there are infinitely many odd integers without the specified representation. The decisive sentence is:

> There is an infinity of distinct, positive odd integers not representable as the sum of a prime and of two positive powers of 2.

This proves $E$ is infinite, but says nothing universal about the divisibility of its elements by 255.

**Hao Pan (2011).** *On the integers not of the form $p+2^a+2^b$*, *Acta Arithmetica* **148**(1), 55–61, Theorem 1.1. DOI: [10.4064/aa148-1-4](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa148-1-4).

Pan proves a substantially stronger lower bound for the number of exceptional integers. In particular, for every $\varepsilon>0$,

$$
|E\cap[1,x]|\gg_\varepsilon x^{1-\varepsilon}.
$$

The arXiv abstract explicitly states this bound. It is a counting result, not a congruence classification.

**Yuchen Ding, Yu-Chen Sun, and Lilu Zhao (2026).** *An improved lower bound for odd integers not of the form $p+2^a+2^b$*, [arXiv:2607.05357](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05357), submitted 6 July 2026.

Their abstract states:

> We improve on Pan's result by showing that, given any η>0

the exceptional-set counting function satisfies, for sufficiently large $x$,

$$
N(x)\gg_\eta
x\exp\left(
-(4+\eta)
\frac{\log\log\log x}{\log\log x}
\log x
\right).
$$

This improves the quantitative lower bound without establishing or refuting (C). Combinatorics

## 2. Proof attempt and partial results

### 2.1. A rigorous infinite subfamily satisfying the conjecture

**Proved, using Crocker's 1971 construction. Not new.**

Crocker's proof gives something stronger than infinitude alone: every integer in the particular infinite family he constructs is divisible by 255.

Define the Fermat numbers

$$
F_j=2^{2^j}+1.
$$

Recall the identity

$$
2^{2^n}-1=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}F_j.
\tag{1}
$$

In the proof of Theorem I, Crocker chooses $k=10$ and a proper divisor

$$
G_{10}=
\frac{2^{1024}+1}{2^{12}\cdot11131+1}
$$

of $F_{10}$.

For each $n>10$, his covering-congruence construction produces exceptional odd integers $t$ satisfying

$$
t\equiv0\pmod{
\frac{2^{2^n}-1}{G_{10}}
}.
\tag{2}
$$

These conditions appear in equation (3) on p. 105 and the proof on pp. 105–106 of Crocker's paper.

**Claim.** Every such $t$ is divisible by 255.

**Proof.**

From (1),

$$
\frac{2^{2^n}-1}{G_{10}}
=
\left(\prod_{\substack{0\le j<n\\j\ne10}}F_j\right)
\frac{F_{10}}{G_{10}}.
$$

Since $n>10$, this product contains the three factors

$$
F_0=3,\qquad F_1=5,\qquad F_2=17.
$$

Therefore,

$$
255=3\cdot5\cdot17
\mid
\frac{2^{2^n}-1}{G_{10}}.
$$

Equation (2) then gives

$$
\boxed{255\mid t.}
$$

Crocker proves that the construction yields infinitely many distinct integers $t$, all belonging to $E$. Consequently,

$$
\boxed{
\#\{t\in E:t\le x,\ 255\mid t\}
\longrightarrow\infty
\quad(x\to\infty).
}
$$

This proves the existence of infinitely many exceptional integers satisfying the conjectured divisibility condition. It does **not** prove that every exceptional integer satisfies it.

### 2.2. Why the construction does not prove the full conjecture

The divisibility arises because Crocker deliberately builds his exceptional integers using products of Fermat-number factors.

The missing implication would be

$$
n\in E,\ n>5
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
3\mid n,\quad5\mid n,\quad17\mid n.
$$

Crocker's construction establishes this only for its particular family. It does not characterize every possible element of $E$.

Moreover, the Chinese remainder theorem cannot establish the missing implication by itself: it produces integers in selected residue classes, but does not show that exceptional integers outside those classes are impossible.

Thus the existence proof and the proposed classification remain logically distinct.

### 2.3. Relationship to the three-powers conjecture

**Proved conditional implication; already noted in OEIS.**

Assume (C). Let $n>7$ be odd.

If $255\nmid n$, then (C) directly gives

$$
n=p+2^a+2^b.
$$

If $255\mid n$, set $m=n-2$. Then $m>5$, $m$ is odd, and

$$
255\nmid m.
$$

Applying (C),

$$
m=p+2^a+2^b,
$$

hence

$$
n=p+2^a+2^b+2.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
(C)\implies
\text{every odd }n>7
\text{ is a prime plus at most three positive powers of }2.
}
$$

This is related to the conjecture of Andrew Granville and K. Soundararajan, discussed in *A binary additive problem of Erdős and the order of $2$ mod $p^2$*, *The Ramanujan Journal* (1998), 283–298.

The implication is one-way. An odd integer may require three powers without violating (C), provided it is divisible by 255. Conversely, a proof of the general three-powers statement would not automatically establish (C).

I therefore do not claim an equivalence or a reduction to a previously solved problem.

### 2.4. Outcome of the proof attempt

No proof or counterexample to the unrestricted statement (C) was obtained.

The strongest unconditional mathematical result identified here is that Crocker's existing infinite family satisfies the divisibility condition. The argument above makes that consequence explicit, but it follows directly from his published construction and should not be claimed as new.

## 3. Computational verification

**Status: computed, not an infinite proof.**

I performed an exhaustive sieve for every positive odd integer through

$$
\boxed{N=1\,200\,000\,000}.
$$

The computation uses a sieve of Eratosthenes and integer bitsets to generate every possible representation $p+2^a+2^b$.

### 3.1. Reproducible Python program

The following is the streamlined version of the algorithm executed. It requires Python 3 and NumPy, and uses approximately gigabyte-scale memory.

```python

from math import isqrt
import numpy as np

N = 1_200_000_000

# Sieve of Eratosthenes
prime = bytearray(b'\x01') * (N + 1)
prime[:2] = b'\x00\x00'

for p in range(2, isqrt(N) + 1):
    if prime[p]:
        prime[p*p:N+1:p] = (
            b'\x00' * ((N - p*p) // p + 1)
        )

# An odd n cannot use p = 2.
prime[2] = 0

# Bit i is 1 precisely when i is an odd prime.
P = int.from_bytes(
    np.packbits(
        np.frombuffer(prime, dtype=np.uint8),
        bitorder="little"
    ).tobytes(),
    "little"
)
del prime

mask = (1 << (N + 1)) - 1

# All allowable sums of two positive powers of 2
S = {
    (1 << a) + (1 << b)
    for a in range(1, (N - 3).bit_length())
    for b in range(1, a + 1)
    if (1 << a) + (1 << b) <= N - 3
}

# Bit n is 1 if n = p + 2^a + 2^b.
represented = 0
for s in sorted(S):
    represented |= (P << s) & mask

# Bits at odd positions only
odd = int.from_bytes(
    b'\xaa' * ((N + 8) // 8), "little"
) & mask

missing = odd & ~represented
terms = []

while missing:
    bit = missing & -missing
    terms.append(bit.bit_length() - 1)
    missing ^= bit

print("N =", N)
print("distinct_two_power_sums =", len(S))
print("missing_odd_terms =", terms)
print(
    "all_missing_gt_5_divisible_by_255 =",
    all(n % 255 == 0 for n in terms if n > 5)
)
```

**Why the computation is exhaustive.**

The prime sieve identifies all primes up to $N$. Every representation of an odd number must use an odd prime, because $2+2^a+2^b$ is even for positive $a,b$.

Every allowable pair $(a,b)$ can be reordered to satisfy $a\ge b\ge1$. The set `S` therefore includes its sum, including equal-exponent cases.

For every $s\in S$, shifting the prime bitset by $s$ marks precisely the integers of the form $p+s$. Taking the bitwise union marks every representable integer through $N$.

Finally, intersecting the complement with the odd-position mask returns exactly the unrepresentable odd integers.

This algorithm tests the complete representation predicate, not merely whether the OEIS-listed terms are multiples of 255.

### 3.5. Limitations

The exhaustive computation establishes the conjecture only through $1.2\times10^9$. OEIS already contains exceptional terms beyond this range, with its published b-file extending below $10^{12}$. Therefore, this computation is an independent verification, not a record extension or a new discovery.

The absence of counterexamples in a finite range cannot settle the universal conjecture.
