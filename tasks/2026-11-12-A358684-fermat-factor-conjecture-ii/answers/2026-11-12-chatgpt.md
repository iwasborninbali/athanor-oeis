# OEIS A358684 — Conjecture II: Status and Proof Attempt

**Investigation date:** 12 November 2026 **Target:** $a(n)\sim 2^n$ as $n\to\infty$ **Verdict:** Open; no proof or counterexample found.

## 1. Status and literature review

### 1.1. The conjecture

Let

$$
F_n=2^{2^n}+1,\qquad
p_n=P^-(F_n),
$$

where $P^-(m)$ denotes the smallest prime factor of $m$, including $m$ itself when $m$ is prime.

OEIS [A358684](https://oeis.org/A358684) defines $a(n)$ as the least nonnegative integer $k$ satisfying

$$
p_n>2^{2^n-k}.
$$

Conjecture II states

$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a(n)}{2^n}=1.}
$$

The statement is mathematically well-defined, but remains unproved in the sources examined.

### 1.2. Decisive existing results

**Lorenzo Sauras-Altuzarra, OEIS A358684, comment dated 11 January 2026.**

The entry explicitly states:

> Conjecture II is thus the question whether floor(log_2(A093179(n)))/2^n tends to zero or not.

The entry also observes that infinitely many Fermat primes would invalidate the conjecture. Its exact formula is

$$
a(n)=2^n-\lfloor\log_2 p_n\rfloor.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
a(n)\sim2^n
\iff
\log_2 p_n=o(2^n).
}
$$

This equivalence is **already known**, not a new discovery.

**Kota Saito, 2025, *Intervals without primes near an iterated linear recurrence sequence*, arXiv:2504.14968v1, §1.**

Saito writes:

> It is a long-standing problem to prove (or disprove) the infinitude of the composite Fermat numbers.

This is relevant because Conjecture II would imply infinitely many composite Fermat numbers—and, more strongly, that all sufficiently large Fermat numbers are composite. Saito's paper does not establish Conjecture II.

## 2. Proof attempt and rigorous partial results

### 2.1. Precise equivalent formulations

**Proposition 1 (proved; known equivalence).** The following statements are equivalent:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{(i)}\quad &a(n)\sim 2^n,\\
\text{(ii)}\quad &\log_2 p_n=o(2^n),\\
\text{(iii)}\quad &p_n=F_n^{o(1)},\\
\text{(iv)}\quad &\forall\varepsilon>0,\ \exists N_\varepsilon:
\ \forall n\ge N_\varepsilon,\quad
p_n<2^{\varepsilon 2^n}.
\end{aligned}
$$

*Proof.*

Put $b_n=\lfloor\log_2 p_n\rfloor$. Since $p_n$ is odd,

$$
2^{b_n}<p_n<2^{b_n+1}.
$$

For an integer $k\ge0$, the defining inequality

$$
p_n>2^{2^n-k}
$$

holds exactly when $2^n-k\le b_n$. Moreover, $p_n\le F_n$ implies $b_n\le2^n$. Therefore the minimum permissible $k$ is

$$
\boxed{a(n)=2^n-b_n.}
$$

Consequently,

$$
\frac{a(n)}{2^n}
=1-\frac{\lfloor\log_2 p_n\rfloor}{2^n}.
$$

Because

$$
0\le\log_2 p_n-\lfloor\log_2 p_n\rfloor<1,
$$

(i) and (ii) are equivalent.

Also,

$$
\log_2 F_n=2^n+o(1),
$$

so (ii) is equivalent to

$$
\frac{\log p_n}{\log F_n}\longrightarrow0,
$$

which is (iii). Finally, the definition of little-$o$ gives the equivalence with (iv). $\square$

**Attribution:** The exact formula and the equivalence (i)–(ii) appear in OEIS A358684 (Sauras-Altuzarra, January 2026). The other formulations follow immediately and are not claimed as novel.

### 2.2. An unconditional gap theorem

**Proposition 2 (proved; elementary).** For every $n\ge1$,

$$
\boxed{
a(n)=0
\quad\text{or}\quad
a(n)\ge2^{n-1}+1.
}
$$

Furthermore,

$$
a(n)=0\iff F_n\text{ is prime}.
$$

*Proof.*

First, if $F_n$ is prime, then $p_n=F_n=2^{2^n}+1$, and therefore

$$
\lfloor\log_2p_n\rfloor=2^n,
$$

giving $a(n)=0$.

Conversely, if $a(n)=0$, then

$$
p_n\ge2^{2^n}.
$$

But $p_n$ is odd and divides the odd integer $2^{2^n}+1$. Hence $p_n=F_n$, so $F_n$ is prime.

Now suppose $F_n$ is composite.

Set

$$
M=2^{2^{n-1}}.
$$

Then

$$
F_n=M^2+1.
$$

Since $F_n$ is composite, its smallest prime factor satisfies

$$
p_n\le\sqrt{M^2+1}<M+1.
$$

As $p_n$ is odd and $M$ is even, we obtain $p_n<M$. Thus

$$
\lfloor\log_2p_n\rfloor\le2^{n-1}-1.
$$

Using Proposition 1,

$$
a(n)\ge2^n-(2^{n-1}-1)
=2^{n-1}+1.
$$

This proves both assertions. $\square$

**Interpretation.** The normalized sequence exhibits a gap:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{a(n)}{2^n}\in
\{0\}\cup
\left[\frac12+2^{-n},1\right].
}
$$

There are no intermediate values between zero and approximately one-half.

This elementary bound does not approach the required asymptotic limit. It is included as a rigorous unconditional partial result, not as a claimed new theorem.

### 2.3. Upper bound from the structure of Fermat factors

**Proposition 3 (known).** For every $n\ge2$,

$$
\boxed{0\le a(n)\le2^n-n-2.}
$$

*Proof.*

A classical theorem about prime divisors of Fermat numbers states that every prime $p\mid F_n$, for $n\ge2$, satisfies

$$
p\equiv1\pmod{2^{n+2}}.
$$

This is recorded in OEIS A000215 and the OEIS Wiki.

Therefore,

$$
p_n\ge2^{n+2}+1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\lfloor\log_2p_n\rfloor\ge n+2.
$$

Substitution into Proposition 1 gives the result. $\square$

Combining Propositions 2 and 3, every composite $F_n$, $n\ge2$, satisfies

$$
\boxed{
2^{n-1}+1\le a(n)\le2^n-n-2.
}
$$

These bounds hold independently of any unproved conjecture.

### 2.4. A sufficient condition involving the number of prime factors

Let $\Omega(m)$ count the prime factors of $m$ with multiplicity.

**Proposition 4 (proved, conditional implication).**

$$
\boxed{
\Omega(F_n)\longrightarrow\infty
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a(n)\sim2^n.
}
$$

*Proof.*

Write

$$
F_n=q_1q_2\cdots q_r,
\qquad r=\Omega(F_n),
$$

with the $q_i$ prime.

Because each $q_i\ge p_n$,

$$
p_n^r\le F_n.
$$

Taking logarithms,

$$
\frac{\log_2p_n}{2^n}
\le
\frac{\log_2F_n}{r\,2^n}.
$$

But

$$
\frac{\log_2F_n}{2^n}\longrightarrow1.
$$

Therefore, if $r=\Omega(F_n)\to\infty$, then

$$
\frac{\log_2p_n}{2^n}\longrightarrow0.
$$

Proposition 1 proves the conclusion. $\square$

This is a useful sufficient condition, but the required hypothesis is not established. It does not resolve the conjecture.

### 2.5. Where the proof stops

The central difficulty is the absence of a uniform upper bound of the form

$$
p_n\le2^{\varepsilon2^n}
$$

for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and all sufficiently large $n$.

The known structural restrictions on Fermat factors give lower bounds for their size. Elementary compositeness gives only an upper bound of square-root order.

Neither is sufficient to obtain

$$
p_n=F_n^{o(1)}.
$$

In particular, merely proving that every sufficiently large Fermat number is composite would yield the square-root upper bound, but not the conjectured subpower bound.

**Result of the proof attempt:** The conjecture remains unresolved. The gap theorem and factor-count criterion are rigorously proved elementary observations; no claim of mathematical novelty is made.
