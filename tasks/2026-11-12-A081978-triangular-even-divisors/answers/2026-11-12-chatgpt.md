## 1. Status: Open, with a conditional proof

**Verdict as of November 12, 2026: The conjecture in OEIS A081978 remains open, as far as the sources I could verify establish.** I found neither an unconditional proof nor a counterexample.

There is, however, a straightforward reduction showing that **Schinzel's Hypothesis H implies the entire conjecture**. I also found an apparent sign error in the OEIS commentary that can be corrected rigorously.

### Precise statement

Let

$$
T_k=\frac{k(k+1)}2,\qquad k\geq1,
$$

and let $\tau(m)$ denote the number of positive divisors of $m$.

The conjecture is

$$
\boxed{\forall j\geq2\ \exists k\geq1:
\tau(T_k)=2j.}
$$

This is equivalent to $a(2j)>0$ for every integer $j>1$ in A081978. It is well-posed.

### Literature and source checks

**OEIS A081978.** Jon E. Schoenfield's commentary, dated May 26, 2014, explicitly states:

> Conjecture: a(2j) > 0 for all j > 1.

The same comment reports computational verification through $2j=1000$. This is evidence, not proof. The live entry still presents the statement as a conjecture.

**Related OEIS entry A323747.** Jon E. Schoenfield (2019, updated 2021) states the analogous conjecture that its terms are positive whenever the associated triangular-number index specifies an even divisor count. This independently confirms the problem's conjectural treatment in OEIS, rather than supplying a proof.

**Known structural result.** Robert Israel (October 26, 2015, OEIS A063440) gives the identities

$$
\tau(T_{2m})=\tau(m)\tau(2m+1),
$$

$$
\tau(T_{2m+1})=\tau(2m+1)\tau(m+1).
$$

These identities are elementary and are the starting point for the proof below.

**Named conjecture.** Andrzej Schinzel and Wacław Sierpiński, *Sur certaines hypothèses concernant les nombres premiers*, *Acta Arithmetica* **4** (1958), 185–208, DOI: 10.4064/aa-4-3-185-208, formulated Hypothesis H. A later explicit formulation by H. W. Hagedorn, *Sieve methods and polynomial sequences*, *Acta Arithmetica* **28** (1975), states:

> Then there exist infinitely many integers n such that each f_i(n) is prime.

This is the conclusion of Hypothesis H under its irreducibility and admissibility conditions.

**Recent literature search.** I searched web-indexed arXiv and Zenodo material both by `A081978` and by terms including *triangular numbers*, *exactly n divisors*, *even divisor counts*, and *conjecture*, with particular attention to October 15–November 12, 2026. I found no relevant proof or counterexample in the returned results. I also checked related OEIS entries and searched for indexed mathematical literature involving this problem.

Limitations: the direct arXiv and Zenodo search interfaces were not accessible through the browsing tool, and I did not conduct an exhaustive subscription-database search of MathSciNet or zbMATH. ## 2. Proof attempt and partial results

### 2.1. The entire conjecture follows from Schinzel's Hypothesis H

PROVED CONDITIONAL IMPLICATION

**Theorem.** If Schinzel's Hypothesis H is true, then for every integer $j\geq2$, there exist infinitely many triangular numbers having exactly $2j$ divisors.

**Proof.**

Fix an arbitrary integer $j\geq2$. Consider the polynomials

$$
f_1(x)=x,\qquad
f_2(x)=2x^{j-1}-1.
$$

We check the hypotheses required by Schinzel's Hypothesis H.

**Irreducibility.**

The polynomial $f_1(x)=x$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.

The reciprocal polynomial of $f_2$, up to multiplication by $-1$, is

$$
x^{j-1}-2.
$$

This is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ by Eisenstein's criterion at the prime $2$. Therefore $f_2$ is irreducible as well.

**Admissibility.**

The product is

$$
F(x)=x(2x^{j-1}-1).
$$

Since

$$
F(1)=1,
$$

no prime divides $F(x)$ for every integer $x$. Thus the pair is admissible.

**Application of Hypothesis H.**

Hypothesis H implies the existence of infinitely many positive integers $p$ such that both

$$
p\quad\text{and}\quad q=2p^{j-1}-1
$$

are prime.

For any such $p$, set

$$
k=2p^{j-1}-1=q.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_k
&=\frac{k(k+1)}2\\
&=\frac{(2p^{j-1}-1)(2p^{j-1})}{2}\\
&=p^{j-1}q.
\end{aligned}
$$

Because $p$ and $q$ are distinct primes,

$$
\tau(T_k)=\big((j-1)+1\big)(1+1)=2j.
$$

The infinitely many values of $p$ supplied by Hypothesis H produce infinitely many distinct triangular numbers.

Since $j\geq2$ was arbitrary, the theorem follows. $\square$

**Conclusion:**

$$
\boxed{\text{Schinzel's Hypothesis H}
\ \Longrightarrow\
a(2j)>0\text{ for every }j\geq2.}
$$

The stronger Bateman–Horn conjecture also implies the result.

**Important qualification:** This establishes an implication, not equivalence. Failure to find primes of the displayed special form would not refute A081978, since other triangular-number factorizations may work.

The argument does not establish the unconditional conjecture, and I make no claim that this elementary conditional reduction is previously unpublished.

### 2.2. Exact classification when half the divisor count is prime

PROVED STRUCTURAL LEMMA

Let $j\geq3$ be an odd prime and put $r=j-1$. Then a triangular number with $2j$ divisors must have the form

$$
T_k=p^r q,
$$

where $p,q$ are distinct primes.

Indeed, $2j$ can be factored into integers greater than $1$ only as $2\cdot j$. Also, except for $T_2=3$, every triangular number has at least two distinct prime factors, because its standard coprime-factor decomposition has two factors greater than $1$.

Using

$$
T_{2m}=m(2m+1),\qquad
T_{2m-1}=m(2m-1),
$$

we obtain four possibilities:

| Allocation of factors | Required value of $q$ |
| --- | --- |
| $m=p^r,\ 2m-1=q$ | $2p^r-1$ |
| $m=p^r,\ 2m+1=q$ | $2p^r+1$ |
| $2m-1=p^r,\ m=q$ | $(p^r+1)/2$ |
| $2m+1=p^r,\ m=q$ | $(p^r-1)/2$ |

The last possibility is impossible.

If $p=2$, then $(p^r-1)/2$ is not an integer.

If $p$ is odd, $r$ is even, so

$$
p^r\equiv1\pmod8.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\frac{p^r-1}{2}\equiv0\pmod4.
$$

It is greater than $2$, hence cannot be prime.

Therefore the exact classification reduces to three possibilities:

$$
\boxed{
q\in\left\{
2p^r-1,\quad
2p^r+1,\quad
\frac{p^r+1}{2}
\right\}.
}
$$

There are two further restrictions.

First, if $p\ne3$,

$$
p^r\equiv1\pmod3,
$$

so $2p^r+1$ is divisible by $3$ and greater than $3$. Hence the second form requires $p=3$.

Second, if $r$ has an odd factor $d>1$, write $r=ds$. Then

$$
p^r+1=(p^s+1)
\left(p^{s(d-1)}-p^{s(d-2)}+\cdots-p^s+1\right).
$$

For odd $p$, dividing by $2$ still leaves two factors greater than $1$. Therefore $(p^r+1)/2$ can be prime only when $r$ is a power of $2$.

Since $j=r+1$ is prime, this means $j$ must be a Fermat prime.

Thus, when $j\geq3$ is a prime that is not a Fermat prime, the conjecture at $n=2j$ is equivalent to the following explicit prime-existence condition:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{gathered}
\exists\text{ prime }p:
2p^{j-1}-1\text{ is prime}\\
\text{or}\\
2\cdot3^{j-1}+1\text{ is prime}.
\end{gathered}}
$$

This is a precise special-prime formulation, not a solution of the resulting prime-existence problem.

### 2.3. A sign error in OEIS A081978

PROVED CORRECTION

The current A081978 commentary lists the third function as

$$
f_3(p^r)=\frac{p^r-1}{2}.
$$

It also claims that the $n=514$ case uses this function with $p=331$.

But the preceding proof shows that this expression cannot be prime for any odd prime $j$.

The correct third function is

$$
\boxed{f_3(p^r)=\frac{p^r+1}{2}.}
$$

This is consistent with the OEIS observation at $n=514$. In particular, the candidate

$$
q=\frac{331^{256}+1}{2}
$$

passes SymPy's probable-prime test, whereas

$$
\frac{331^{256}-1}{2}
$$

is divisible by $3$.

This is a correction to the explanatory comment, not a counterexample to the conjecture. The probable-prime test is not presented as a formal primality certificate for the 645-digit candidate.

### 2.4. An unconditional weaker theorem

PROVED

For every $j\geq2$, there exists a triangular number whose divisor count is **divisible by** $2j$.

Take $k=2^j-1$. Then

$$
T_k=2^{j-1}(2^j-1)
$$

and

$$
\tau(T_k)=j\,\tau(2^j-1).
$$

Since $2^j-1\equiv3\pmod4$, this number cannot be a square. Its divisor count is therefore even.

Hence

$$
2j\mid\tau(T_{2^j-1}).
$$

This elementary observation illustrates the obstacle: enforcing divisibility by $2j$ is substantially easier than achieving the exact count $2j$.

## 3. Computational checks

COMPUTED — INTEGER-EXACT ENUMERATION

I ran a Python 3 program examining all

$$
1\leq k\leq1{,}000{,}000.
$$

It computes the divisor-count function by a divisor sieve, then uses the coprime-factor identity to obtain $\tau(T_k)$.

It also tests the structural classification above for every triangular number in this range having $2j$ divisors with $j>2$ prime.

### Reproducible program

```python

from array import array
from math import isqrt

K = 1_000_000
N = K + 2

D = array('H', [0]) * N
for d in range(1, N):
    for m in range(d, N, d):
        D[m] += 1

spf = array('I', range(N))
for p in range(2, isqrt(N-1) + 1):
    if spf[p] == p:
        for m in range(p*p, N, p):
            if spf[m] == m:
                spf[m] = p

def factor(x):
    z = {}
    while x > 1:
        p = spf[x]
        z[p] = z.get(p, 0) + 1
        x //= p
    return z

first = {}
forms = {
    "2p^r-1": 0,
    "2p^r+1": 0,
    "(p^r+1)/2": 0
}
prime_index_cases = 0

for k in range(1, K + 1):
    if k % 2 == 0:
        a, b = k // 2, k + 1
    else:
        a, b = k, (k + 1) // 2

    t = D[a] * D[b]
    first.setdefault(t, k)
    j = t // 2

    if t % 2 == 0 and j > 2 and spf[j] == j:
        fac = factor(a) | factor(b)
        assert len(fac) == 2
        assert sorted(fac.values()) == [1, j-1]

        p = next(x for x, e in fac.items() if e == j-1)
        q = next(x for x, e in fac.items() if e == 1)
        x = p ** (j-1)

        if q == 2*x - 1:
            forms["2p^r-1"] += 1
        elif q == 2*x + 1:
            forms["2p^r+1"] += 1
        elif x % 2 == 1 and q == (x+1)//2:
            forms["(p^r+1)/2"] += 1
        else:
            raise AssertionError((k, j, p, q))

        prime_index_cases += 1

print("triangular indices tested:", K)
print("distinct divisor counts:", len(first))
print("even divisor counts:", sum(t % 2 == 0 for t in first))
print("missing even n in [2,100]:",
      [t for t in range(2,101,2) if t not in first])
print("prime-half cases:", prime_index_cases)
print("three allowed forms:", forms)
print("f3-minus at p=331,r=256 divisible by 3:",
      ((331**256-1)//2) % 3 == 0)
```
