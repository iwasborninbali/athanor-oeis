Their theorem applies directly to independent uniform $\{0,1\}$ entries and yields a quantitative strengthening of the OEIS conjecture.

### Precise statement

Let

$$
p_n=\frac{A046747(n)}{2^{n^2}}
=\Pr(\det M_n=0),
$$

where $M_n$ has independent, uniformly distributed $\{0,1\}$ entries.

The OEIS conjecture states

$$
\boxed{p_n\sim\binom{n+1}{2}2^{-(n-1)}
=n(n+1)2^{-n}.}
$$

Hu–Song–Wu's result implies the stronger estimate

$$
\boxed{
p_n=n(n+1)2^{-n}
+O_\varepsilon(n^{1+\varepsilon}2^{-n})
}
$$

for every fixed $0<\varepsilon<1$.

Consequently,

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}
\frac{A046747(n)}
{2^{n^2}n(n+1)2^{-n}}=1.
$$

## 2. Proof — deduction from the known theorem

No new proof of the underlying random-matrix theorem is necessary. The following gives a checkable deduction of the precise OEIS statement from Hu–Song–Wu.

### 2.1 Upper bound

**Known theorem (Hu–Song–Wu, 2026, Theorem 1.1).**

For a random matrix with independent entries uniform on a fixed finite set $S$ of cardinality $q$, define

$$
a_n(S)=2n\Pr(\xi=0)^n+
n(n-1)\left(q^{-n}+\Pr(\xi=-\xi')^n\right),
$$

where $\xi,\xi'$ are independent uniform samples from $S$.

For every fixed $\varepsilon>0$, their theorem gives, at zero smallest singular value,

$$
\Pr(\det M_n=0)
\leq a_n(S)+C_\varepsilon n^{1+\varepsilon}q^{-n}.
$$

The constant depends on $S$ and $\varepsilon$, not on $n$.

**Deduction for $S=\{0,1\}$.**

Here,

$$
q=2,\qquad
\Pr(\xi=0)=\frac12,\qquad
\Pr(\xi=-\xi')=\frac14.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_n(\{0,1\})
&=2n\,2^{-n}
+n(n-1)(2^{-n}+4^{-n})\\
&=n(n+1)2^{-n}+n(n-1)4^{-n}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
p_n\leq n(n+1)2^{-n}
+n(n-1)4^{-n}
+C_\varepsilon n^{1+\varepsilon}2^{-n}.
}
$$

This is the required upper bound, directly from the existing theorem.

### 2.2 Lower bound: elementary singularities

**Elementary argument, proved here.**

Consider the following events:

- A specified row or column is zero.
- A specified pair of rows or columns is identical.

Each event forces singularity.

There are

$$
2n+2\binom n2=n(n+1)
$$

such events, each with probability exactly $2^{-n}$.

For any two distinct events, their intersection has probability at most

$$
2^{-(2n-1)}.
$$

To verify this, consider the possible pairs: two zero-vector conditions, two equality conditions, or one of each. Conditions involving disjoint rows or columns impose $2n$ independent binary restrictions. When a row and column intersect, one restriction may be redundant, leaving $2n-1$. Equality conditions sharing a row similarly impose $2n$ restrictions. Thus at least $2n-1$ restrictions remain in every case.

By the first two terms of inclusion–exclusion,

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_n
&\geq n(n+1)2^{-n}\\
&\quad-\binom{n(n+1)}2\,2^{-(2n-1)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{
p_n\geq n(n+1)2^{-n}-O(n^4 4^{-n}).
}
$$

Combining the two bounds gives

$$
p_n=n(n+1)2^{-n}
+O_\varepsilon(n^{1+\varepsilon}2^{-n}),
$$

because the exponentially smaller $n^4 4^{-n}$ error is absorbed by the stated error.

For any fixed $0<\varepsilon<1$,

$$
\left|
\frac{p_n}{n(n+1)2^{-n}}-1
\right|
=O_\varepsilon(n^{-1+\varepsilon})\longrightarrow0.
$$

This completes the deduction of the OEIS conjecture.

### 2.3 An exact equivalence with random sign matrices

**Known identity (Kevin Costello, OEIS A057982, 18 May 2005).**

Let $b(N)$ count singular $N\times N$ matrices with entries in $\{-1,1\}$. Then

$$
b(N)=2^{2N-1}A046747(N-1).
$$

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{
\Pr(M_n^{\{0,1\}}\text{ singular})
=
\Pr(M_{n+1}^{\{-1,1\}}\text{ singular}).
}
$$

This exact identity provides a second route to the conjecture through the classical Rademacher singularity problem.

For completeness, the identity can be verified by multiplying each column of a sign matrix by its first-row entry, making the first row entirely ones. Subtract the first column from the others. The lower-right $n\times n$ block, after nonzero row rescaling, becomes a binary matrix, with singularity preserved. Each binary matrix has precisely $2^{2n+1}$ preimages. Dividing by the total number of sign matrices establishes the probability identity.

Hu–Song–Wu prove

$$
\Pr(M_N^{\{-1,1\}}\text{ singular})
=(2+o(1))N^2 2^{-N}.
$$

Substituting $N=n+1$ gives

$$
p_n=(1+o(1))(n+1)^2 2^{-n}
\sim n(n+1)2^{-n},
$$

independently confirming the desired leading asymptotic. The more precise error estimate above comes from their general theorem.

## 3. Computational verification

**Computed:** exact singular-matrix counts for $1\leq n\leq5$, plus exact counts of matrices having at least one elementary singularity.

Rather than enumerate all $2^{25}$ matrices of order five, the program enumerates unordered selections of $n$ distinct nonzero binary rows. Each full-rank matrix corresponds to exactly one such selection and one of its $n!$ row orderings.

Gaussian elimination is performed modulo 101. This is exact for $n\leq5$: Hadamard's determinant bound gives

$$
|\det M|\leq n^{n/2}\leq5^{5/2}<101.
$$

Thus a nonzero integer determinant in this range cannot vanish modulo 101.

```python

from itertools import combinations
from math import factorial

P = 101

def independent(rows, n):
    basis = {}
    for bits in rows:
        v = [(bits >> j) & 1 for j in range(n)]
        for j in range(n):
            if not v[j]:
                continue
            if j in basis:
                c = v[j]
                v = [(x - c*y) % P
                     for x, y in zip(v, basis[j])]
            else:
                inv = pow(v[j], -1, P)
                basis[j] = [(x*inv) % P for x in v]
                break
        else:
            return False
    return True

print("n singular elementary non_elementary_singular ratio")
for n in range(1, 6):
    indep_sets = no_elem_sets = 0
    for rows in combinations(range(1, 1 << n), n):
        indep_sets += independent(rows, n)

        cols = [
            sum(((r >> j) & 1) << i
                for i, r in enumerate(rows))
            for j in range(n)
        ]
        no_elem_sets += (
            0 not in cols and len(set(cols)) == n
        )

    all_matrices = 1 << (n*n)
    singular = all_matrices - factorial(n)*indep_sets
    elementary = all_matrices - factorial(n)*no_elem_sets
    ratio = (singular/all_matrices)/(n*(n+1)/2**n)

    print(n, singular, elementary,
          singular-elementary, f"{ratio:.9f}")
```

The first two numerical count columns and the difference are exact integers. The final column is the nine-decimal approximation to

All five singular-matrix counts agree with A046747. The independently computed elementary-event counts also verify that simple zero/equal-row-or-column obstructions do not account for every singular matrix at finite $n$.

The results illustrate an important limitation: these small values do not establish the asymptotic ratio. The convergence is an asymptotic theorem, not a finite-size numerical observation.
