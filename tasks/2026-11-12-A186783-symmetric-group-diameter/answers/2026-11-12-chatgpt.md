# OEIS A186783 — Status and proof attempt

**Research date:** 12 November 2026 **Subject:** Diameter and uniqueness of the farthest permutation in the symmetric group generated by $L,R,X$.

**Verdict:** The exact diameter conjecture has been proved in a preprint dated **12 November 2026**. The proposed permutation is also proved to attain the diameter. However, the stronger conjecture that it is the *unique* farthest permutation is not established by that theorem and remains open as far as the sources checked show.

## 1. Status and literature

### 1.1. The decisive new result

Sergei Uskov, Alexander Chervov, Fedor Levkovich-Maslyuk, Ivan Litvak, Andrei Smolensky, Alexander Soibelman, and Dia Taha (2026), *The exact diameter of the symmetric group $S_n$ with LRX generators*, Hexagon preprint **2611.00333v1**, 12 November 2026.

Theorem 1.1, page 3:

> For every $n\geq4$ the diameter of $\Gamma_n$ equals $N$.

Here $N=n(n-1)/2$. The theorem additionally states that this distance is attained between the identity and

$$
p_n^*=(2,1,n,n-1,\ldots,3).
$$

Thus the paper proves, for all $n\geq4$,

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{diam}(\Gamma_n)=\frac{n(n-1)}2}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{d(e,p_n^*)=\frac{n(n-1)}2}.
$$

**Status: proved in the cited preprint, not independently reproved here.**

The authors provide a constructive upper-bound argument, an independent lower-bound argument, finite C++ certificates, and a Lean 4 formalization.

The associated formalization is available in the [LRX Lean repository, release v1.0.0](https://github.com/snow-ghost/lrx-lean/releases/tag/v1.0.0), published on 10 November 2026. Its release notes identify `LRX.exact_diameter_all` and `LRX.sharp_diameter_all` as main results and report successful kernel and CI checks. I inspected the release information but did not independently run its full Lean build.

### 1.2. Does this prove uniqueness?

**No. The distinction is essential.**

Section 1.1, page 3 of the new paper explicitly describes the uniqueness assertion as a conjecture:

> The entry also records the conjecture that the farthest element is unique

Theorem 1.1 proves that the designated permutation reaches the diameter, not that every other permutation falls strictly below it.

Section 1.2 reports computational uniqueness for $4\le n\le9$, but the general upper-bound proof establishes only

$$
d(e,g)\leq\binom n2
\quad\text{for every }g\in S_n.
$$

It does not establish the strict inequality

$$
d(e,g)<\binom n2
\quad\text{for every }g\ne p_n^*.
$$

Accordingly, the exact diameter is **proved**, while uniqueness is **still conjectural** on the evidence inspected.

### 1.3. Earlier work

- **Chervov, Soibelman, Lytkin, et al. (2025),** *CayleyPy RL: Pathfinding and Reinforcement Learning on Cayley Graphs*, arXiv:2502.18663, Section 4.2. The authors reported exhaustive calculations through $n=14$, observed a unique farthest element, and conjectured the general pattern. The decisive wording was: “we observe that this element is always unique”.
- **Grigorii Antiufeev (2026),** *A Lower Bound for the Diameter of Cayley Graph of the Symmetric Group $S_n$*, arXiv:2601.08715, Theorem 2. This established the lower bound $n(n-1)/2$ for $n\ge4$, but not a matching universal upper bound or uniqueness.
- **Uskov et al. (2026),** *Exact diameter of the symmetric group with LX generators*, Hexagon 2611.00039v1, 3 November 2026. This solves the related **directed** two-generator problem, OEIS A039745, not the undirected LRX problem.
- **OEIS A186783**, Tony Bartoletti (2011), currently records the uniqueness conjecture and the earlier diameter formula as conjectural. Its displayed terms end at $n=13$. The entry has not incorporated the new theorem in the version inspected.

The 12 November LRX preprint also notes that Andrea Guerrieri independently obtained a proof of the diameter, but cites that work as *in preparation*. I found no public, checkable uniqueness theorem from it.

**Search coverage:** I checked the OEIS entry, its related entries and linked literature, the OEIS wiki via indexed searches, arXiv and Zenodo using the sequence number and relevant keywords, searches of MathSciNet/zbMATH-indexed literature, and recent preprints covering 15 October–12 November 2026. The decisive new result was located on Hexagon, not arXiv. I did not inspect subscription-only MathSciNet or zbMATH records directly. No independently verifiable general uniqueness proof was found.

## 2. Proof, counterexample, and strongest partial result

### 2.1. Precise statement

For $n\ge2$, consider permutations of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ in one-line notation. Define moves

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(a_1,\ldots,a_n)&=(a_2,\ldots,a_n,a_1),\\
R(a_1,\ldots,a_n)&=(a_n,a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1}),\\
X(a_1,\ldots,a_n)&=(a_2,a_1,a_3,\ldots,a_n).
\end{aligned}
$$

Every move has unit cost.

Let $d_n(p,q)$ be the minimum number of moves transforming $p$ into $q$, and put

$$
e_n=(1,2,\ldots,n),\qquad
p_n^*=(2,1,n,n-1,\ldots,3).
$$

The remaining conjecture is

$$
\boxed{
\forall n\ge4,\quad
\{g\in S_n:d_n(e_n,g)=\tbinom n2\}
=\{p_n^*\}.
}
$$

This formulation correctly distinguishes the unique farthest vertex **from the identity** from diametral pairs throughout the graph.

### 2.2. Proven consequence of the new diameter theorem

**Claim (known consequence, proved).** For every $n\ge4$ and every permutation $q$, there exists a permutation at distance exactly $\binom n2$ from $q$.

**Proof.** The graph is a Cayley graph. Relabelling the symbols in every arrangement by a fixed permutation preserves all three moves: rotations and swapping the first two positions do not depend on the symbols' values.

Choose the relabelling taking $e_n$ to $q$, and let $q^*$ be its image of $p_n^*$. It follows that

$$
d_n(q,q^*)=d_n(e_n,p_n^*)
=\binom n2.
$$

The last equality is Theorem 1.1 of Uskov et al. Therefore every vertex has a diametral partner. $\square$

This is elementary vertex transitivity, not a new result or a proof of uniqueness.

### 2.3. A counterexample to the unrestricted wording at $n=3$

If the original OEIS comment is interpreted as asserting uniqueness for every positive $n$, it is false.

For $n=3$, the complete BFS layers from $123$ are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
B_0&=\{123\},\\
B_1&=\{213,231,312\},\\
B_2&=\{132,321\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Consequently,

$$
\operatorname{diam}(\Gamma_3)=2,
$$

and there are **two** farthest elements, $132$ and $321$.

Moreover, $p_3^*=213$ has distance $1$, not $2$.

**Status: proved by exhaustive enumeration of six permutations.**

This does not refute the intended $n\ge4$ conjecture; it shows that the domain restriction must be made explicit.

### 2.4. Remaining gap

For $n\ge4$, a proof of uniqueness must establish

$$
\forall g\in S_n\setminus\{p_n^*\},
\qquad
d_n(e_n,g)\leq\binom n2-1.
$$

The published upper bound of Uskov et al. is one move weaker for such targets. Tightening it would require analysing equality cases in their constructive routing argument, or finding an independent strict bound for all nonexceptional permutations.

I did not obtain such a general argument or a counterexample for $n\ge4$. The stronger uniqueness conjecture therefore remains unresolved in this report.

## 3. Independent computational check

I implemented a breadth-first search using Lehmer ranks to index permutations bijectively by integers from $0$ to $n!-1$. Every BFS edge is one of the three moves $L,R,X$, so the computed distances are exact. Each run checks the number of reached states, the diameter, the number of farthest permutations, and the distance of $p_n^*$.

### 3.1. Reproducible C++17 program

```

#include <algorithm>
#include <cstdint>
#include <cstdio>
#include <vector>
#include <numeric>
using namespace std;

uint32_t f[12];

uint32_t rankp(const vector<unsigned char>& a) {
    uint32_t z = 0;
    unsigned used = 0;
    int n = a.size();

    for (int i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
        unsigned m = (1u << a[i]) - 1;
        z += (a[i] - __builtin_popcount(used & m))
             * f[n-1-i];
        used |= 1u << a[i];
    }
    return z;
}

vector<unsigned char> unrankp(uint32_t z, int n) {
    vector<unsigned char> a(n);
    unsigned used = 0;

    for (int i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
        unsigned k = z / f[n-1-i];
        z %= f[n-1-i];

        for (int v = 0; v < n; ++v)
            if (!(used & (1u << v))) {
                if (k-- == 0) {
                    a[i] = v;
                    used |= 1u << v;
                    break;
                }
            }
    }
    return a;
}

int main() {
    f[0] = 1;
    for (int i = 1; i <= 11; ++i)
        f[i] = f[i-1] * i;

    for (int n = 1; n <= 10; ++n) {
        vector<int8_t> dist(f[n], -1);
        vector<uint32_t> q(f[n]);

        uint32_t head = 0, tail = 1;
        dist[0] = 0;
        q[0] = 0;

        int diameter = 0;
        uint32_t count = 0;

        vector<unsigned char> p(n);
        iota(p.begin(), p.end(), 0);

        vector<unsigned char> star(n);
        if (n == 1) star[0] = 0;
        else {
            star[0] = 1;
            star[1] = 0;
            for (int i = 2; i < n; ++i)
                star[i] = n + 1 - i;
        }

        uint32_t pstar = rankp(star);

        while (head < tail) {
            uint32_t v = q[head++];
            int d = dist[v];

            if (d > diameter) {
                diameter = d;
                count = 0;
            }
            if (d == diameter) ++count;

            p = unrankp(v, n);

            for (int move = 0; move < 3; ++move) {
                auto a = p;

                if (move == 0)
                    rotate(a.begin(), a.begin()+1, a.end());
                if (move == 1)
                    rotate(a.begin(), a.end()-1, a.end());
                if (move == 2 && n > 1)
                    swap(a[0], a[1]);

                uint32_t w = rankp(a);
                if (dist[w] == -1) {
                    dist[w] = d + 1;
                    q[tail++] = w;
                }
            }
        }

        printf(
            "n=%d states=%u diameter=%d "
            "farthest=%u pstar_distance=%d "
            "unique_pstar=%s\n",
            n, tail, diameter, count,
            (int)dist[pstar],
            count == 1 && dist[pstar] == diameter
                ? "yes" : "no"
        );
    }
}
```

### 3.3. Interpretation

**Computed (exactly):** For every $4\le n\le10$,

$$
d_n(e_n,p_n^*)=\operatorname{diam}(\Gamma_n)
=\binom n2,
$$

and precisely one permutation achieves this distance.

For $n=10$, the run exhaustively visited all $3{,}628{,}800$ permutations.

The computation is independent of the cited paper's implementation, but it does not extend the published computational range. The CayleyPy authors reported computations through $n=14$, whereas the OEIS entry itself currently lists diameter values through $n=13$.

**Proof of computational correctness:** Lehmer ranking and unranking are mutually inverse bijections. BFS begins at the identity and visits every previously unseen neighbour through each allowed generator. Standard BFS induction shows that each stored distance is the shortest path length. Once the queue is exhausted, the largest stored distance is the graph diameter, and counting its occurrences gives exactly the number of farthest vertices.

No numerical approximation, heuristic search, or unverified pruning is used.

## 4. Verdict

**As of 12 November 2026, OEIS A186783's exact diameter formula and the extremality of the proposed permutation are proved by Uskov, Chervov, Levkovich-Maslyuk, Litvak, Smolensky, Soibelman, and Taha, Hexagon preprint 2611.00333v1, Theorem 1.1, page 3. The uniqueness assertion for $n\ge4$ is not proved by that theorem and remains open on the checked evidence.** My independent exhaustive BFS confirms uniqueness for $4\le n\le10$, without extending earlier computations; it also confirms that the unrestricted statement fails at $n=3$. No new general theorem is claimed. The strongest useful OEIS update would cite the new proof of $a(n)=n(n-1)/2$ for all $n\ge4$, note that $p_n^*$ is provably diametral, retain uniqueness as a separate conjecture, and explicitly restrict it to $n\ge4$.
