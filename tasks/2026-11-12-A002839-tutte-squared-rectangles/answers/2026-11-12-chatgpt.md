# 1. Status of the conjecture

**Verdict: apparently still open as of November 12, 2026.** I found neither a proof nor a counterexample. However, one of the assumptions underlying Tutte's original argument was proved decades ago. This leads to a rigorous upper bound with exactly the conjectured leading constant.

Let $a_n$ denote OEIS A002839: the number of *simple perfect squared rectangles* of order $n$, including squared squares, counted up to rotations and reflections. *Perfect* means that all constituent squares have different sizes; *simple* means that the dissection contains no proper squared subrectangle. These are the definitions used in the current OEIS entry.

The precise conjecture is

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
a_n\sim \frac{4^n}{243\sqrt{\pi}\,n^{5/2}}
\qquad(n\to\infty).
}
\tag{1}
$$

Here $f_n\sim g_n$ means $f_n/g_n\to1$.

### Primary sources and what they establish

**William T. Tutte (1963).** In *“A Census of Planar Maps,” Canadian Journal of Mathematics* **15** (1963), 249–271, §9, p. 267, Tutte writes:

> “Experience suggests that an unsymmetrical c-net nearly always gives rise to n simple perfect rectangles of order n − 1.”

He then states the asymptotic in (1) as a conjecture, not a theorem. The crucial word is *suggests*: no estimate for the proportion of networks yielding imperfect or otherwise inadmissible dissections is proved there.

**L. B. Richmond and N. C. Wormald (1982).** Their paper *“The asymptotic number of convex polyhedra,” Transactions of the American Mathematical Society* **273** (1982), 721–735, DOI [10.1090/S0002-9947-1982-0667170-5](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0002-9947-1982-0667170-5), establishes the necessary asymptotic enumeration of unrooted 3-connected planar maps. University

**E. A. Bender and N. C. Wormald (1985).** Their paper *“Almost All Convex Polyhedra Are Asymmetric,” Canadian Journal of Mathematics* **37** (1985), 854–871, identifies the Richmond–Wormald result as covering “all n-edged 3-connected planar maps.” Corollary 4.4, p. 870, explains that Tutte's rooted-map asymptotic “can be divided by 4n” to enumerate unrooted maps asymptotically.

Thus, **Tutte's assumption that almost all relevant planar maps are asymmetric is known to be true**. That result alone does not establish the squared-rectangle conjecture: the unproved step concerns how often deleting an edge produces a *perfect* dissection.

**Current OEIS status.** A002839 still explicitly labels (1) a *conjecture*. The related entries A219766 (nonsquare perfect rectangles) and A220167 (all simple squared rectangles) also retain conjectural asymptotic statements. In particular, A220167 does not constitute an independent proof.

I found no relevant proof or counterexample in that period. The appropriate status is therefore *apparently open*, rather than a claim that every possible source has been exhausted.

# 2. Proof attempt: a sharp rigorous upper bound

The following is a deduction from classical results. It is **not a proof of (1)**, and I do not claim the underlying correspondence or enumeration as new.

## The rooted-map count

Let $c_m$ be the number of rooted 3-connected planar maps with $m$ edges, called *rooted c-nets* in Tutte's terminology. Tutte proved, in equation (8.17) on p. 266, that

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
c_m\sim
\frac{2\,4^m}{243\sqrt{\pi}\,m^{5/2}}.
}
\tag{2}
$$

Unlike his squared-rectangle formula, this is a proved asymptotic.

## Proposition

For every $n\ge9$,

$$
\boxed{8a_n\le c_{n+1}.}
\tag{3}
$$

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
\frac{243\sqrt{\pi}\,n^{5/2}a_n}{4^n}
\le1.
}
\tag{4}
$$

### Proof

**Step 1: Associate a planar network with a perfect dissection.**

Given a squared rectangle of order $n$, construct its *normal polar net* by replacing maximal horizontal boundary segments of tiles with vertices and each tile with an edge. Edge currents represent tile side lengths. Add an edge joining the two poles, obtaining an embedded network with $n+1$ edges.

Brooks, Smith, Stone and Tutte, *“The Dissection of Rectangles into Squares,” Duke Mathematical Journal* **7** (1940), 312–340, establish this construction and its inverse using Kirchhoff's laws (§§1–4). Their theorem (5.23), p. 324, shows that a network derived from a **simple perfect** rectangle and its dual satisfy the relevant 3-connectivity condition. Thus the completed network is a c-net.

**Step 2: Keep track of the orientation.**

A rooted planar map has a directed root edge together with a specified side of that edge. For a squared rectangle, the construction can be performed using either its horizontal or vertical polar net. In either case, distinguishing the two poles and the two sides of the added edge specifies the corresponding orientation.

These choices give

$$
2\text{ polar-net directions}
\times
2\text{ pole orders}
\times
2\text{ sides}
=8
$$

framed versions of the dissection.

They are distinct for a perfect dissection of order greater than one. Indeed, any geometric symmetry would have to preserve each tile individually, because all tile sizes differ. A nontrivial rotation cannot fix the centers of multiple distinct tiles. A reflection likewise cannot preserve such a dissection: for an axial reflection all tile centers would lie on the reflection axis, forcing every horizontal or vertical cross-section to consist of one tile spanning the full width; for a diagonal reflection of a square container, the tile containing a corner off the diagonal would have to be the entire container. Both contradict a nontrivial perfect dissection.

**Step 3: The encoding is injective.**

The rooted network determines the oriented dissection up to common scaling by solving its electrical-network equations. In particular, two different framed perfect dissections cannot produce the same rooted c-net.

Therefore, each symmetry class counted by $a_n$ contributes eight distinct rooted c-nets with $n+1$ edges. Counting their images gives (3).

This argument uses the *normal* polar-net construction, so the uniqueness assertion concerns the actual dissection, not merely an arbitrary non-normal electrical drawing.

**Step 4: Apply Tutte's theorem.**

From (2),

$$
c_{n+1}
\sim
\frac{2\,4^{n+1}}
{243\sqrt{\pi}(n+1)^{5/2}}
\sim
\frac{8\,4^n}
{243\sqrt{\pi}n^{5/2}}.
$$

Combining this with (3),

$$
a_n\le\frac{c_{n+1}}8
\sim
\frac{4^n}{243\sqrt{\pi}n^{5/2}},
$$

which proves (4). $\square$

### Exactly what remains open

Define the nonnegative integer

$$
D_n=c_{n+1}-8a_n.
$$

Because of (2), Tutte's conjecture (1) is **equivalent** to the precise density statement

$$
\boxed{\displaystyle
\frac{D_n}{c_{n+1}}\longrightarrow0.
}
\tag{5}
$$

This equivalence is proved: it follows directly from (2) and the definition of $D_n$.

In combinatorial terms, (5) says that asymptotically almost every rooted c-net belongs to the eightfold image of simple perfect squared rectangles. Tutte anticipated this, but the necessary estimate on exceptional nets has not been established in the sources I found.

There is additional evidence for what the obstruction may be. OEIS A002881 counts *simple imperfect squared rectangles*, and the combined sequence A220167 counts all simple squared rectangles. The available data suggest that the imperfect proportion decreases with $n$, but proving that it tends to zero is itself a substantial missing step.

# 3. Computational check

I independently calculated Tutte's rooted-map counts from his exact recurrence (8.15)–(8.16), p. 265, and compared them with the published terms of A002839 and A002881 for every $9\le n\le26$.

Tutte's recurrence is

$$
S_k=27k^2+9k-2,\qquad R_0=0,
$$

$$
S_kR_{k-1}+2S_{k-1}R_k
=2\binom{2k}{k}
\quad(k\ge1),
$$

$$
c_{k+1}=2(-1)^{k+1}+R_k
\quad(k\ge3).
$$

All recurrence arithmetic below uses exact rational numbers; floating-point operations appear only in the displayed asymptotic ratios.

### Reproducible Python program

```python
from fractions import Fraction
from math import comb, sqrt, pi

# A002839, n = 9,...,26
a = [
    2, 6, 22, 67, 213, 744, 2609, 9016, 31426,
    110381, 390223, 1383905, 4931308, 17633773,
    63301427, 228130926, 825229110, 2994833854
]

# A002881, n = 9,...,26
b = [
    1, 0, 0, 9, 34, 104, 283, 953, 3029,
    9513, 30359, 98969, 323646, 1080659,
    3668432, 12608491, 43745771, 153812801
]

S = lambda k: 27*k*k + 9*k - 2
R = [Fraction(0)]

for k in range(1, 27):
    R.append(
        (2*comb(2*k, k) - S(k)*R[-1])
        / (2*S(k-1))
    )

def c(m):
    x = 2*(-1)**m + R[m-1]
    assert x.denominator == 1
    return int(x)

print("n    a(n)        c(n+1)       8a/c       a/T(n)      8(a+b)/c")

for n in range(9, 27):
    an, bn = a[n-9], b[n-9]
    cn = c(n+1)
    T = 4**n / (243*sqrt(pi)*n**2.5)
    if n in (9, 10, 12, 16, 20, 24, 26):
        print(
            f"{n:2d} {an:12d} {cn:14d} "
            f"{8*an/cn:10.6f} {an/T:12.6f} "
            f"{8*(an+bn)/cn:14.6f}"
        )

print("c(10) =", c(10), "; c(27) =", c(27))
print("8*a(n) <= c(n+1), all n=9..26:",
      all(8*x <= c(n+1) for n, x in enumerate(a, 9)))
print("8*(a(n)+b(n)) <= c(n+1), all n=9..26:",
      all(8*(x+b[n-9]) <= c(n+1)
          for n, x in enumerate(a, 9)))
print("a(26)/T(26) =",
      f"{a[-1]/(4**26/(243*sqrt(pi)*26**2.5)):.8f}")
```
